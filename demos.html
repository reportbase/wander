Wander demos

Wander is a world of solids you fly through, and a lab that compares what a reader can recover from arriving signals against known physics. These are its pages: the world, the lab, three small demos of one idea each, and three on the physics correspondence.

the world

Fly

The world itself: suns, planets, moons and belts, drawn only from the signals that reach you, with a readout of what can be recovered from them. Hold W to gather speed and let go to glide; tap beside a planet to go round it, scroll to come in close. Far off a body is a plain ball; close up its craters and mountains show.

a demo: arrays and the Cauchy

The floor's depth

An array index is ⌊x⌋, and the floor throws the fraction away. a demo: what H is

What is H?

H is no measured size: it is the value the reader picks to start the system, and it only decides where amounts land. Slide it from nothing to everything: a world of amounts crosses the quarter turn; at H of ∞ the reader's lay is the number line, at H of 0 the logarithm, and only an H among the amounts makes a corner. With a finite count the world is lost whole at both ends, three ways; two amounts are told apart best at their geometric mean.

a demo: parallax from the corner

Parallax from the corner

Two standpoints a baseline apart each read only directions: alone, a figure of rays with no distance. Together, with the baseline's length, the rays cross. Parallax has a corner of its own, the circle with the baseline as its diameter, where the baseline is seen at a right angle: on the bisector, there its depth is read best and its error is round. a demo in 3D: the wedge

The wedge

Not a sphere: the reader's own space. Three unit relations, h, v and v₂, each from 0 to 1, every party drawn at H; every [h, v, v₂] is a point of the unit cube and its directions are one octant, the wedge, made by one observation. A relation is never negative, so a sphere's other seven octants are only this wedge faced another way. Seen from home, the apex, through a flat screen the wedge is the triangle h + v + v₂ = 1: each direction lands at its shares, each pair's corner is a median, and the six chambers are its six small triangles. The hand points by altitude and azimuth; Lambert's map shows the wedge equal-area. A world is laid along the rays, in proportion to the corner and in levels past it, and H, the zoom, slides each thing along its own ray, never across it. The situations are ways of holding the wedge, at once or in order: a camera's frame, a slit panoramic camera, a line camera tipped up, a single-pixel scanner. Two motions: the wedge is first placed anywhere on the sphere by one rotation, then the hand sweeps inside it; the readings are the same wherever it is placed, and only the sphere's own frame records where.

The physics correspondence

The labs take results known from the view from nowhere and show they also come out for a reader who holds only what arrives. These three pages explain one line of that, the inverse square: the same signal, caught in a smaller share, and the reader's grain deciding how the loss shows.

a demo: the inverse square

The same signal, a smaller share

A ball sends the same light near or far; you catch less of it as it spreads. Near, it covers many of your grains, each as bright as ever: distance costs grains. Far, it is under one grain, and that grain dims: distance costs light. The switch, at one grain across, is your resolution limit; the law has no scale. All the standpoints together catch it all, at every distance.

sketch: the whole shot, ahead of time

Trick shots

Full screen. Set an angle and a power and see the whole shot before you take it: every ball it reaches, every contact, every pot, to rest, as far as a player could see: three contacts deep by default (each contact costs about as many levels of aim as the run before it spanned). Two angles, as in pool: where the cue's tip meets the white (the round inset, corners too: draw, follow and side spin, or a miscue past the chalk's grip), and where the white meets the first ball. Raise the cue for massé and jump shots; balls grip each other a little (throw), more when they meet slowly, as measured on real balls. Drag any ball to set up a trick shot; shoot, undo, try another angle, or pull the strip at the side down and let go. You hear each contact. Played from inside: each ball touches another exactly at h + v = 1. Or play the fifteen challenges, each one mechanic, each with its horizon fixed: the first in sight, then only the white to the ghost ball, then the pot past what you can see.

Marbles

A bowl heaped with five hundred glass marbles, poured as you watch. Tap where to throw a steelie and how hard; the dashed arc is the throw as far as it can be seen ahead, to its first contact. Past that the heap answers all at once: every marble at rest is held by its neighbours, each pair touching while its readings sit past the line h + v = 1. Ten throws, as many knocked out of the bowl as you can. Walk round the bowl, and turn on the chains to see the weight running through the heap. You hear every click.

The corner in flight

Why the ground turns into sky so awkwardly in flight sims. Level flight over a wireframe grid: the corner, the ground as far ahead as you are high, stays on one row of the screen at any height. Before it the ground reads evenly; past it each doubling of distance gets half the room. Level of detail by metres, the engine's way, slides as you climb; by v/h, levels split at the corner, 2v, 4v …, plain doublings, nothing on the screen moves. A few posts are recovered from the sweep alone: readings a second of travel apart cross at each post's place, shown on a plan, until the screen's step runs out.
