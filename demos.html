Wander demos

Wander is a world of solids you fly through, and a lab that compares what a reader can recover from arriving signals against known physics. These are its pages: the world, the lab, three small demos of one idea each, and three on the physics correspondence.

the world

Fly

The world itself: suns, planets, moons and belts, drawn only from the signals that reach you, with a readout of what can be recovered from them. Hold W to gather speed and let go to glide; tap beside a planet to go round it, scroll to come in close. Far off a body is a plain ball; close up its craters and mountains show.

Try: flywithout the lab button

the lab

The lab

Every experiment, one card each. Each lab writes down its prediction and what would kill it before it runs, and keeps the result, killed or not. Run one from its card, or all of them at once and read the full report.

Try: the cardsrun every labrun BAL

a demo: the sweep

The sweep device

Pairs of amounts, each divided by its own second amount, laid on the unit sweep g = (2/π)·atan(v/h): from 0 at home through ½ at the corner toward 1 at the horizon. A cursor reads them one at a time (serial) or all at once (parallel), with the quarter circle, divided by the whole, beside it.

Try: planetsa shapea spreadthe number lineparallel

a demo: the reader's grain

The H dial

A reader with no model, only its own pixels H wide, looks at two points one apart. Below one pixel across it sees "two" only by chance; from one pixel across on, every time. Turn the dial, look, slide H, or approach from afar. The cheapest sure reading is at one pixel across, and costs the same at s and 1/s.

Try: below one pixel acrossat one pixel acrosspast it

a demo: three fill levels

The sphere

Three fill levels (v, h, f), each 0 to 1, and a scale. Divide out the scale and an address on the sphere's first octant is left; the scale is lost for good. The octant splits into six chambers that meet at the corner v = h = f; a reader holding H can be near in one direction and far in another. Beside it, a body whose relief (its depth) shows only once it covers enough of the reader's pixels.

Try: the cornerfar in botha held wholefar offclose up

a demo: the second degree of freedom

The facing

One sweep from A to B. The straight line holds only where you are; the facing turns from toward B, through square to the line, to toward A. The corner is where it is square, equally between them: 45° of the sweep, 90° of the facing. Beside it, the quarter arc standing off the line, most at the corner.

Try: at Athe cornerat B

a demo: the sweep as a walk

The walk

I am the horizontal, you are the vertical, and the sweep is my walk to you. The sweep's angle is the angle at which I look up at you: 0° on my horizon, 45° at the corner, where the distance equals your height, 90° at your foot. Far off, each level nearer costs twice the steps of the one after it, and the steps bunch toward the horizon, which is never reached; near, past the corner, the walk is in proportion.

Try: walk from farthe cornernear

a demo: the levels

The levels

The circle in the middle holds the near field, up to the corner, in proportion. Outside it each ring is one doubling, drawn half as wide as the one before, crowding toward a horizon they never reach; a ring narrower than one grain can no longer be seen, so about log₂(radius ÷ grain) of them show. Send a thing out and it crosses one ring a second. A perturbed circle wraps it and crosses the levels, and a second circle is your level of detail: inside it you hold the shape's bumps, past it only its broad form. Divide out the size and the shape's middle lands on the corner: the corner is the baseline, and what stands off it, in levels, is the depth. Pick a sun, planet, moon or asteroid from the world, or load a .tvf3d file, and its outline is the shape.

The physics correspondence

The labs take results known from the view from nowhere and show they also come out for a reader who holds only what arrives. These three pages explain one line of that, the inverse square: the same signal, caught in a smaller share, and the reader's grain deciding how the loss shows.

a demo: the inverse square

The same signal, a smaller share

A ball sends the same light near or far; you catch less of it as it spreads. Near, it covers many of your grains, each as bright as ever: distance costs grains. Far, it is under one grain, and that grain dims: distance costs light. The switch, at one grain across, is your resolution limit; the law has no scale. All the standpoints together catch it all, at every distance.

Try: nearone grain acrossfarvery far

a demo: the distance ladder

The ladder

Three ways to find how far a body is, each one length read against another: your own travel (parallax), its width, and its light. Parallax and width each give out at the resolution limit, where what they read falls under one address; light goes on past both, as the inverse square goes on as dimming, until too few photons arrive.

Try: parallax still readswidth, not parallaxlight onlywith photons

a demo: Olbers' dark sky

The dark sky

If bodies filled an endless world that had always been, the night sky would blaze: each farther shell is fainter by the square of its distance and more numerous by the same, so every shell gives the same light. It is dark because only what has arrived is seen. Slide the reach and the lit share follows 1 − e−L/λ; read it backwards and the dark gives the age.

Try: a young skyone free pathalways been

Gameplay sketches

Small pages, each trying one mechanic that comes out of the physics, so that each can be explored and changed on its own before anything goes into the flying page.

sketch: drawing far bodies

Far lights

Fly through 3,000 bodies. Under a pixel, a body can dim by the inverse square, vanish, or stay a full-bright pixel (the usual shortcut). Switch between them and compare the skies.

Try: dimvanishnever under a pixel

sketch: events on approach

Resolving

Approach a planet: a dimming point, then one pixel across (resolved), then its shape, its terrain, its surface. Each first crossing is logged, the kind of moment a game can reward.

Try: from far offclose

sketch: distances earned

The navigator's ladder

Ten bodies of two kinds at distances you are not told. Parallax for the near; log one and its kind's size and light open width and light for the rest. Score the bodies you place within 10%, in as few turns as you can.

Try: play

sketch: light that takes time

The sky fills

A world that starts when you do: stars appear as their light arrives, nearest first, and the count of stars seen reads the game time back. "Instant light" shows the usual game for comparison.

Try: playmidwayinstant light
