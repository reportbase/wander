Wander demos

Wander is a world of solids you fly through, and a lab that compares what can be recovered from arriving signals against known physics. These are its pages: the world, the lab, the demos of the geometry (one standpoint, a read's directions and their heat given by where it stands, and what arrives painting them), three on the physics correspondence, and gameplay sketches. The essays that explain the geometry, in order, are on their own page, and the scrolling explainers on theirs. The known physics the demos touch, sorted by the two tests, is in Reads and Writes in Physics; a card says where (in physics).

the world

Fly

The world itself: suns, planets, moons and belts, drawn only from the signals that reach you, with a readout of what can be recovered from them. Hold W to gather speed and let go to glide; tap beside a planet to go round it, scroll to come in close. Far off a body is a plain ball; close up its craters and mountains show.

a demo: what fairness fixes

One sweep, three paths

From all of h to all of v along a circle, a straight line or a square's two edges, each taken uniformly along its own length. The reading v/h is the direction alone, the same on every path; the share of the path up to a direction differs. All three agree at home, the corner and the horizon and part in between; none has a mean. Only the circle is even in the turn, and gives 2/π: the turn and its shadow, π/2 and 2/π at the horizon. Read and walk: on every path the mean gain per step times the length walked is 1 (2/π × π/2, 1/√2 × √2, ½ × 2); only the split is the path's.

a demo: reading and writing

The pinhole

A read never divides: the ray through the pinhole is the reading, a direction, which is already a ratio. Make the thing bigger and farther together and nothing moves; send its breadth to infinity and it arrives as the horizon, off the flat screen and bounded on the turn. The division V*/d is the blueprint's account. A ruler that divides two amounts touches breadths: that is writing. A uniform sky on the flat screen is the Cauchy map, cos³α off the axis, three of the four cosines of a photograph's dark corners (essay 17).

In physics: The cos³ law of illumination: an even source on a flat receiver gives P/2 times the two-dimensional Cauchy of scale d, the inverse square its height on the axis, the receiver catching exactly half; a lens adds the fourth cosine. (Reads and Writes in Physics, §6.1)

The room

A wireframe room seen from its floor corner, one wedge. The inscribed sweep is the directions from the corner from one wall to the other, given whole: where things are, by direction only. Walking the room

The observer walks the floor, and wherever it stands the geometry gives both sweeps: the inscribed, the directions once round, given, no amount; the central, from each thing's centre toward the observer, with a radius along it in a unit nothing reads (a range, a writing), so walking round a thing collects its shape. From two standpoints the bearings place every thing in the walk's first leg; counting that leg in strides writes the room in metres. A readout keeps what is read, held and written. Through the window a tree: moved away, it can no longer be walked round, so it loses its shape and is placed by parallax alone; further still, only a direction.

a demo: the two sweeps

One turn, one read

One direction from home meets one point of the circle drawn on the chord. As a direction from home it is the direction with its heat (the inscribed sweep); seen from the centre, at twice the angle, it is where the paint lands (the central sweep); both end at the same point (Euclid III.20). Over the quarter the central sweep measures the line as π/2, the inscribed as 2/π, their product 1. In complex form the point is the direction squared, ½e^(2iθ): +1 at zero, i at the corner, e^(iπ) = −1, home, at the horizon; the Cayley disc shows the same read with zero at −1 and the horizon at +1. The Cauchy's constant from the same turn: the area under 1/(1 + s²) fills to π/2 as the point reaches e^(iπ) = −1, the half-Cauchy's 2/π one over it. From any standpoint z the same total, weighted by a turn e^(its), is e^(izt): drag t and it spirals in from 1. Draw the other party at any V other than H and the two part. None of the facts needs π or e: the product is s × (1/s) = 1, the half turn is two corners, i × i = −1, and the heat is halved at the corner; π and e name the completed circle's totals.

The read's form of Euler's identity is on this page too: w(s) × w(1/s) = −1 at every slope, i × i = −1 at the corner; the blueprint's e^(iπ) = −1 is the member that needs the horizon.

In physics: e^(izt) is a spectral line: a phase turning and fading has the Lorentzian as its spectrum, its width and the lifetime one height read two ways; for sodium's D2 line, measured each way independently, Γτ = 1.0010 ± 0.0025. (Reads and Writes in Physics, §4.3)

a demo: the Cauchy map

The Cauchy map

Stand a distance d from a wall and turn evenly: equal turns of the look cross the wall crowded at its foot, the heat of the directions, the Cauchy, there before any light arrives. Release random walkers from the same point and they land on the wall by the same law, harmonic measure, in the plane and on a flat wall in space; the blueprint's tiles are flat. Stepping back keeps every pattern (e^(−d|k|), heights adding), a Gaussian too but falling by k², a box blur losing some outright. And the second map, in distance, laid by the thumb: hottest at one thumb. Last, where no turn can be found: 35 measured wind-dispersed seed kernels against the turn a falling seed would make, their tails fatter, the test that could fail and did.

The heat is rational in the slope and the corner halves it; π enters only when the total is closed.

In physics: the seeds are the sort's one failure kept in the open: a fat tail from a mixture over wind speeds, not a turn, so a fat tail alone is no evidence of one; and the line's Lorentzian is this map in frequency (Reads and Writes in Physics, §11, §4.3)

a demo: one law in the plane, three in space

Why the plane is special

In the plane the read's Cauchy is four things at once: the uniform turn carried by a Möbius map, where a walker released at the standpoint lands, the law whose mean is the standpoint, and the two sweeps' rates combined. Drag the standpoint and they stay one curve. On the sphere the same read has three laws: harmonic measure (its mean still the standpoint), the boosted sky (its mean coth η − η/sinh² η) and the landing, and no mix of them agrees. Two quarter turns of a book, in either order, end 120° apart. What survives in space, and what was the plane's alone. On a flat wall, though, the look and the walker agree in every dimension: harmonic measure is the Cauchy map (essay 17).

In physics: The boosted sky is a moving observer's: two boosts along different axes, joined, leave the Thomas–Wigner rotation, fixed by three stars' directions before and after and absent from any single boost. (Reads and Writes in Physics, §5.3)

Circles and standpoints

Epicycles draw a closed shape with circles on circles, each turning a whole number of times a lap: one standpoint at the centre, turning evenly. The Cauchy draws with the same circles two ways. From a height it shrinks circle k by e−γ|k| and moves none, so the fine circles fade first. From standpoints stepped toward the detail (Takenaka–Malmquist terms, chosen greedily), a dent the best 85 circles draw to 10⁻³ takes about a dozen terms, and the standpoints sit at the dent. A broad shape with nothing local goes the other way: the even circles win. Seen from a height, every circle dims and none moves: stepping back is the Poisson blur, e^(−γ|k|) (essay 17).

a demo: ratios as places

A read is a slide rule

Every direction is already a ratio, v/h. Laid on the levels, the ratio between two things is the gap between their places, so N places hold every one of the N(N − 1)/2 ratios: put the slide's 1 on any thing and read every ratio to it at once. A tower of links laid end to end telescopes to its two ends, a loop closes to nothing, and a misread link leaves a remainder the loop shows but cannot place. Directions work the same way on the turn. A read places things; it does no ratio arithmetic.

a demo: direction and size apart

Euler's plane

One grid of points and lines, the eye at its corner, drawn three ways. As the blueprint has it, straight lines. On Euler's plane, ln(z/H) = ln(d/H) + iθ: direction runs up, distance in thumbs runs across, so turning the world slides everything up or down and scaling it (or moving the thumb) slides it sideways; the shells at one, two, four, eight thumbs are evenly spaced lines and the corner is a mirror. On the thumb's lay the distance is squeezed by (2/π)·atan(d/H), Mercator's function, so the far field bunches on a horizon at the edge. Side by side, or one sliding into the next; and n reads composed, (1 + iθ/n)ⁿ, closing on e^(iθ). Behind all three is one sphere, the blueprint's: one thumb its equator, the eye one pole and the horizon the other; the three pictures are its maps, and moving the thumb is a boost along its axis. On Euler's plane every straight line is the same curve moved and a spiral is straight. In space the split survives and the turns stop being slides: two quarter turns in the other order land 120° apart, and a loop of turns comes home turned. The physics correspondence

The labs take results known on the blueprint and show they also come out from a standpoint that holds only what arrives. These three pages explain one line of that, the inverse square: the same signal, caught in a smaller share, and the observer's grain deciding how the loss shows. The known physics behind them, and much besides, is sorted in Reads and Writes in Physics.

a demo: the inverse square

The same signal, a smaller share

A ball sends the same light near or far; you catch less of it as it spreads. Near, it covers many of your grains, each as bright as ever: distance costs grains. Far, it is under one grain, and that grain dims: distance costs light. The switch, at one grain across, is your resolution limit; the law has no scale. All the standpoints together catch it all, at every distance.

In physics: The paint per direction is the same at every distance; the inverse square lives in how many directions the source covers. A disc's light turns from near to far exactly at s = 1, but only counted as a share of directions. (Reads and Writes in Physics, §6.1, §6.2)
