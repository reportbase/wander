The face from home

Standing at home, every ray of your sweep reports one thing: did it meet the moon. That is the inscribed sweep, directions only. The moon's face, its hills and hollows, is an amount along each direction: how far out the surface is. Can home earn it? Two ways, shown here on a slice through the moon's middle as it crosses the sky.

Ranges: looking plus a known signal speed

Each ray also brings back how far it went before it hit, as radar or lidar does. Every hit is then a point on the moon, in a real unit. The face comes out, and so do the moon's distance and speed: the signal's speed is an amount carried in, so ranging writes.

Silhouettes: looking alone

Only the patch's two edges each tick. As the line of sight turns, the edges wrap the moon from many sides; keep what stayed between them every time (the visual hull). It hugs the moon where an edge grazed it and bridges every hollow no edge touched. Up to size: scale the world and nothing moves.

From home: the quarter turn and the moon's path. The rays that meet the moon this tick fan out from home; gold, its two edges.

At the moon: every other ray that meets it. With ranges each ends at its first surface point (teal); the edges graze it and go on.

In the moon's own frame, every tick so far: ranged points; the edge rays and the hull they leave; the true outline dashed; all drawn by the referee where it really lies, with + where each reader puts the centre when it fits it.

The face as a painting: radius over its geometric mean, round the moon. White the truth, shaded where it has been seen from home; teal from ranges, violet from the hull.

The test is CFH (run 1, in three dimensions on the whole moon: ranges with the centre given give the face exactly, with it fitted to 4% rms, and write distance and speed to 0.25%; the hull contains the moon, is tight on the rim to about one address and keeps none of the face elsewhere; nothing in it moves when the world is scaled).
