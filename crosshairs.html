How H makes the scene

Three thumbs, held square to each other at arm's length: one vertical, one horizontal, one in depth. The look runs down between them, so each thumb is 55° off the line of sight, and the crosshair is where all three thumbs' sweeps meet: the corner. H is the thumb. Drag to look (or the arrow keys), W A S D or the wheel to walk (▲ ▼ on a phone), the wheel or a pinch zooms, Space (or the menu) holds H. The wireframes, the ground and the look are plain perspective and use no H at all; everything below is what H adds.

Where H comes from

  1. Pointing sets it. The line of sight through the crosshair is followed into the field, and the first thing whose box it passes through is the thing pointed at. Its reach along each thumb is H: one thumb in depth, one vertical, one horizontal, all three meeting there. (Its depth along the look is H·√3, the cube's diagonal.)
  2. The ground counts, the sky keeps it. Between things, below the horizon, the crosshair meets the ground, and that point is the corner. Above the horizon nothing is met, and the thumbs stay where they last were.
  3. Holding keeps it on one thing. With H held you look round and walk while H stays on the thing that was pointed at.
  4. It glides. A new H is reached on its logarithm in about a fifth of a second.

The one-thumb cube

  1. The near field is a cube. The three thumbs, each one thumb long, are the edges of a cube with the eye at one corner and the crosshair at the other (menu: the one-thumb cube). Everything nearer than one thumb along all three is the near field; the thing pointed at is the far corner.
  2. It looks the same at every H. Seen from its own corner a cube of any size looks the same, so the gold lines round the crosshair never change as H changes: H changes what lies inside it, never how it looks.
  3. The Cauchy is centred on the crosshair. Directions laid evenly by turning thin out from the look as cos³ of the angle off it, the two-dimensional Cauchy (menu: the Cauchy's shading). It is a heat map, hottest at the crosshair and cooling toward the horizon, nothing counted in it; it is laid by the look, not on the ground. The heat is given; the paint is met.

What every thing gets from H

  1. Three readings. Its reach along each thumb, in thumbs. All three are one at the crosshair.
  2. Its field. All three below one: the near field, read in proportion. The largest at one: the corner. The largest past one: the far field, read as a ratio, one in two, one in four, one in eight. One below zero: behind a thumb, in another wedge, not this look's (faint grey).
  3. Its colour and words (menu: words on things). Near, a fraction of the thumb; far, a ratio, dimmer for each halving. Counted in thumbs (two, four, eight) the far field is an extent; a read has the ratio.
  4. Its place in the wedge (menu: the wedge from home). Its three readings as shares of their sum lay it on the triangle seen down the look: the crosshair at the centre, each thumb a corner, each pair of thumbs' corner a median through the centre. The line (menu) lays the largest reading as the read lays distance: in proportion to the corner, then each halving of the ratio the same room.

What H shares out

  1. K, the fineness. Read continuously; counted, it is K places at the heat's quantiles, and half of them are always inside one thumb, whatever H is; on the line they are the ticks, the same marks at every H, and the things slide under them as you point.
  2. The counts of near, far and another wedge (menu: the readout) are only how H falls among the things.
  3. The walking pace. A third of the way to what is pointed at each second, faster while held, and a wheel notch a tenth: a convenience of this page, not part of the reading.

What H does not change

  1. Scaling the world. Scale every length by 10, the eye's place included, and H in units grows by 10, but every reading, colour, word and place in the wedge is as it was. H in units is a write.
  2. The field itself. No thing moves when H moves; only the near and far fields do, because they are where the three thumbs meet.

Fidelity, finest at the crosshair

  1. Each thing's grain. The fineness K is spread evenly by turning, so at a reading q one of the K counted places is a relative cell of (π/K)·cosh(ln q): least at one, the corner. A thing's grain is the worst of its three readings. The thing at the crosshair, one in all three, is held finest; a thing off the look, or nearer or farther than the corner, coarser, as cosh of how far its ratio is from one. At K = 32 about 10% at the corner, 12% at one in 2, 40% at one in 8, over 300% at one in 64.
  2. Laid by turns (menu: view). The grain is a precision, how finely the fineness K holds a thing, not its size: a fixed fineness squeezes more of the world into each of its places toward home, the direction's grain times the distance's (in the three thumbs' levels the two are at right angles). Seen, a thing spans the angle it spans, which already falls by half each time its distance doubles. The view is laid by equal turns off the look rather than on a flat screen: the two agree near the crosshair and part only far out, the flat screen stretching by tan α/α, 1.10 at 30°, 1.27 at 45°.
  3. The one-thumb circle. A ball one thumb across round the crosshair's point touches the wedge's three faces, at the cube's corners one thumb back along each thumb. From the eye its outline is a circle 35.26° round the crosshair, the largest inside the wedge, holding 73% of its directions. It never changes size: H follows what is pointed at. The corner is its centre; home is on it, at the three points where it touches (gold dots), where one reading is zero. So the circle runs from the corner out to home, not from undistorted to compressed. Past those points lies another wedge; between them the wedge runs on in three points out to the thumbs, 54.74° off.
  4. The three corners. Each pair of thumbs has its own corner, where the two read alike: a line on the screen through the crosshair (drawn faint gold). Along it lies a whole quarter turn of the third thumb against the pair: home at the circle's dot (35.26° off the look), the corner at the crosshair, the horizon at the third thumb's own direction (the ring, 54.74° off on the other side); 35.26° + 54.74° = 90°. Three quarter turns, crossed, each with its corner on the crosshair: that is why it is the corner of all three.
  5. Nothing hidden or blurred. Every thing is drawn whole and at full strength, near, far or in another wedge (grey); its colour says which field it is in, never how faintly. The view is laid by turns (menu: view), or on a flat screen.
  6. Zoom is not H. Zooming (the wheel, a pinch, + and −, F for what is pointed at) narrows the view, so a stone far off or an airplane high up can be seen and pointed at; it changes how large things look, never a reading. H still comes from what the crosshairs pass through: zoom onto an airplane and H is its distance, onto a stone at your feet and H is a step. Held on an airplane, the look follows it as it flies.
  7. The views (menu: view). By the heat in distance, the default: across, the direction; up, the share of the distance's heat, (2/π)·atan, your feet at the bottom, the thumb at the crosshair, the horizon at the top of the ground, never reached. Heights are laid in the same shares as depths, so past the thumb everything is flattened more and more: one in 2, one in 4, one in 8, each band thinner, the whole far world bunched under the horizon. The world has no count: things are made from their place as you look, at every size, and only those coarse enough to be held at your fineness K are made at all. The Cauchy tunnel, the default: what the crossed thumbs give where you look. Each direction's reading off the line of sight, in thumbs, is laid by its heat: every ring of the screen holds as much of the heat as its room, so the Cauchy's thinning becomes the rings bunching. The rings are the tunnel's walls: the corner one thumb off, then one in 2, one in 4, one in 8, each closer to the last, toward the horizon's rim, which no reading reaches. Nothing is behind it. A read is the quarter turn, so by default the tunnel is drawn in the wedge only, every pair of thumbs from its home (0) to its horizon (∞), out to 35.26° at a home and 54.74° at a thumb; the full round about the look is the head turned all the way round (menu: tunnel turned all the way round). Equal area: the read's directions, every one alike by area, and this is the screen that gives each the same room (Lambert's equal-area projection about the crosshair, radius 2·sin(α/2)). True at the crosshair, squeezed along the radius by cos(α/2) and widened across by as much, so the distortion is nothing at first and grows: under 1% at 15°, 3% at 30°, 8% at 45°. The shading, the directions per bit of screen, is flat on it. By turns: equal angles (radius α). Silver ball: an approximation, the grain's √6 taken as exact. And the flat screen, where the shading is the cos³ falloff.
  8. The whole sky (menu, the ◯ button, or O). The equal-area view carried all the way round: every direction at once, behind you included, still each with the same room. The sphere becomes a disc, the look at its centre, the directions square to it on the dashed circle (radius √2) and straight behind on the rim (radius 2). A flat screen can never pass 90°; games that show everything at once use this kind of map. Zoom in and it becomes the usual view.
