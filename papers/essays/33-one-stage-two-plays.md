# One stage, two plays: a look and a pull

*The thirty-third of a series of essays on what can be known from where one stands. Draft, 24 November 2026; brought up
to FCL's twelfth run on 25 November, and to ORB's orbit (§13) the same day; on 25 November too, bodies, motion and light's delay (§14, GDI, MVM, LDL, ACC and COS,
`plans/gdi-plan.md`, `plans/mvm-plan.md`, `plans/ldl-plan.md`, `plans/acc-plan.md`, `plans/cos-plan.md`). It writes up these tests and three earlier ones: GRV (`plans/grv-plan.md`, `runs/grv/`), the twelve runs
of FCL (`plans/fcl-plan.md`, `runs/fcl/`) and ORB runs 2–8 (`plans/orb-plan.md`, `runs/orb/`). Every run but one had its predictions committed before it ran (the exception,
on measured data, is said in §11), and the kills are gathered in §15. Every number below is from those runs. It began as
exploration and ends on one ruling (§16): a place with no facing is not a read. The pull is never called a
read, and nothing here is new physics. The mathematics is classical throughout and is named where it is used; the sorting is the series' own,
and is marked as a reading.*

A plumb line is a weight on a string. Let it hang and it shows which way is down. Builders have used one for as long as
there have been walls.

What it shows is stranger than it looks. The weight is pulled by the ground under it, and also by the ground a mile off,
and by the far side of the Earth, and by the hill to the north. Every piece of matter in every direction pulls on it at
once, and the string settles along the one line that is left when all of those pulls are added. In 1774 Nevil Maskelyne
hung a plumb line on each side of a Scottish mountain and found the two lines leaning very slightly toward it.

An eye at the same place does something different. It faces one way. It takes in what lies in front, keeps the
directions apart, and of each direction it has only the first thing on that line.

The series has been about the second of these: a look from one standpoint facing one way, the single hemispherical
vantage (SHV). This essay puts the first beside it. The question came from Tom on 24 November: "if you don't have a
facing, you get all facings at once". What does a place with no facing take in, and how does it compare with a look?

The short answer, from thirteen runs: the two stand on one stage. The weight a look lays over directions, the Cauchy, is
the same weight a mass lays over a plane. A pull differs from a look in two ways only. It carries an amount, where a
look carries none. And it joins a facing to its opposite by subtracting, where a look's two halves are joined by
adding. Tom's words for the frame, the same day: "cauchy is the stage, not the play itself."

The order: one stage (§1), a place with no facing (§2), the corners (§3), a facing given back (§4), the sheet (§5), two
masses (§6), the pair in motion (§7), light past a mass (§8), what a pull's direction can find (§9), adding and
subtracting from the look's side (§10), a tunnel under a hill (§11), the front and the depth (§12), an orbit (§13), bodies,
motion and delay (§14), what was killed (§15), what this does not say (§16), what it changes (§17).

## 1. One stage

Hold out a thumb at arm's length and look past it at a wall. The series calls the thumb's length H. A direction a
little off the straight-ahead line meets the wall a little way from the point straight ahead; call that distance r, in
thumbs. If the directions in front are spread evenly, the places where they meet the wall are not. They crowd near the
foot and thin out far from it, as 1/(1 + r²)^(3/2). Half of them land within √3 thumbs of the foot. That law is the
Cauchy in two dimensions, and the series calls it the look's heat on a flat screen.

Now take away the eye and put a small mass where it stood, a height d above a plane. Ask how much of the mass's pull
crosses each part of the plane. The answer is the same law: 1/(1 + r²)^(3/2), with r counted in heights. Half of the
pull that crosses the plane crosses it within √3 heights of the foot. The total is 2πGM whatever the height.

This is not a likeness. It is one formula, known in potential theory as the Poisson kernel of the half-space. GRV
checked it to fifty digits. Turned round, it says the pull at a point above a flat sheet of matter is the sheet's density
averaged with that same weight; a ripple in the sheet of wavelength 2π/k fades with height as e^(−kh), the same fading
the series found when a standpoint steps back from a wall.

So the stage is shared. What is laid on it differs: for a look, paint, which has no amount; for a pull, mass, which
does.

## 2. A place with no facing

Give a place no facing and let it take in every direction at once, summed. FCL's first run asked what it then holds.

It holds three numbers, however much there is around it: the direction and the size of the one pull that is left. A
scene of one mass and a scene of a thousand leave the same kind of thing. Inside a uniform shell it holds nothing at
all, though a look from the same place meets the shell in every direction.

It cannot part amount from distance. A mass m at distance d and a mass 4m at 2d, on one line, pull alike, to forty
digits. The direction of the pull does not change when the whole world is scaled; its size does. So the direction is
unmoved by scaling, as a reading is, and the size is an amount.

A far body arrives in order: first its whole mass at its centre of mass, then finer terms, each falling faster with
distance. The run found the first two falling as the distance to the power −2 and −3 (measured slopes −1.9994 and
−2.992).

And placing things takes more places, as it does for looks. Two takes that share a frame place one mass exactly and
give its amount as well. A scene of n masses needs the takes from at least 4n/3 places. That is parallax again, with
one difference: a look's parallax gives where, and a pull's gives where and how much.

## 3. The corners

A look has corners: places where a share is exactly a half or a third, set by the stage and not by what is looked at.
Does a pull have them?

On the plane, yes, and they are the look's own. Half of a mass's pull crosses within one height of the line through the
foot, counted across one thumb, and the strips at x and 1/x (in heights) add to the whole. A third crosses inside the
square one height each way. Half crosses within √3 heights round the foot. None of these carries the mass, and none
moves when the world is scaled.

Along the line through the body, no. Inside a uniform ball the pull grows in proportion to the distance from the
centre; outside it falls as the inverse square; it is greatest exactly at the surface. So the body's own border splits
near from far. But the places of equal pull are x and 1/√x, not x and 1/x. Along the line the pull is an amount, and
the ball's radius stands where the thumb stands for a look. The radius is the body's own. The thumb is chosen.

## 4. A facing given back

The sixth run asked Tom's question directly. Give the place a facing after all, and let it keep only what arrives from
in front, and only the part of it along the facing. Call that a faced take. It is what would cross a small window
square to the facing. The definition was made for the run; nothing here says that any thing in the world takes a pull
this way.

Three things came out, all exact.

A facing and its opposite subtract. The pull along any line is the front take less the back take. For a look, the two
hemispheres are joined by adding: together they make the whole sky. For a pull they are joined by subtracting: whatever
is alike in front and behind goes.

"No facing" is all facings at once, each keeping its direction. Average the faced takes over every facing, each one
carried along its own facing, and the result is one sixth of the faceless pull. So Tom's sentence holds in an exact
form. Average them without their directions and what is left is only an amount, a quarter of the sky's total. Six
facings, three square pairs, are already enough to rebuild the pull.

The shell's nothing is a balance. Inside a uniform shell each facing takes in a good deal, between 0.26 and 0.44 in
the shell's own units at the places tried, and a quarter more across the line to the centre than along it when close to
the wall. The pull is nothing because every facing equals its opposite. Newton's argument for the shell is this one:
opposite cones.

And with a facing the look's exchange comes back whole. Take a sky that is alike in every direction. The share of its
faced take that lands within r thumbs of the foot is r²/(1 + r²). Half lands within one thumb, and the shares at r and
1/r add to the whole, exactly. The look's own half on that screen is at √3 thumbs, and a single mass's pull, in §3, had
no such pairing round the foot at all. The extra power is the facing's own: a pull counts only its part along the
facing.

## 5. The sheet

That left a puzzle. GRV found the look's law on a flat sheet, half within √3 heights. The sixth run found another law
for an even sky, half within one height. A sheet is taken facing straight down. Why does it follow the look?

The seventh run found the reason, and it is a cancellation.

A flat sheet is not a sky alike in every direction. Seen from above it leans: at slope r off the straight-down line it
carries √(1 + r²) times as much, per unit of direction, as it does straight down. A slanted line crosses more of it.
At slope 1 that is 1.41 times; at slope 5, 5.1 times; without bound toward the horizon.

A pull taken with a facing counts only its part along the facing, which is 1/√(1 + r²) of the whole.

The two cancel exactly. What a flat sheet sends along the straight-down facing is the same from every direction. That
is the uniform turn, the thing the look's law comes from. Laid back on the sheet, it is the look's heat.

So the flat sheet is the one arrangement of matter whose pull, faced square to it, lays out exactly as a look lays out.
Thin the sheet by 1/√(1 + r²) and it becomes an even sky, with the sixth run's law, half within one height, and half
the total. It is also why a sheet's pull has no height in it: every direction gives the same, and the directions do
not change as the place rises.

Turn the facing along the sheet and there is no limit. The lean is no longer cancelled. From a disc of sheet ten
heights across the sideways take is 4.0; from a hundred, 8.6; from a thousand, 13.2. It grows by the same step, 2·ln 10
in the sheet's units, for each tenfold, and never settles. The opposite facing grows the same, and their difference,
the pull, is nothing. One facing of an endless even world has no total. Two opposite facings have a difference.

## 6. Two masses

Put two masses a distance apart. Name a place on the line between them by ρ, its distance from the first over its
distance from the second, and let μ be the first mass over the second.

The two pulls cancel at exactly one place, ρ = √μ. Off the line they cancel nowhere.

That place is a corner in the look's sense. The ratio of the two pulls at ρ is μ/ρ². The places ρ and μ/ρ carry
reciprocal ratios, and the one place that exchange leaves alone is √μ. For two looks the pair's corner is where their
readings multiply to 1 (essay 30). Here it is where the pulls' ratio is 1. It is the same exchange, with the corner
moved from 1 to √μ by what is laid on the stage.

Where it stands is a ratio of two masses and nothing else. It does not move when every length is scaled, or when both
masses are scaled together. It moves when one mass changes.

There are two centres. The meeting point sits toward the lighter mass, at √μ. The centre of mass sits toward the
heavier, at 1/μ. They agree only when the masses are equal, at the middle. The centre of mass is a mean, a sum over the
masses. The meeting point is the exchange's fixed point.

Off the line, the places where the two pulls are equal in size form a sphere round the lighter mass, the sphere of
Apollonius. At every point of it the net pull aims exactly at the meeting point.

## 7. The pair in motion

Set the pair turning about each other and look from the frame that turns with them. One meeting point becomes five
balance points, three on the line and two off it. These are Euler's and Lagrange's.

Two things change, and both bear on the corner.

Off the line the corner goes back to 1. The two balance points there stand at equal distances from both masses, and
that distance is the pair's own separation, whatever the masses are. At rest the meeting point stood at √μ. In motion
the masses' ratio drops out of the place altogether.

On the line the power changes. The balance point between the masses stands much farther from the lighter one than the
meeting point at rest. For a lighter mass a billionth of the total, it stands at 0.00069 of the separation, where at
rest it stood at 0.00003. At rest the distance goes as the square root of the lighter's share; in motion, as the cube
root of a third of it.

All five places are unmoved by scaling, as long as the rate of turning follows from the masses. Set the rate by hand
and they move.

## 8. Light past a mass

Everything so far has a mass playing on the stage. In one case a mass acts on the stage itself: it bends the
directions a look receives.

Take a point mass between the eye and a far sky, and the standard lens equation for weak bending. Lay directions on a
flat screen and measure in Einstein radii, the radius of the ring that a source exactly behind the mass is seen as.

Every source has two images, on opposite sides of the mass, and their positions multiply to −1. That is the look's
exchange, with a change of side, and the Einstein ring is its fixed circle. Half of all images lie inside the ring,
whatever the sky is: the run counted 20,000 inside and 20,000 outside for each of three different skies.

Along a line through the mass, the bending keeps the Cauchy family exactly. Images that follow a Cauchy come from
sources that follow a Cauchy. Sources that follow a Cauchy are seen as exactly two Cauchys, one for each image, swapped
by the ring's exchange, and their two standpoints add up to the source's. The sources' spread is least when the images
sit at the ring. Tom's phrase, "it merges and combines and remains a cauchy", holds for the bending here to thirty
digits.

Over the whole screen it does not. The images of a Cauchy sky are no Cauchy: their density is exactly zero on the
ring, where every Cauchy is positive. What breaks the family is the stretch round the ring, which a line does not
have.

So the ring is a corner too. But its place is set by the mass, an amount. The look's corner is chosen with the thumb.

## 9. What a pull's direction can find

A plumb line gives a direction and no strength. Run 9 asked how much of two masses at rest can be found with that
alone.

More than one might guess. The place where the two pulls cancel can be found from the line's direction, with no
strength measured. Directions taken at four places a counted step apart give where both masses are, and the ratio of
one mass to the other, all in steps. If the ratio of the pull's strength between places is taken as well, three places
are enough.

What never comes out is a mass. Ratios of directions give no mass. Ratios of strengths give no mass. They give the
ratio of the two masses and nothing past it. One strength measured in a unit gives G times the mass, and still not the
mass by itself.

The series has met this before. A read gives directions and their ratios and never V, the true size of the mountain;
V comes only with an amount carried in. A mass stands to pulls as V stands to a read. The run was in ordinary
arithmetic, not the exact kind, and nothing was killed.

## 10. Adding and subtracting, from the look's side

Section 4 found that a pull's two halves subtract. Run 10 asked the same question from the other side: does a look
have anything that subtracts?

It has one thing. Take the heat a look meets below its corner and take away the heat it meets above. On the bare
stage the two are equal and the difference is zero. With paint on the stage they differ, and to first order the
difference is how far the paint has moved the median off the corner.

A pull is the same kind of difference, taken with amounts: the paint's first moment over two looks back to back. The
rule that covers both is short. Halves subtract for a moment that has a direction, and add for one that has none.

They are still different moments. In the run's scenes a paint could move one and leave the other, and the two lay
8.25° apart in one of them. Replace every mass m by m³, which keeps the order of the masses, and the pull's direction
moved by more than a degree in twenty scenes of twenty. The look's balance did not move in any. A look's centre is a
median, which only order sets. A pull's is a sum, which amounts set. That is Claude's reading and is not ruled: it puts
gravity on the paint's side of the series' split between heat and paint.

## 11. A tunnel under a hill

Section 5 says a ripple in a sheet's pull fades with height as the Cauchy says. Turned round, that is a rule
geophysicists have long used: a buried line of missing mass shows at the surface as a dip in gravity shaped like the
Cauchy, and the dip's half-width at half its depth is how far down the line lies. No density is needed. The shape
gives the depth.

Run 11 tried it on measurements. No open table of such a survey was found, so the values were read off a published
figure: two gravity profiles across the Březno railway tunnel, from a preprint by Blecha and Mašín. The predictions
were written after the values had been seen, which the lab's rules do not allow as a test, and the plan says so.

Fitted with the Cauchy's curve, the two profiles put the tunnel 18.9 ± 2.3 m and 13.6 ± 1.8 m down, against true
mid-heights of 16.25 and 17.25 m. Their mean is 15.6 ± 1.4 m against 16.75 m, about 12% off. Multiply every measured
value by 7 and the depth read does not move, as it should not: the depth is in the shape. The data could not tell a
buried line from a buried ball; both curves fitted. One prediction was killed (§15). A second site, Medford Cave, read
7 ft against 24 ft; a cave is not a clean line, and the miss stands as it came.

So the rule holds once, to about an eighth, on one clean site. It is the classical rule and nothing more. A second
clean site is still wanted.

## 12. The front and the depth

On 25 November Tom asked what the heat is, for gravity: "What if cauchy heat for gravity is just the depth".

The stage's heat is the same for a look and a pull. What differs is what each takes along a direction. A look takes
the front: the first thing met on that line, and nothing behind it. That is why a read holds no distance. A pull takes
the depth: everything along the line, front to back, nothing hidden behind anything.

Split by direction, the pull at a place is a fixed weight for each direction times the matter met along it. The inverse
square cancels against the way volume grows with distance, so distance drops out and each direction gives its column:
the density times how deep the matter goes along that line. For an even density that is the depth itself. More depth
along a direction, more pull from it.

Three old results read plainly this way. Inside a shell, opposite directions have equal depth and cancel, which is
Newton's own argument. On an even ball the depth along a direction is the chord through it, and the chords add to the
pull at the surface. Above a flat slab the depth grows with the slant exactly as the facing's cosine shrinks, so every
direction counts alike (§5). The depth is the paint, not the heat, and it is why a pull carries an amount.

Observation has a depth too: how far along a direction a thing lies, in thumbs. Tom, the same day: "Observation has
it's own version of depth". Run 12 asked how far the likeness goes. It was exact, and nothing was killed.

It goes as far as parallax. Two looks a counted step B apart give a distance in steps, 1/(d/B) = 1/s₁ + 1/s₂, with no
amount. Two strengths of a pull, taken a step B apart along the line to a mass, give d/B = 1/(√ρ − 1), where ρ is the
ratio of the two strengths. No mass is in it, and no G. Gravity has a parallax of its own.

It stops at the lay. Observation's depth is laid by the thumb: half the heat lies within one thumb, near and far swap
by d ↔ H²/d, and scaling the world moves nothing. Gravity's depth is laid flat. Every length of matter counts alike at
any distance. Its half point is the middle of the matter, which is no length of the standpoint's. No exchange fits it;
the best one misses by 0.17. And scaling moves it.

Two smaller differences. Two looks are best placed half a step from the thing; two strengths are best nearest and get
worse steadily, with no best place between. And a pull's direction is a sum: two of them cross on a single mass, but on
a pair of masses 2a apart they cross off the centre by about 3(a/d)², where two looks at a thing cross on it.

Claude's reading, not ruled: the likeness holds in place and in parallax, and stops at the lay. One depth is a ratio.
The other is an amount.

## 13. An orbit

The same day Tom asked whether the Cauchy could carry a planet's orbit: "We would have to create a payload that gives
the same results". ORB runs 2 to 5 did it exactly, with predictions committed first.

A planet on an ellipse of eccentricity e has three angles, all old. The true anomaly ν is its direction from the Sun.
The mean anomaly M runs evenly with time. The eccentric anomaly E is a helper between them, and the three are joined by
tan(ν/2) = √((1 + e)/(1 − e))·tan(E/2) and by Kepler's equation, M = E − e·sin E.

The stage: take E evenly round the orbit and look at the direction ν it gives. That spread is a wrapped Cauchy, the
Cauchy bent round a circle, of width β = (1 − √(1 − e²))/e, centred on the orbit's far point. It is set by the
eccentricity alone. No size, no mass, no G.

The payload: the time the planet spends in each direction is that stage times r/a, its distance from the Sun in the
orbit's own size, exactly. Summed over the stage it is Kepler's equation. Amounts come in at one place only, the period
2π√(a³/GM), which has no e in it. The clock's own law over directions is no wrapped Cauchy at all: the stage carries the
shape, the payload carries the time.

Where the stage stands (runs 4 and 5): its standpoint is −β in the circle's disc. Write e = sin φ, φ the angular
eccentricity of the old tables, and β = tan(φ/2), a half-slope. Write e = tanh η instead, and the map from E to ν is a
hyperbolic translation by η. That is the form of relativistic aberration, the way a moving observer's sky is squeezed
forward, with the eccentricity standing where the speed stands. Two such maps one after the other compose as
relativity adds speeds, (e₁ + e₂)/(1 + e₁e₂). The stage's far-over-near ratio is r_max/r_min.

A shared form, not a claim that an orbit is a boost. Every piece is classical: the half-angle relation, β (Brouwer and
Clemence define it), the areal law, and the closure of the wrapped Cauchy under maps of the circle (McCullagh, 1996). A
prior-work search was run afterwards, its expectations committed first (`plans/orb-literature.md`), and then a second
one. The map is Gauss's own: "Greater elegance is attained in most of these formulas by introducing in the place of e
the angle the sine of which = e" (*Theoria Motus*, 1809, Book I, Art. 8), and his formula VII is tan ½E =
tan ½v·tan(45° − ½φ). β is in the textbooks. The likeness to aberration is named once: a short
unrefereed paper, "Gudermannian – Kepler Equations of Planetary Motion" (on Academia.edu, author and date unknown),
writes the anomaly relation as a Lorentz transformation, "similar to light aberration equations"; only its abstract
was seen, and it would not open. It is not in the standard accounts: Wikipedia's articles on relativistic aberration
and on the eccentric and true anomalies, all opened, do not mention it, and a result that was known would be explained
there (Tom, 25 November: "we should expect this idea to be well known if it was known and the well explained in wiki").
So it is not well known, whatever that one paper says. Not found: the composition by velocity addition, the stage as a wrapped Cauchy from −β, and time per
direction as the stage times r/a. Not found is not absent; these are recorded for later confirmation, not claimed.

Past the ellipse (runs 6 to 8, exact) the eccentric anomaly's stage goes, since an open orbit has no finite total, and
a plainer Cauchy takes its place. On a parabola the directions from the Sun, taken evenly, land across the axis as the
Cauchy of scale p, y = p·tan(ν/2): a lighthouse turning at half the rate, with the orbit itself as the shore. The clock
over y is that density turned upside down, and its sum is Barker's equation, the parabola's Kepler. On a hyperbola the
even directions give the same Cauchy cut off at the asymptotes. The payload r/a holds on every conic. And an ellipse of
eccentricity e and a hyperbola of eccentricity 1/e share the factor k exactly, their maps differing only by tan
against tanh: an exchange, e ↔ 1/e, with the parabola its fixed point, as the corner is the reciprocal's.

## 14. Bodies, motion and delay

Six more runs on 25 November (GDI, MVM, LDL, ACC twice, COS, all exact, their predictions committed first) took what §9 found for two
masses at rest to bodies with size, to masses that move and speed up, and to light's own delay.

A ball. Two plumb lines outside a ball point at its centre exactly. No set of directions gives its radius: a place
inside reads the same direction as a place outside, and the radius appears in neither. What gives the radius is a
ratio of strengths along a line through the centre: outside, the strength times the distance squared stays the same;
inside it falls as the cube of the distance over the radius. One such ratio inside gives the radius in counted steps,
with no mass.

A lens. A point mass makes two images of one source, on a line through it. Their directions alone give the ring's
size, the geometric mean of their two distances from the lens, and the source's place. Their brightness ratio is the
square of the ratio of those distances (a prediction of the fourth power was Claude's arithmetic slip, and stays
killed). So the brightness ratio places a lens no one sees. An isothermal sphere, a lens whose bending is the same at
every distance, puts its ring at the arithmetic mean instead. Which mean is the corner belongs to how the lens bends,
the paint, not to the stage.

A pair in orbit. In the frame turning with the pair, gravity and the turning together, the directions at six places
give the mass ratio, the separation and the places, to fifteen digits, and scaling every length with the rate of the
orbit moves none of them. The one second solution the fit found is the same pair with its two masses' labels swapped.

A moving mass. At rest, a look and a pull point the same way. When the mass moves they part. The look points where the
light left it, the retarded place; the present place comes from one look only with the speed and the distance carried
in. The pull, in the weak field of general relativity, points where the mass is now, departing only at the square of
the speed: a tenth of light's speed leaves it 0.0145 radians off, the look 0.080. (A prediction of the look's angle left
out a ratio of distances; it was killed by Claude's geometry, and the triangle's own relation holds to 191 digits.) Not
foreseen: along the line of motion, past c/√3, the pull turns into a push, a known and debated result.

Light's delay. The two images of a lensed source arrive at different times. For a point mass the delay, in its own
units, is ½(q − 1/q) for the extra path and ln q for the slowing near the mass, q the ratio of the images' distances
from the lens. Both come from the directions alone; the slowing is the exchange's own logarithm, the level between the
two images, and the extra path is never the smaller. For the isothermal sphere the extra paths cancel and the delay is
all slowing. The delay's size is an amount: 4GM(1 + z)/c³ for the point mass, so a delay counted in days gives the
mass, with no distance, once G and the second are carried in.

An accelerated mass. A fourth run (ACC) took a source at rest at one moment but speeding up. The look points where the
light left, which, for a source just come to rest, is a little along its acceleration: half the acceleration times the
light-time squared. An opposite charge's pull points at the same place, to that order and the next. Gravity's weak-field
pull points seven times as far along, three and a half times the acceleration times the light-time squared. The
departure is the acceleration times the distance over c², times the sine of the angle between them, and scaling every
length and time moves none of it. What accelerates the mass, a rope or a rocket or a second body, was left out, and its
own field would add, so the seven is the moving mass's part of the pull. A second run gave the mass a partner, a pair in orbit, each speeding the other up, with nothing left out at that order: the seven stays. The partner's own field adds terms that grow with the square of the masses and with the speeds, and none that holds an acceleration. The whole agrees, term by term, with the Einstein–Infeld–Hoffmann equation of 1938.

A cosine with no amount. One question from §10 was whether a read holds anything weighted by the cosine to a facing, as a pull is, but with no amount. It does (COS). Take the paint as presence only, a direction occupied or not, and add up the occupied directions each weighted by its cosine: the projected solid angle, which lighting engineers call a view factor. A facing's share less its opposite's is that sum's component along the facing, as with a pull, and scaling the world or remapping every density leaves it alone. A pull is the same sum with each occupied direction weighed by its column of matter. For a ball the sum is π times the radius over the distance, squared: an inverse square with no mass in it, and the pull is that times one amount, the density times four thirds of the radius, at every distance outside (Newton's shell theorem, read this way). For a cube the amount changes with distance, by 43% between half a side and twenty sides off. And a ball hidden behind another adds nothing to the read's sum and its whole share to the pull: a read counts a direction once, a pull sums all along it.

Each of these sorts as §9 did. Directions give places, and ratios give sizes in steps or in angle. A mass, a distance,
a delay in seconds: each comes with an amount carried in, and with nothing else.

## 15. What was killed

Nineteen runs, about a hundred and twenty predictions. Nine were killed, all by Claude's own guesses, arithmetic or code, and they
stay recorded as killed.

- GRV's third prediction guessed that the pairing of opposite columns holds between two cubes as it does between two
  spheres. It does not; the pairing is the sphere's.

## 16. What this does not say

It does not say what gravity is. Tom, the same day: "what is gravity specificaly. but lets not get bogged down".
Every formula here is Newton's or older, or the textbook lens equation taken as given. The size of the Einstein radius
is general relativity's and was not tested.

It does not say that the pull is a read. The series keeps that word for a standpoint facing one way, taking the first
thing on each line, with no amount. A pull has none of those three. What the runs show is narrower: the weight over
directions is shared.

It does not say that anything in the world takes a pull with a facing. The faced take is a definition, made to test
one sentence.

The question it started from, whether a place with no facing is a second way to read, is settled by a ruling made after
the runs (24 November): it is not a read. A read is one facing, continuous, unmoved by scaling, and carries no amount. A
pull has no facing, is a sum, moves with scaling, and carries an amount. What the two share is the stage. The sixth run
gives the exact sense in which no facing is all facings at once: each facing taken with its direction attached, and
each joined to its opposite by subtracting.

## 17. What it changes

For the series, six things, each a reading.

The stage is wider than the look. The Cauchy was met as the weight a standpoint lays over directions. It is also the
weight a mass lays over a plane, the weight a sheet's pull is averaged with, and, along a line, a law the bending of
light carries onto itself. In each case what is the stage's does not carry the amount and does not move under scaling,
and what carries the amount is the play's.

Why the stage is shared can be said in one line. The Cauchy is the law of anything spread evenly over directions from a
point, laid on a plane. A look is that with no amount, over one hemisphere. A pull is that with an amount, over both
hemispheres at once, summed at a place. So this is no new physics and no mechanism. It is about how the Cauchy joins
observation and gravity: one geometric fact, used twice, with light and sound from a point as further uses. Tom
narrowed the point on 24 November: "The cauchy provides new ways to think about gravity. Even small insights are
valuable." A way of thinking about known gravity is what the runs give, and that is said plainly here.

The heat and the paint come apart. Tom, 25 November: "classical does not distinguish the payload from the heat. We show
that gravity is separate from the heat". The classical formula does have two factors, a weight over directions and the
matter met along each. It holds both as gravity's, the weight being what the inverse square gives. Here the weight is
the stage's: the same for a look with no mass in it, and for every arrangement of matter. What is gravity's is the
paint, the amount along each direction (§12). The separation is of roles. A pull needs both, and no classical number
changes. This is no judgement of the classical account, which gets every number right.

Agreeing with it is itself a result. Tom, 24 November: "Same results with cauchy framing to classical is a result in
it self". Each run was a place where the stage's account and the classical one could have parted, and they did not.
What tells the two forms apart is what each needs. The shape of a pull through a plane, and a buried line's depth
(§11), come with no amount at all.

A corner can be chosen or owned. The look's corner sits where the thumb puts it. A pair of masses has a corner at √μ,
a lens has one at its ring, a ball has one at its surface. The exchange is the same in each. What sets the corner's
place is different: a choice for the look, an amount for the rest.

And a facing is what separates adding from subtracting. A look's two halves add to a whole. A pull's two halves
subtract to a difference, and only the difference has a limit. One facing of an endless even world has no total, as the
Cauchy has no mean.

For the plumb line: it hangs along the one line left when every direction has been taken with its opposite. It has no
front. That is why it can show which way is down.
