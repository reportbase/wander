# Scaffolding: what a denominator is for

*The twentieth of a series of essays on what can be known from where one stands. Draft, 12 November 2026. It gathers
the ruling of 12 November (R883) and an older idea it brings back, the centring of the archived* Degrees of Freedom
*§3.1 and §4.2, and follows* Reads and Writes *§1 ("A denominator is scaffolding", "The paper in one equation") and §6
("A tower of extents"). Every number in it is plain arithmetic.*

A wall goes up inside scaffolding. The scaffolding is there first, it can be of any make, and when the wall stands it
comes down and leaves no mark on the wall. Nobody looking at the finished building can tell whether the poles were steel
or bamboo.

This essay is about the part of a ratio that works that way. Every reading in this series is one thing *in terms of*
another, and the other, the denominator, is needed before there is any reading at all. Yet when readings are put
together it drops out of what they give. It is important and it is absent, and those are not in conflict: that is what
scaffolding is.

The series has two ideas, reading against writing and the Cauchy, and two tests, scale the world and change what
arrives. Here the first test is looked at from underneath. A reading is unmoved when the world is scaled because its
denominator scaled with it; the essay asks what that denominator is, when it comes down, and where it never does.

The order: in terms of (§1), any size will do (§2), never zero (§3), the tower, where the middle comes down (§4), the
last storey (§5), the circle's equation (§6), every figure (§7), a denominator forgotten (§8), where the picture fails
(§9), who saw which piece (§10), and what it changes (§11).

---

## 1. In terms of

Hold your thumb out at arm's length and look at a door across the street. The door is three thumbs tall. That is a
reading, and it is the door *in terms of* the thumb. Take the thumb away and nothing is left to say: "three" by itself
is not a reading of anything.

So the denominator is not a detail of the reading. It is half of it. In this series the reading is the slope s = v/h,
the up share against the thumb's share, and the thumb, H, is what everything is read against. A read with no
denominator is not a scale-free read. It is no read.

One caution, kept through the essay. Within a single read nothing is taken away. The thumb's size was never in the
reading to begin with; the door is three thumbs tall whether the thumb is two centimetres or two metres across, provided
the world is that much larger too. What comes down, later, comes down when readings are joined.

## 2. Any size will do

The test of scaffolding is that the finished thing must not depend on which scaffolding was used. Take two lengths, 3
and 4, and read each in terms of a third. In terms of 1 they read 3 and 4. In terms of 7 they read 3/7 and 4/7. In terms
of 1000 they read 0.003 and 0.004. In terms of 0.004 they read 750 and 1000. Put the two readings against each other and
every time the answer is 3/4.

Four different scaffolds, four different pairs of readings, one result. This is the scale test of essay 1 seen from the
other side: scale the world, thumb included, and no reading moves, because the thing each reading was in terms of moved
with it.

## 3. Never zero

Scaffolding can be of any make. It cannot be missing. Let the denominator go to zero and the reading does not become
free of it; the reading runs off without limit.

The series has a name for that place: the horizon. In the sweep from the thumb to the thing, the thumb's share h empties
as the up share v fills, and where h is empty the slope v/h has nothing under it. The horizon is not a place too far
away. It is the place where the thing a reading is in terms of has gone. A read does not arrive there, and for the
same reason no arithmetic gets past it.

So the two properties sit together. Any size: the result does not care. Never zero: there is no result without it.

## 4. The tower: the middle comes down

Now join readings. A table is three quarters as tall as the door. The door is two fifths as tall as the wall. Then the
table is three tenths as tall as the wall:

  3/4 × 2/5 = 3/10.

The door was the denominator of the first reading and the numerator of the second. It was needed for both. It is in
neither side of the result. Had the door been half as tall, the two readings would have been 3/2 and 1/5, and the table
would still be three tenths of the wall.

That is a tower of readings, each storey read against the next, and the storeys in the middle are scaffolding. Three
things follow, all of them in *Reads and Writes* §6 already and all exact.

*A chain collapses to its ends.* However many storeys, the product is the top in terms of the bottom. On the walk of
essay 19 the house's corner is 514 strides and two sevenths from the door, and none of the looks in between need be
kept: each look's reading against the next telescopes, and two looks one counted stride apart give the whole.

*A loop closes.* Read the wall in terms of the table, 10/3, and go round: 3/4 × 2/5 × 10/3 = 1. If the door against the
wall was misread as 1/2, the loop gives 5/4. The loop shows an error. It cannot say which reading made it.

*N things hold every ratio between them.* Twenty-four things laid by their readings against one of them give all 276
ratios between pairs, as gaps, with nothing computed. Which of the twenty-four is the one read against does not matter.

Joining is not a single read's doing. A read is one standpoint, one moment; a chain is several, kept and put together,
and "the door drops out" is said by whoever keeps them, in things, which are writes. Scaffolding is a word for writes. A read has the three
thumbs of the door and nothing has come down.

## 5. The last storey

The scaffolding comes down in the middle of the tower. It does not come down at the bottom. The table is three tenths
*of the wall*, and that last "in terms of" is still standing.

This is the oldest statement of what a read lacks, in a new place. A tower of ratios gives the whole building one
number short, and the one number is the size of whatever the bottom storey was read against. There are three places a
tower can end.

- *It ends on the thumb.* Everything is in thumbs. Nothing is kept and nothing is known about size.
- *It ends on a counted step.* Two looks a stride apart place a thing: twelve strides off, say, and another thing
  thirty. The stride is a scaffold that was put up, a thing with a size, a write; the counts laid over it, twelve and
  thirty, have no unit. Take the two against each other, 12/30 = 2/5,
  and the stride comes down: with a stride twice as long the counts are 6 and 15 and the ratio the same. Take one alone,
  twelve strides, and the stride is still under it. Essay 19 called the stride the smallest write there is, and
  said a law brings the chain of writes down to its least and not to zero. The least is this last storey.
- *It ends on an agreed unit.* The stride is 0.7 metres. Now the bottom storey is a stick kept somewhere and agreed
  on, and the result is moved by scaling the world against that stick. That is an amount put down in a unit, the write the first
  test finds (essay 1).

A unit, on this picture, is scaffolding that everyone has agreed to leave up.

## 6. The circle's equation

The same thing is in the most familiar equation there is. Take a point 3 across and 4 up. Its distance from the centre
is 5, and

  3² + 4² = 5².

Kept as written, this is in terms of a unit: metres, inches, anything agreed. Scale the world and every term moves.
Now choose a denominator from inside the figure. There are two to choose from.

| in terms of | the equation becomes | what it is |
|---|---|---|
| a unit | x² + y² = r² | the circle as a size: 9 + 16 = 25 |
| the pair's own size, r | h² + v² = 1 | the circle as shares: 0.36 + 0.64 = 1 |
| the thumb, x | 1 + s² = (r/x)² | one slope, s = 4/3: 1 + 16/9 = 25/9 |

The second row is the circle everyone knows, and it says something plainer than a fact about triangles. The two shares
0.6 and 0.8 add up in squares to 1 exactly because both were read against the same denominator, and that denominator
was the pair's own size. The older papers called the equation a receipt: proof that two readings were taken in terms of
one thing. Change the 3 and 4 to 21 and 28, or to 0.03 and 0.04, and the shares are 0.6 and 0.8 again. The 5 was needed
to get them. It is in neither.

The third row is the read's. It needs no 5, only the one slope 4/3, and its right side turned over is the thumb's
share squared, 9/25. That quantity, 1/(1 + s²), is the heat of essay 2: the Cauchy. So the circle and the Cauchy are
one equation taken in terms of two different things from inside it.

They do not cost the same. The slope needs nothing but the two lengths against each other. The shares need the 5,
which is a square root of the two together, and in general no fraction: that is the blueprint's construction, as essay 15
has it. And the circle held whole, closed and with every sign, is the blueprint's altogether: classical
geometry's circle, seen from no standpoint, set here for comparison. A read has the slope and
its heat.

## 7. Every figure

Read a figure from a point inside it: 2 to its edge in one direction, 3 in the next, 6 in a third. Take each against
the one before: 3/2, then 2, then, coming back round, 1/3. Those ratios are the figure's shape. Each radius served as
the denominator of the next reading and then came down. Go all the way round and the product is 1,

  3/2 × 2 × 1/3 = 1,

which is §4's loop again: it checks the reading, not the figure.

Two things follow. The ratios give the whole figure one number short, and the number is its size: scale the three radii
to 20, 30 and 60 and the ratios do not move. And the circle is the figure whose ratios are all 1, the one figure that
leaves nothing behind when its scaffolding comes down. The older papers put this as "every figure is a centring; the
circle is the one with no residue". *Reads and Writes* §2 says it as the payload recovered by its ratios, all but the
overall size.

## 8. A denominator forgotten

Because the denominator leaves no mark, it is easy to forget which one was used, and then two readings are joined that
were never in terms of the same thing.

A price falls by half and then rises by half. It does not come back: 100 becomes 50 and then 75. The first half was in
terms of 100 and the second in terms of 50. Or take two lengths, 3 and 4. The longer is a third more than the shorter,
and the shorter is a quarter less than the longer; a third and a quarter are the same gap read in terms of its two ends.
Essay 9 collects paradoxes of this kind. Each is a tower built with a storey that does not rest on the one below.

The fullest case is two people across a field, each seeing the other smaller (*Each Sees the Other Smaller*, the
series' older paper on denominator paradoxes). Each reads the other in terms of its own outstretched hand, so the two
readings stand on different scaffolds, both private. Say they are 1.8 and 1.6 tall and ten arm's lengths apart: one
reads 0.089, the other 0.1125, and both are less than 1. Written down as bare numbers they contradict each other. The
number line has no place to record whose hand a reading was in terms of, so the scaffold is lost exactly where it was
still needed.

The paper's remedy is an exchange, and it is scaffolding taken down in the right order. Each reading has two things
under it: the observer's own size, private, and the distance between them, shared. Divide one reading by the other and the
shared distance comes down, leaving the sizes against each other: 0.089/0.1125 is 0.79, whose square root is 0.889, and
1.6/1.8 is 0.889. Multiply them and the private sizes come down, leaving the distance: 0.089 × 0.1125 is 0.01, whose
square root is one in ten. Neither read had either result alone. Its other cases are this essay's too: exchange rates
round a triangle multiply to 1, which is §4's loop, and a rate that is off shows in the loop and cannot be placed by it.

Light does the same exchange. Two discs facing each other each take a share of the other's view, A/d² to first order;
the two reads are reciprocal (each disc's area times its share of the other is the same), so their ratio leaves the
sizes against each other and their product, far off, the distance in the sizes' geometric mean (ISX, exact;
`../inverse-square.md`, "From one vantage").

The check is §4's loop and §6's receipt. Readings on one denominator close: the loop multiplies to 1, the squares of the
shares add to 1. When they do not, a denominator changed somewhere.

## 9. Where the picture fails

The idea was first put as a Roman arch, built on a wooden centring that is struck once the keystone is in. That picture
was tried in the older papers and withdrawn, and the reasons still hold.

An arch is a thing in sizes: stones that bear weight. A reading is not; it is a direction. And an arch is built, where a
read builds nothing. Today's vocabulary says why the second objection was right and what to keep of it. Building is
writing. A scaffold is something put up and kept so that reads can be joined: a thing with a size, a write, not
part of any read.

So the picture is used here with three limits. Nothing is struck from a single read; the thumb's size was never in it.
What comes down comes down in the middle of a chain, and it is the keeper of the chain who sees it go. And the bottom
storey never comes down at all.

## 10. Who saw which piece

The pieces are old. Euclid's fifth book, after Eudoxus, defines a ratio as a relation in respect of size between two
magnitudes of the same kind: a reading with its denominator named. That middle terms drop out of a chain is its
proposition 22, the ratio *ex aequali*. Dimensional analysis (Buckingham 1914) is the statement that the laws of
physics can be written in ratios from which the units have come down, with a count of how many must be left standing.
Homogeneous coordinates (Möbius 1827) keep numerator and denominator as a pair so that a zero denominator is a place
and not a failure: the horizon of §3. And the Roman centring is as old as the arch.

What the series adds is the sorting. The denominator is the read's "in terms of"; its size is in no read; in a chain it
is the keeper's scaffolding; the last one standing is the difference between a ratio, a count and a size.

## 11. What it changes

*Importance and absence stop looking like a puzzle.* The thumb is in every reading and its size is in none. That is not
a cancellation that happened to work out. It is the job.

*There are three places a tower can end.* On the thumb, a read. On a step, an extent: a read still where two looks
hold it together, a count where the steps are counted out. On an agreed unit, a size, a write. The series' two tests ask, of any number, what its last storey is.

*The circle and the Cauchy are one equation.* The difference is what it was taken in terms of, and that choice is also
the difference in what each costs.

*An equation that closes is a receipt.* Shares that add to one, a loop that multiplies to one: each says the readings
shared a denominator, and neither says how large it was.

*A unit is scaffolding left up by agreement.* That is where the world's size comes in, and the only place.
