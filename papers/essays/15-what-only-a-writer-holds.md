# What only a writer holds: π, e and the completed infinity

*The fifteenth of a series of essays on what can be known from where one stands. Draft, 30 October 2026.*

In 1683 Jacob Bernoulli asked a banker's question. Lend one coin at a hundred per cent a year, paid once at the year's
end, and you get two back. Pay half the interest at midyear and let it earn too, and you get (1 + ½)² = 2.25. Pay it
monthly and you get (1 + 1/12)¹² = 2.6130; daily, 2.7146; hourly, 2.71813; every minute, 2.718279. The amounts climb
and the climb slows, and Bernoulli saw that they close in on a number between 2 and 3 that no number of payments
reaches. It is now called e, 2.718281828…

Every step of the bank's arithmetic is a fraction. One coin, a rate given as a fraction, a count of payments: whatever
the count, the result is a fraction too, (n + 1)ⁿ/nⁿ. The number they close in on is not. To have e you must have done
every payment there could be, which no bank and no clock does. It is the end of a process that does not end.

π is the same. 1 − 1/3 + 1/5 − 1/7 + … is a sum of fractions, and every partial sum is a fraction; five terms give
0.8349, a thousand give π/4 to within a two-thousandth, and none gives π/4. To have π you must have added every term.

The earlier essays found that π is not a reader's number (essay 14): it is the total of a completed turn, and the
reader, which holds one length and never closes its turn, does not hold it. This essay asks the same of e, and finds
the same answer for a reason of the same kind. Its claim:

> The transcendental numbers are what a finished infinity gives. The reader makes every step toward them and holds
> none of them. π completes the turn; e completes the scale. Only a writer, holding the whole, holds either.

None of this changes a digit of either number. It says whose numbers they are.

## 1. The ladder

The series has been climbing a ladder of numbers by what it takes to make them (essay 14, the sixth section).

**One length, finite steps: the rationals.** A reader holds one length, H. It can count H, double it, halve it, and
take any length it has made over any other. Everything it makes that way is a ratio of whole numbers, a rational. Its
readings compose by tangent addition, (a + b)/(1 − ab), which takes rationals to rationals; they turn over (s to 1/s)
and stay rational; their order is kept. The corner, s = 1, is rational. A reader working in finite steps from one length
never leaves the rationals.

**Two lengths, finite steps: the algebraic numbers.** Combine two lengths at right angles and the diagonal of the unit
square is √2, which is no ratio of whole numbers. Square roots, cube roots, any root of a polynomial with rational
coefficients: these are the algebraic numbers, and every one of them is reached in finitely many steps by adding,
multiplying, dividing and taking roots. It takes a second variable held against the first, so it is the writer's: the
reader meets the direction of the diagonal (it reads s = 1 there, exactly) and never makes the length.

**A completed infinity: the transcendental numbers.** The algebraic numbers are closed under every finite operation:
add, subtract, multiply, divide or take roots of algebraic numbers, any finite number of times, and the result is
algebraic. So a number that is not algebraic, a transcendental, is not reached by any finite construction at all. It is
reached only as the limit of an infinite one, and is had only by someone for whom that infinity is finished.

Being a limit is not what makes a number transcendental (√2 is a limit too, of 1, 1.4, 1.41, …). Being reachable only
as a limit is. That is the ladder's top rung, and the reader, which never finishes, is on the first.

## 2. π completes the turn

The reader turns from home and meets the corner, the direction where v equals h. It reads it as s = 1, exactly, a
rational. What it does not read is how much of a turn that direction is. The share of a quarter turn passed at the
corner is ½: that is the median of the reader's sweep, a count, and rational. The angle is π/4, and π/4 is

  arctan 1 = 1 − 1/3 + 1/5 − 1/7 + 1/9 − …

(Madhava's series, found again by Gregory and Leibniz). The direction is the reader's; its angle is that infinite sum.
To state the angle is to hold the whole turn against which the corner is a quarter of a half, and then to finish the sum.

Machin's formula (1706) shows the split with no rounding at all. The reader can compose directions by tangent addition.
Take the direction 1/5 four times over: (1/5 ⊕ 1/5) is 5/12, twice that is 120/119. Take away 1/239: (120/119 − 1/239)
over (1 + 120/(119·239)) is exactly 1. Four steps of one fifth and one step back of one part in 239 land on the corner,
in fractions, every step checkable. That much is the reader's, and it is exact. Machin's π is the next step: write each
direction as its angle by the arctangent's series and add, π/4 = 4·arctan(1/5) − arctan(1/239). The series for 1/5 and
1/239 converge fast (that is why Machin chose them; he had a hundred digits in 1706), but they are series. The identity
among the directions is finite; the number π is its completion.

Viète's product (1593) shows it from the other side:

  2/π = (√2/2) · (√(2 + √2)/2) · (√(2 + √(2 + √2))/2) · …

Every factor is algebraic, a square root of the one before plus two: each is the writer's bisection of a turn (OFS §5,
"Why halving"). Any finite number of factors gives an algebraic number. The product of all of them is 2/π.

So π sits where essay 1 put V. The reader never knows V (OFS §3.8): to know V is to hold both hemispheres, the turn
closed. A circle's circumference over its diameter is a statement about the closed curve, held whole; the reader's turn
runs from home toward a horizon it never reaches. π is the total of a turn that has been completed, and the completing is
the writer's.

## 3. e completes the scale

The reader's other freedom is size. Essay 3 and OFS §2 named the reader's ignorance as two groups, after Jaynes: turns
(it does not know which way it faces) and rescalings (it does not know how large anything is, κ = V*/H). Turns gave π.
Rescalings give e.

The reader counts sizes in doublings. Past the corner its addresses go a level a doubling (essay 4), and the count of
levels between H and a thing 8H away is 3, exactly. A doubling is a step the reader takes, like a step of tangent
addition: from one length, finite, rational. Halving H is as easy. A thing 3H away is between levels, and the reader
holds it there, between its first and second doubling, by its order among the levels (essay 4, "the count is the level
of detail").

The number of levels as a continuous quantity is the logarithm, and to give it a value is to choose a unit for it and
finish a series. In doublings, log₂ 3 = 1.58496…; in the natural unit, ln 3 = 1.0986…; one doubling is ln 2 =
0.693147…, which is

  ln 2 = 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + …

again an alternating sum of fractions, again every partial sum a fraction (a thousand terms give 0.69265), again the
number itself only at the end. The doubling is the reader's step. Its length in nats is the writer's measure of it.

e is the base that makes that measure natural: the size reached by growing continuously at a rate of one, every bit of
growth itself growing at once. That is Bernoulli's bank with the payments made infinitely often, and it is

  e = 1 + 1/1! + 1/2! + 1/3! + … ,

ten terms of which give 98641/36288 = 2.7182815, three ten-millionths short, and no number of terms gives e. The
reader steps its size by doubling, a step at a time. To compound without steps is to have finished infinitely many
steps, and that is what e records.

And as π was the turn completed, e is tied to the size completed. The unknown size of the world, in the units the reader
might have used, is a₀ = ln(V*/H) (OFS §7.4, essay 6: a shape as ln r = a₀ + f(θ), where no ratio reads a₀). The reader
reads the shape, f, as differences of levels, and never a₀. To give a₀ a value is to know V*, the size as an amount: a
writing. The constant that turns a size into a number of nats belongs to the same writer who knows the size.

## 4. Two groups, two constants

Put the two side by side and they are one fact about two groups.

Each of the reader's two groups is a one-parameter family of transformations: turning by θ, rescaling by κ. Each has an
exponential map, the function that turns a parameter that adds into a transformation that composes. For rescalings the
map is κ = e^t: two rescalings compose by multiplying, their parameters add. For turns it is e^(iθ): two turns compose,
their angles add. The constants live in those two maps.

- The turns close. A turn by a full revolution is no turn at all, so the turn's exponential map has a period, and the
  period is 2π. π is the measure of the group's closing.
- The rescalings do not close. Doubling, doubling again, never comes back. Their exponential map has no period, only a
  base, and the base that makes its parameter count growth at a rate of one is e.

So π is what you need to say that the turn comes back, and e is what you need to say how the scale goes on. The reader
uses both groups without stating either constant: it composes turns by tangent addition and sizes by doubling, finite
steps, rational. The writer, who holds the whole of each group (the turn once round, the scale as a continuum of
amounts), states its constant.

The complex logarithm puts them in one line:

  ln z = ln r + iθ.

The real part is the scale's, measured in e's unit; the imaginary part is the turn's, measured against π's total.
Essay 2 (§9) read it as address and payload, the turn the address and the size the payload, with the scale test on the
real part alone. Here it reads as the two completions: e on the real axis, π on the imaginary.

## 5. Where they meet

Euler's identity is the place where the two constants touch:

  e^(iπ) = −1.

The scale's base, raised to the turn's half total, lands on −1. Essay 2 met this already as the writer's notation for
something the reader holds in finite terms: the central sweep's landing point at the horizon (OFS §4, the landing point
(h + iv)² at twice the angle reaching −1 when the inscribed reading reaches the horizon). The reader holds that
direction as a pair, [0 : 1], with nothing infinite in it. The writer writes the same thing with both its constants.

It is also where the history of the two numbers met. Euler showed e irrational in 1737; Lambert showed π irrational in
1761. Liouville (1844) built the first numbers proved transcendental, made to order by an infinite series whose terms
shrink too fast for any polynomial to catch. Hermite (1873) proved e transcendental. Lindemann (1882) proved π
transcendental, and he did it through e: a power of e with an algebraic exponent other than zero is never algebraic; if
π were algebraic, so would be iπ, and e^(iπ) would be transcendental; but it is −1. The proof that π cannot be made in
finite steps goes through the point where the turn's constant and the scale's meet.

Cantor added the measure of it (1874): the algebraic numbers can be listed, one after another, and the real numbers
cannot, so almost every number is transcendental. On this essay's reading, almost every number is a completed
infinity, and the numbers anyone makes in finite steps are a thin, countable scaffolding among them.

## 6. Met, not made

None of this keeps transcendental numbers out of the reader's world. They are there, and the reader meets them.

A direction whose reading v/h is tan(1) (the turn of one radian, 1.5574…) is a direction like any other. Paint arrives
along it; the reader's hand passes it; the reader orders it among its neighbours, exactly. What the reader does not do is
make its number. It holds the direction as a pair and its place by order; the decimal 1.5574… is the writer's account of
where it is.

The same goes for the levels. A thing e times H away is a thing the reader holds between its first and second doubling,
nearer the second. The reader holds it; it does not hold "e times".

This is essay 14's "met, not made" one rung higher. The reader meets irrational directions and does not make √2; it
meets transcendental directions and sizes and does not make π or e. Its own numbers, the ones it makes, are the
rationals, and with them it does everything a reader does: composes, turns over, orders, counts levels, finds the corner.

V is learned the same way. A reader becoming a writer by parallax (the sixth essay, "Becoming the writer") knows V by
degrees: in the step it took, to within a band set by its baseline and its resolution, for the things near enough. Each
standpoint more is one more finite step; holding V exactly, like holding π, is the end of a process that does not end.

## 7. A machine is a reader here

Essay 12 asked what a computer can hold, and found that every machine operation on numbers is monotone and finite. Here
is the same thing from the ladder.

Every floating-point number is rational: an integer times a power of two. The machine's π, `Math.PI` in JavaScript, is
884279719003555/281474976710656, a fraction with 2⁴⁸ below the line, within 1.2 × 10⁻¹⁶ of π and not π. Its e is
another such fraction. A machine computing π to a billion digits is running Madhava's or Machin's series, or one of their
faster cousins, for longer; at every moment it holds a rational, the next approximant. It is doing what the reader does,
and doing it well.

What the machine can do, when asked to keep the writer's numbers, is keep them as names: a computer algebra system holds
`pi` and `E` as symbols, and proves `exp(I*pi) == -1` by rules about the symbols, not by finishing the series. It holds
the writer's statement, not the writer's number. That is the same move essay 12 recommended for the horizon: keep the
pair [0 : 1] instead of the infinite chart value. Where the infinity is a horizon or a completion, a machine is faithful
by naming it and computing with order and finite steps; it is never faithful by pretending to have finished.

## 8. What it changes

It changes nothing about π or e or how to compute them. It sorts.

- **π is a writer's number** because it states a turn closed, and closing the turn is knowing V (OFS §3.8). Wherever π
  appears in a result (π/2 and 2/π, the Cauchy's normalizing 1/π, a circle's area), the result is the writer's measure
  of something, and the reader's own version of it is a finite statement: an order, a count, a corner.
- **e is a writer's number** because it states a scale completed, and giving a size as an amount is knowing V*. Wherever
  e or the natural logarithm appears (the lay's 1 + ln x, a decay's e^(−t/τ), the Gaussian's e^(−x²/2)), the reader's
  version is a count of doublings, an order, a ratio.
- **The reader's arithmetic is the rationals**, made from one length in finite steps, and it is enough for everything
  the reader does. The algebraic numbers need a second length, the transcendentals a completed whole; both are where the
  reader becomes a writer (essay 6, "Becoming the writer").

Essay 14 ended on five things called 1. This one ends on two numbers that are not 1 and are not the reader's, and on why:
the turn comes back, and the scale goes on, and to say either in a number you have to have held all of it.
