# What only a writer holds: π, e and the completed infinity

*The fifteenth of a series of essays on what can be known from where one stands. Draft, 30 October 2026.*

In 1683 Jacob Bernoulli asked a banker's question. Lend one coin at a hundred per cent a year, paid once at the year's
end, and you get two back. Pay half the interest at midyear and let it earn too, and you get (1 + ½)² = 2.25. Pay it
monthly and you get (1 + 1/12)¹² = 2.6130; daily, 2.7146; hourly, 2.71813; every minute, 2.718279. The amounts climb
and the climb slows, and Bernoulli saw that they close in on a number between 2 and 3 that no number of payments
reaches. It is now called e, 2.718281828…

Every step of the bank's arithmetic is a fraction. One coin, a rate given as a fraction, a count of payments: whatever
the count, the result is a fraction too, (n + 1)ⁿ/nⁿ. The number they close in on is not. To have e you must have done
every payment there could be, which no bank and no clock does. It is the end of a process that does not end.

π is the same. 1 − 1/3 + 1/5 − 1/7 + … is a sum of fractions, and every partial sum is a fraction; five terms give
0.8349, a thousand give π/4 to within a two-thousandth, and none gives π/4. To have π you must have added every term.

The earlier essays found that π is not a reader's number (essay 14): it is the total of a completed half turn (a line's period; 2π an oriented turn's), and the
reader, which holds one length and never closes its turn, does not hold it. This essay asks the same of e, and finds
the same answer for a reason of the same kind. Its claim:

> The transcendental numbers are what a finished infinity gives. The reader makes every step toward them and holds
> none of them. π completes the turn; e completes the scale. Only a writer, holding the whole, holds either.

None of this changes a digit of either number. It says whose numbers they are.

## 1. The ladder

The series has been climbing a ladder of numbers by what it takes to make them (essay 14, the sixth section).

**One length, finite steps: the rationals.** A reader holds one length, H, the thumb. It can count thumbs, double H, halve
it, and take any length it has made over any other. Everything it makes that way is a ratio of whole numbers, a rational.
Its readings compose by tangent addition, (a + b)/(1 − ab), which takes rationals to rationals; they turn over (s to 1/s)
and stay rational; their order is kept. The corner, s = 1, is rational. A reader working in finite steps from one length
never leaves the rationals.

**Two lengths, finite steps: square roots.** Combine two lengths at right angles and the diagonal of the unit square is √2,
which is no ratio of whole numbers. Square roots, nested as deep as one likes, are what two lengths and a straight edge and
compass give. They are not all the algebraic numbers, the roots of polynomials with rational coefficients: the cube root of
2, which doubles a cube, is beyond them (Wantzel 1837), though it too is fixed by a finite equation. It takes a second
variable held against the first, so it is the writer's: the reader meets the direction of the diagonal (it reads s = 1
there, exactly) and never makes the length.

**A limit: the transcendental numbers.** The algebraic numbers are closed under every finite operation: add,
subtract, multiply, divide or take roots of algebraic numbers, any finite number of times, and the result is algebraic. So
a number that is not algebraic, a transcendental, is reached by no finite algebraic step. As a number it is had only
through a limit, by someone for whom that infinity is finished. It may still have a short name (tan 1, or π = 4·arctan 1):
the reader meets the direction tan 1 whole, and only stating it as a number needs the limit.

Being a limit is not what makes a number transcendental (√2 is a limit too, of 1, 1.4, 1.41, …). Being reachable only
as a limit is. That is the ladder's top rung, and the reader, which never finishes, is on the first.

## 2. π completes the turn

The reader turns from home and meets the corner, the direction where v equals h. It reads it as s = 1, exactly, a
rational. What it does not read is how much of a turn that direction is. The share of a quarter turn passed at the
corner is ½: that is the median of the reader's sweep, a count, and rational. The angle is π/4, and π/4 is

  arctan 1 = 1 − 1/3 + 1/5 − 1/7 + 1/9 − …

(Madhava's series, found again by Gregory and Leibniz). The direction is the reader's; its angle is that infinite sum.
To state the angle is to hold the whole turn against which the corner is a quarter of a half, and then to finish the sum.

Machin's formula (1706) shows the split with no rounding at all. The reader can compose directions by tangent addition.
Take the direction 1/5 four times over: (1/5 ⊕ 1/5) is 5/12, twice that is 120/119. Take away 1/239: (120/119 − 1/239)
over (1 + 120/(119·239)) is exactly 1. Four steps of one fifth and one step back of one part in 239 land on the corner,
in fractions, every step checkable. That much is the reader's, and it is exact. Machin's π is the next step: write each
direction as its angle by the arctangent's series and add, π/4 = 4·arctan(1/5) − arctan(1/239). The series for 1/5 and
1/239 converge fast (that is why Machin chose them; he had a hundred digits in 1706), but they are series. The identity
among the directions is finite; the number π is its completion.

Viète's product (1593) shows it from the other side:

  2/π = (√2/2) · (√(2 + √2)/2) · (√(2 + √(2 + √2))/2) · …

Every factor is algebraic, a square root of the one before plus two: each is the writer's bisection of a turn (OFS §5,
"Why halving"). Any finite number of factors gives an algebraic number. The product of all of them is 2/π.

So π sits where essay 1 put V. The reader never knows V (OFS §3.8): to know V is to hold both hemispheres, the turn
closed. A circle's circumference over its diameter is a statement about the closed curve, held whole; the reader's turn
runs from home toward a horizon it never reaches. π is the total of a turn that has been completed, and the completing is
the writer's.

## 3. e completes the scale

The reader's other freedom is size. Essay 3 and OFS §2 named the reader's ignorance as two groups, after Jaynes: turns
(it does not know which way it faces) and rescalings (it does not know how large anything is, κ = V*/H). Turns gave π.
Rescalings give e.

The reader reads sizes as ratios of its thumb: a thing one thumb wide reads one in two, then one in four, one in eight.
Past the corner a writer laying out its fineness gives as many places to each stretch over which the reading halves (essay 4), and a thing that reads
one in eight is three such stretches out, which counted is 8 thumbs, past 2, 4 and 8, a place. Counting is done in
parallax, where it gives places, and in writing, not in a pure read; what one length counted makes is finite and rational. A thing 3H away reads
one in three, between one in two and one in four, and the reader holds it there by its order among the readings (essay
4, "the count is the detail").

How many times a writer's thumbs go twice over to reach a thing, counted as a continuous quantity, is the logarithm, and to give
it a value is to choose a unit for it and finish a series. Counted in twos, log₂ 3 = 1.58496…; in the natural unit, ln 3 =
1.0986…; one stretch, where the reading halves, is ln 2 = 0.693147…, which is

  ln 2 = 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + …

again an alternating sum of fractions, again every partial sum a fraction (a thousand terms give 0.69265), again the
number itself only at the end. Twice the thumbs is the reader's step. Its length in nats is the writer's measure of it.

e is the base that makes that measure natural: the size reached by growing continuously at a rate of one, every bit of
growth itself growing at once. That is Bernoulli's bank with the payments made infinitely often, and it is

  e = 1 + 1/1! + 1/2! + 1/3! + … ,

ten terms of which give 98641/36288 = 2.7182815, three ten-millionths short, and no number of terms gives e. The
reader steps its size by taking it twice, a step at a time. To compound without steps is to have finished infinitely many
steps, and that is what e records.

And as π was the turn completed, e is tied to the size completed. The unknown size of the world, in the units the reader
might have used, is a₀ = ln(V*/H) (OFS §7.4, essay 6: a shape as ln r = a₀ + f(θ), where no ratio reads a₀). The reader
reads the shape, f, as ratios of its radii, and never a₀. To give a₀ a value is to know V*, the size as an amount: a
writing. The constant that turns a size into a number of nats belongs to the same writer who knows the size.

## 4. Two groups, two constants

Put the two side by side and they are one fact about two groups.

Each of the reader's two groups is a one-parameter family of transformations: turning by θ, rescaling by κ (θ and the
logarithm of κ are the writer's parameters, the ones that add). Each has an
exponential map, the function that turns a parameter that adds into a transformation that composes. For rescalings the
map is κ = e^t: two rescalings compose by multiplying, their parameters add. For turns it is e^(iθ): two turns compose,
their angles add. The constants live in those two maps.

- The turns close. A turn by a full revolution is no turn at all, so the turn's exponential map has a period, and the
  period is 2π. π is the measure of the group's closing.
- The rescalings do not close. Twice, then twice again, never comes back. Their exponential map has no period, only a base,
  and the base that makes its parameter count growth at a rate of one is e.

So π is what you need to say that the turn comes back, and e is what you need to say how the scale goes on. The reader uses
both groups without stating either constant: it composes turns by tangent addition and sizes by taking them twice, finite
steps, rational. The writer, who holds the whole of each group (the turn once round, the scale as a continuum of amounts),
states its constant.

The complex logarithm puts them in one line:

  ln z = ln r + iθ.

The real part is the scale's, measured in e's unit; the imaginary part is the turn's, measured against π's total.
Essay 2 (§9) read it as direction and payload, the turn the direction and the size the payload, with the scale test on the
real part alone. Here it reads as the two completions: e on the real axis, π on the imaginary.

**Which constant goes with which side.** The two parts of that line are the essays' two sides. Turns act on the imaginary
part alone, the direction; scaling the world acts on the real part alone, the payload's size. So π goes with the direction and
e with the payload, and the two are not alike. The direction closes, so it has a total, and π is that total; it is forced.
The payload's size lies on an open line with no total and no natural size; its base is a convention (doublings would count
it as well), and e is the base that lets one unit serve both parts, so that e^(iθ) is a turn of θ. That open line, with no
mark on it, is why no reading ever gives a₀, the overall size (the sixth essay). Closed and forced, open and chosen:
direction and payload, heat and paint, in two numbers.

## 5. Where they meet

Euler's identity is the place where the two constants touch:

  e^(iπ) = −1.

The scale's base, raised to the turn's half total, lands on −1. Essay 2 met this already as the writer's notation for
something the reader holds in finite terms: the central sweep's landing point at the horizon (OFS §4, the landing point
(h + iv)² at twice the angle reaching −1 when the inscribed reading reaches the horizon). The reader holds that
direction as a pair, [0 : 1], with nothing infinite in it. The writer writes the same thing with both its constants.

The Cauchy's own constant comes from the same place. Write the reading s as the point w = (1 + is)/(1 − is), which is
e^(2iθ). From home to the horizon w goes from 1 to −1, and its logarithm gathers iπ on the way; the rate at which it
gathers, (1/2i)·d(ln w)/ds, is 1/(1 + s²). So the half-Cauchy's 2/π is the identity, the logarithm and the change of
variable together (essay 2 §9). Each step the reader can follow in finite terms (w at the horizon is −1, a pair); the
total iπ is the turn completed, the writer's.

The two constants also part at one division. The Gaussian, the writer's law of amounts, written round a point is
(1/2π)·e^(−r²/2) dx dy = (dθ/2π)·(e^(−r²/2) r dr): a uniform turn times a law for the length. Divide by the thumb, one
amount over another, and the length is integrated away: the e goes with it, and what is left is the turn, whose slope is
the Cauchy, the reader's law of directions, with π alone in its closing constant. The scale's constant lives in the
amount; the turn's in the direction. The reader's part of the Cauchy, the heat 1/(1 + s²), has neither; it is made a
probability only by the 2/π that closes it.

What the reader holds of each identity is finite, and it is one fact: the exchange s ↔ 1/s and its corner. Of the circle,
that the heat in the v/h chart and the heat in the h/v chart, 1/(1 + s²) and s²/(1 + s²), add to one. Of Euler's
identity, that w(s)·w(1/s) = −1 for w = (1 + is)/(1 − is), at the corner i × i = −1. Of the total, that the heat below
the corner equals the heat above it: equal halves, with no π/4 in them. In the plane, π is the price of turning "equal halves" into
"how much" (essay 2 §9, "Four identities, one exchange"); in space the same price is 2, with no π in it. And the two numbers the writer puts on the sweep measure two
acts: 2/π what turning sees, the thumb's mean share, approached; π/2 what walking counts, the arc in thumbs, reached.

"The writer holds π" needs one more distinction, which the ladder of §1 already drew. A writer at work walks and counts,
and every walk actually made is a finite count, so what it holds is a fraction: 22/7, 355/113, a series stopped at some
term. It uses π's name and its approximants; it never has the value, which would take a count without end. Only the
paper holds π itself, the completed whole, every facing at once; the paper is the writer at the limit, never a writer
anyone is. So π only seems free. What the sweep gives at no cost is the relation, the corner, the equal halves, the four
facings (π/2 of relations, four times, is the circle) and the 1 that the two numbers multiply to; and that 1 needs no π,
since the two are reciprocals and any fraction held for π/2 gives it exactly, 11/7 × 7/11 = 1 (essay 2 §7).

The identity this section is about goes the same way: the −1 never needed e or π. The corner is the quarter turn,
exactly and with no root in it, (1 + i)/(1 − i) = i, and two corners composed are the half turn, i × i = −1. That is the
reader's whole holding of e^(iπ) = −1: the exchange and its corner, no numbers. A writer at work reaches −1 exactly by
those two corner steps and by no finer equal ones, since the only turns with a rational slope that are a rational part
of the whole turn are the corner's multiples (Niven); its (1 + iθ/n)ⁿ, with fractions for θ, lands near −1 and never on
it. The paper holds the limit, with the arc named π and the base named e. So e and π are the two parts of the identity
only the paper holds, and the two parts the fact does not depend on: π is the price of saying how long the arc to −1
is, e the price of saying it as one smooth turn where the reader has two corner steps.

The Cauchy's total goes the same way, and more easily. Its heat 1/(1 + s²) is rational, the corner is its median because
the two halves are equal, and it has no mean; π enters only in the constant 2/π that closes it to 1. A writer at work
has shares by count, k of K equal turns, and Gregory's and Leibniz's series for the half below the corner,
1 − 1/3 + 1/5 − 1/7 + … = π/4, each partial sum a fraction; the limit is the paper's (essay 2 §9).

It is also where the history of the two numbers met. Euler showed e irrational in 1737; Lambert showed π irrational in
1761. Liouville (1844) built the first numbers proved transcendental, made to order by an infinite series whose terms
shrink too fast for any polynomial to catch. Hermite (1873) proved e transcendental. Lindemann (1882) proved π
transcendental, and he did it through e: a power of e with an algebraic exponent other than zero is never algebraic; if
π were algebraic, so would be iπ, and e^(iπ) would be transcendental; but it is −1. The proof that π cannot be made in
finite steps goes through the point where the turn's constant and the scale's meet.

Cantor added the measure of it (1874): the algebraic numbers can be listed, one after another, and the real numbers
cannot, so almost every number is transcendental. On this essay's reading, almost every number is a completed
infinity, and the numbers anyone makes in finite steps are a thin, countable scaffolding among them.

## 6. Met, not made

None of this keeps transcendental numbers out of the reader's world. They are there, and the reader meets them.

A direction whose reading v/h is tan(1) (the turn of one radian, 1.5574…) is a direction like any other. Paint arrives
along it; the reader's hand passes it; the reader orders it among its neighbours, exactly. What the reader does not do is
make its number. It holds the direction as a pair and its place by order; the decimal 1.5574… is the writer's account of
where it is.

The same goes for sizes. A thing e times H away reads one in e, a reading the reader holds between one in two and one
in four. The reader holds it; it does not hold "e times".

This is essay 14's "met, not made" one rung higher. The reader meets irrational directions and does not make √2; it meets
transcendental directions and sizes and does not make π or e. It makes no numbers by reading: it composes, turns over,
orders and finds the corner, and the rationals are made where a count comes in, in parallax (places) and in writing.

V, the mountain in the distance, is learned the same way. An observer taking standpoints (the sixth essay, "Becoming the
writer") places V by degrees: as V\*/H in the step it took, to within a band set by its baseline and its resolution,
for the things near enough; only the step counted in an agreed unit makes it an amount, a write. Each standpoint more is one more finite step; holding V exactly, like holding π, is the end of
a process that does not end. So V is knowable, not known, and the exact V is a completed join of π's kind: the same as
the one-length circle and the completed turn, held whole from the writer's vantage, none of them moved by scaling the
world. Euler's identity is the hinge (the fifth section): the turn's end, endless in the reader's slope, is one named
point, −1, from the centre, and a named point is what can be carried onto paper (the twelfth essay's four things).

## 7. A machine is a reader here

Essay 12 asked what a computer can hold, and found that every machine operation on numbers is monotone and finite. Here
is the same thing from the ladder.

Every floating-point number is rational: an integer times a power of two. The machine's π, `Math.PI` in JavaScript, is
884279719003555/281474976710656, a fraction with 2⁴⁸ below the line, within 1.2 × 10⁻¹⁶ of π and not π. Its e is
another such fraction. A machine computing π to a billion digits is running Madhava's or Machin's series, or one of their
faster cousins, for longer; at every moment it holds a rational, the next approximant. It is doing what the reader does,
and doing it well.

What the machine can do, when asked to keep the writer's numbers, is keep them as names: a computer algebra system holds
`pi` and `E` as symbols, and proves `exp(I*pi) == -1` by rules about the symbols, not by finishing the series. It holds
the writer's statement, not the writer's number. That is the same move essay 12 recommended for the horizon: keep the
pair [0 : 1] instead of the infinite chart value. Where the infinity is a horizon or a completion, a machine is faithful
by naming it and computing with order and finite steps; it is never faithful by pretending to have finished.

**In physics.** Every bridge in physics is a write of this kind: the speed of light, a rest frequency, the astronomical
unit, a kind of star, a clock. The sort gives a law's form and leaves its values to them, and some things it does not
give at all: c's value, or why a change of frame is a hyperbolic turn and not a circular one. One open number sits here:
the proposal that the matter fraction Ω_m is 1/π. Its derivation is not accepted, since it multiplies 2/π and ½, both
the writer's, as if they were filters; but it was written down before the next surveys report, so it stands as a
registered prediction, 0.4σ from Planck and 2.4σ from DESI's second release, to be dropped if the 2027–2028 surveys put
it past 3σ (*Reads and Writes in Physics* (`../reads-and-writes-physics.md`), §4.2, §12.2).

## 8. What it changes

It changes nothing about π or e or how to compute them. It sorts.

- **π is a writer's number** because it states a turn closed, every facing joined and held whole: a completed join, not an amount,
  and unmoved when the world is scaled. Wherever π
  appears in a result (π/2 and 2/π, the Cauchy's normalizing 1/π, a circle's area), the result is the writer's measure
  of something, and the reader's own version of it is a finite statement: an order, a count, a corner.
- **e is a writer's number** because it states a scale completed, and giving a size as an amount is knowing V*. Wherever e
  or the natural logarithm appears (the lay's 1 + ln x, a decay's e^(−t/τ), the Gaussian's e^(−x²/2)), the reader's version
  is a ratio and an order: one in two, one in four, one in eight. Counting them, two thumbs, four, eight, gives places.
- **The rationals are the first count's arithmetic**, made from one length counted in finite steps, in parallax, as
  places, and in writing; a pure read needs only directions and their order. Square roots need a second length, the
  transcendentals a limit; all three are where the observer goes past a read (essay 6, "From reads to places to writes").

Essay 14 ended on five things called 1. This one ends on two numbers that are not 1 and are not the reader's, and on why:
the turn comes back, and the scale goes on, and to say either in a number you have to have held all of it.
