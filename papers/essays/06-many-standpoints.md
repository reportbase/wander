# Many standpoints

*The sixth of a series of essays on what can be known from where one stands. Draft, 28 October 2026.*

Everything so far has been read from one place. One standpoint gives directions, and directions are a great deal: the
whole circle, the Cauchy, the corner, the near and far fields. What one standpoint never gives is a place. It gives the
line a thing lies on, never where along the line.

This essay is about what a second standpoint buys, and a third, and what each loses as things recede. The answer is a
ladder. One standpoint fixes lines. Two fix the figure up to its size. One agreed length fixes the size. Along the way the
reader finds parallax, which turns out to have a corner of its own, a bell of precision around it, and a far edge past
which a place becomes a ray again.

---

## 1. One eye, then the other

Hold a finger up at arm's length and look at it with one eye, then the other. It jumps against the background. Nothing
about the finger changed; your standpoint did, by the few centimetres between your eyes. That jump is parallax, and it is
the oldest way there is of telling near from far.

With one eye closed, the finger is a direction, a line from your eye through it to whatever lies beyond. Every point on
that line looks the same. With both eyes, two lines cross at the finger, and the crossing is a place.

That is the whole of this essay in miniature. A direction is a line. Two directions from two places are a point. And the
distance between your eyes, which you have never measured, is the length every place is found in.

*Demo: parallax from the corner, mode "A alone", then "both".*

---

## 2. What one standpoint gives

A reader that does not move has the directions to everything it can see, and the steady turn lays them as the Cauchy.
That much the earlier essays established. Three more things are worth saying about one standpoint before leaving it.

**Turning in place gives no parallax.** Turn your head about the point you see from, and every direction turns by the
same angle, near and far alike. Nothing shifts against anything else. Turning gives the directions, the frame and the
corner, and no distance.

A real head is not quite a point, though. Your eye sits a few centimetres in front of the axis your head turns on, so a
turn carries the eye along a short arc, and that arc is a baseline. A head turning through a quarter turn with the eye
10 cm off its axis can place things out to about 100 m at a resolution of a milliradian. The eye alone, rotating in its
socket about a centre 7.6 mm behind its lens, reaches about 4.5 m at a resolution of one arcminute. That is real
parallax, but it comes from the eye moving, not from the turn. A pure turn about the seeing point places nothing.

**One stream is one relation.** Read over time from one place, a reading is a ratio of two amounts, a over b. Multiply
both by any changing factor and the reading does not change. So a thing coming nearer and a thing at rest growing at the
matching rate give the same stream, and so does every mixture of the two. What the stream does fix is its own pace: the
reading over its rate of change. For a thing approaching steadily, that is the time left until contact. David Lee
proposed in 1976 that drivers read exactly this when braking, without ever knowing the distance or the speed. Over time,
a standpoint is given motion before it is given an object.

**A shared denominator gives relative payloads.** Two readings taken at the same moment against the same thing, say two
objects side by side at the same distance, have a ratio in which the distance cancels. So a neighbour at the same depth
tells growth from approach: under approach the ratio of the two readings holds still, and under growth it follows the
growth. Reading at once recovers payloads relative to each other, never their scale.

*Demos: turning the head; one moon (the detections give time to contact, ω and τ).*

## 3. The ladder

Suppose each reader reads only directions: for each place on some figure, the direction in which it stands. Then there is
a ladder of what each rung buys.

- **One reader fixes, for each place, only a line through it.** However many readings it takes, it fixes no place. Laid
  out with nothing else, its readings return the circle of the second essay, the same for every figure.
- **Two readers, whose placement relative to each other is known up to scale, fix every place both can see,** except
  places in line with them both, up to one common scale. Two lines from different points meet in one point; move both
  readers by a common shape-preserving change and every meeting point moves the same way.
- **One agreed length between them fixes the scale.** That is the one amount of the first essay.

This is triangulation, read as a ladder of what each further standpoint buys. That two views fix a scene up to its scale
is the basis of reconstructing a scene from two photographs, as Christopher Longuet-Higgins showed in 1981.

The figure comes back from the second standpoint. Its size comes from an agreed length. Neither comes from looking
harder from one place.

## 4. Parallax has a corner

Two standpoints a baseline B apart, both looking at a target at distance d. The angle the baseline subtends at the
target, γ, is the parallax. How well does the pair read the target's depth?

Make it a pair in the essays' sense: the half-baseline B/2 and the distance d. Their ratio, k = d over B/2, is the pair's
reading, and the pair has a corner where k = 1, where the target is as far away as half the baseline. At the corner the
baseline is seen square: the two lines of sight meet at a right angle. The places where that happens form a circle with
the baseline as diameter, Thales' circle, which the second essay met as the arc from the chord. Parallax's corner is that
circle.

Now ask how much the depth moves per unit error in the angle. The answer is exact and simple: the relative depth error
per unit error in γ is the hyperbolic cosine of ln k.

- **It is least at the corner,** where it is 1.
- **It is the same at k and 1/k.** The target at distance d and the one at (B/2)²/d are read equally well. The swap is
  the corner's symmetry again.
- **It grows on both sides, and far off it nearly doubles per level.** At 0, 1, 2, 3 and 4 doublings either side of the
  corner it is 1, 1.25, 2.125, 4.06 and 8.03. Far off, the depth error is the error in the angle times d/B, the familiar
  rule that stereo error grows as the square of distance.

This bell is not a coincidence. The steady turn lays its readings densest at the corner because there the pair's facing
turns fastest per level of the pair's reading. Parallax reads depth best at the corner for the same reason. They are one
function: the rate at which the pair's facing turns.

The error has a shape too. Along the line of sight, the depth's error is least at the corner. Across the line of sight,
the error belongs to the direction, and it keeps falling with distance. Combined, the total error is least a little
beyond the corner, at √2 times the half-baseline. At the corner itself the error is round, provided the two standpoints
read equally well. Elsewhere it stretches along the line of sight by d over the half-baseline.

*Demo: parallax from the corner (the bell; round at the corner, stretched elsewhere).*

## 5. The band, and its far edge

Read the angle to a step ε, the smallest change the pair can tell. The relative depth error stays below 1 for a band of
about log₂(2/ε) doublings on each side of the corner. At ε = 10⁻³, that is 10.97 doublings each side.

- **The far edge is near d = B/ε,** where the whole parallax is one step.
- **The near edge is its mirror,** εB/4.
- **Past the far edge a place is a ray.** The shift between the two views is less than one step, and the pair reads one
  direction, as a single standpoint does. Parallax needs situated reading, and past its band it returns to it.

The baseline works like a second zoom. Choosing B slides the band along the doublings, as choosing H slides the reader's
window in the fourth essay. The band's width in doublings is set by the step alone.

At published precisions:

| reader | baseline | step in angle | corner | far edge |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| two eyes | 6.5 cm | about 20 arcseconds | 3.25 cm | about 650 m |
| Earth's orbit, Hipparcos | 2 AU | 2 milliarcseconds | 1 AU | about 1 kiloparsec |
| Earth's orbit, Gaia | 2 AU | 40 microarcseconds | 1 AU | about 50 kiloparsecs |

Two things follow. The eyes' corner, 3 cm off, is nearer than they can focus, so binocular depth is only ever used on the
far side of its bell. And every star is many doublings past the orbit's corner: one parsec is 17.7 doublings beyond it.
Stellar parallax lives in the bell's far tail.

## 6. Working back from what astronomy already knows

Parallax is old, and astronomy has settled a set of practices around it. Read backward, each one forces the next, and
the chain ends at the essays' geometry.

1. **The distance is the half-baseline over the tangent of the parallax.** That is the ratio of two amounts: parallax is
   the reading of the pair, and its corner is where the target is one half-baseline away.
2. **The half-baseline was measured from outside.** For two centuries the astronomical unit was found by means other
   than parallax: transits of Venus, then radar. Since 2012 it is fixed by agreement at 149,597,870,700 metres exactly.
   That is the one amount carried in, and its history is the first essay's distinction between reading and writing
   played out over two hundred years.
3. **The relative distance error equals the relative parallax error.** That rule is the far-field limit of an exact
   ratio, γ/sin γ, which is the second essay's π/2 reading of the turn γ. It is exactly 1 far off, π/2 on Thales' circle,
   and grows without bound toward the baseline. Its reciprocal is the 2/π reading, and their product is 1.
4. **An intersection is best when its rays cross square.** That is the corner.
5. **Do not invert a noisy parallax.** Astronomers learned, from Thomas Lutz and Douglas Kelker in 1973 and many since,
   that once a parallax's error is more than about a fifth of the parallax, a distance must be inferred with care, not
   read as one over the parallax. The geometry says why. The distance is the reciprocal of the reading. A reading whose
   error has any weight near zero gives distances with a long tail and no mean, while the median passes straight through
   the reciprocal. Order is kept; sums are lost. It is the third essay's rule, now on the far side of a reciprocal: never
   average the inverted parallaxes; take the median, or infer.

What the chain starts from is small. One reading, the tangent of the parallax. Two standpoints that read alike. And one
amount from outside. The second is this pair's version of reading both parties at one length; the third is the one
number a situated reader lacks.

## 7. Far off: shape first, then place, then direction

A thing's shape, its outline about its own centre, needs directions from all round it. From outside, those come only from
standpoints spread round it, each giving one direction from its centre. Seen from the thing, the standpoints span its
parallax angle, about B/d.

As the thing recedes, three things are lost in order.

- **The shape goes first.** It needs a good share of the whole turn round the thing, and B/d soon falls short of that.
- **The place goes next.** It needs only two directions that differ by more than the resolution ε, so it lasts out to
  about d = B/ε.
- **The direction is left.** Past that, every standpoint reads the thing along one line.

Near, a thing gives shape and place. Farther, place without shape. Farthest, direction alone. Each loss is set by a ratio,
of the baseline or the thing to the distance, not by any amount.

Robotics meets the same order from the other side. A single moving camera recovers a scene and its own path only up to
one common scale, which stays undetermined unless a known length is supplied. Javier Civera, Andrew Davison and José
Montiel wrote in 2008 that a feature showing no parallax "cannot be used for estimating camera translation but is a
perfect bearing reference for estimating rotation". Such features are kept as a direction, with a depth that may be
infinite, until the motion gives them a place. Joining every direction from every standpoint into one figure is what
photogrammetry calls bundle adjustment.

*Demo: walking the room (a tree through the window, 2.5 m to 10 km out: both sweeps, parallax only, a ray).*

## 8. From one place: the outline is free, the face is carried in

A standpoint that does not move still sees a thing from many sides if the thing crosses its view. Each look gives the
directions that meet the thing. The two at its edges are lines that touch it. Over the crossing these lines close in from
every side the line of sight turned through, and what lies within them at every look is the thing's *visual hull*, as
Aldo Laurentini named it in 1994.

The hull contains the thing. It meets the surface wherever some edge touched it and elsewhere only spans it: a hollow no
edge reached is bridged over. It is built of directions alone, so it is the outline up to the common scale, and nothing
in it moves when the world is scaled.

The face, the surface between the edges, needs more: how far the surface lies along each direction, an amount in a unit.
A range gives it, the time for a signal to go out and come back, times the signal's speed. That speed is an amount
measured beforehand and carried in. So ranging is writing. With the ranges, every point met is held in the speed's unit,
and with it the face, the thing's distance and its speed.

From one place, then, the addresses, their paint and the outline come free. The face needs an amount along each
direction, written, or further standpoints.

*Demos: the face from home (ranges against silhouettes); one moon.*

## 9. Inside a room

A room is the place all of this is easiest to see.

From one corner the things in the room are directions, the room's layout, with no amount in it. Each direction is painted
by the thing it meets. Moving a thing changes its direction and not its shape; reshaping it changes its shape and not its
direction. Its size in the room's own units comes from neither, but from a shared denominator: the thing and the wall side
by side at one distance give the one against the other, and the distance cancels.

Take one step and the room becomes a map, every corner placed and every thing sized, in steps. That was the first essay's
opening scene. Walk round a thing and its shape fills in, direction by direction from its centre.

Then the first essay's distinction returns in a practical form. **To move the furniture is to write.** A reader that wants
a chair a metre from the wall, or a picture at eye level, wants a thing to be at an amount, and no reading holds that. It
must become a writer, and writing needs what reading never does: the reader must move and engage with the room
physically. A known step, a hand laid flat against the wall, a tape measure. One amount carried in writes every length of
the room in its unit, and only then can the place wanted be named. Reading the room again afterwards is free.

*Demos: the room; walking the room; the room in ratios; one blueprint (write the room).*

## 10. Below the resolution: the centroid first

What does a reader get from things too close together to tell apart?

Suppose several payloads lie closer together than the reader's resolution. The blurred reading has exactly the right
total and exactly the right centroid, the weighted average position, however close the payloads are. Their arrangement is
not lost entirely. It enters the spread, smaller than the blur's own by the square of the separation over the resolution,
and the higher moments, fainter still. So reading a close group as one payload at its centroid is an approximation good to
second order. It is exact only in the total and the centroid.

So there are two centres, and they must not be confused.

- **The addresses' centre is the median,** an order statistic, which a mean cannot replace, and which a reader has only by
  holding its readings together.
- **The unresolved payload's centre is the centroid,** a weighted mean. It arrives first and exactly.

Fingers order; colours add.

## 11. Closing the loop

The first essay showed that a chain of readings cannot find its own errors and a second route can. With many standpoints
that becomes the main tool. A network of triangles, each corner read from several places, closes on itself again and
again. Each closure that fails says something is misread; where several fail together, they point to the link.

None of this computes anything in the reader. The projection through each standpoint divides. That directions compose,
and that routes close or fail to, is the geometry's. What the reader contributes is position: the places it chooses to
stand, and the one amount it is handed.

*Demo: the reader is a slide rule (a misread link, a loop that will not close).*

---

## 12. What it comes to

**Reading and writing.** Standpoints are how a reader climbs from directions to places without ever writing. One gives
lines, two give the figure, and the figure is exact, in the reader's own length. Writing enters at exactly one rung: the
agreed length that fixes the scale. Astronomy's history of the astronomical unit is that rung, measured from outside for
two centuries and finally agreed. Everything else in the cosmic distance scale is reading.

**The Cauchy.** It appears twice here. Parallax has the corner and the bell of the steady turn, for the same reason: the
pair's facing turns fastest at the corner. And on the far side of the reciprocal, inverted parallaxes behave exactly as
the third essay said directions do. Their median survives the inversion and their mean does not exist. Astronomy's hard-won
practice of never averaging inverted parallaxes is the address rule, met by experience.

Back to the finger at arm's length. One eye gives a line. Two eyes give a place. The gap between your eyes, never measured,
is the length every place is found in.
