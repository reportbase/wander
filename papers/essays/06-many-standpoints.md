# Many standpoints

*The sixth of a series of essays on what can be known from where one stands. Draft, 28 October 2026.*

Everything so far has been read from one place. One standpoint gives directions, and directions are a great deal: the
circle's whole geometry with no circle held, the Cauchy, the corner, the near and far fields. What one standpoint never
gives is a place. It gives the line a thing lies on, never where along the line.

This essay is about what a second standpoint buys, and a third, and what each loses as things recede. The answer is a
ladder. One standpoint fixes lines. Two fix the figure up to its size. One agreed length fixes the size. Along the way the
reader finds parallax, which turns out to have a corner of its own, a bell of precision around it, and a far edge past
which a place becomes a ray again.

---

## 1. One eye, then the other

Hold a finger up at arm's length and look at it with one eye, then the other. It jumps against the background. Nothing
about the finger changed; your standpoint did, by the few centimetres between your eyes. That jump is parallax, and it is
the oldest way there is of telling near from far.

With one eye closed, the finger is a direction, a line from your eye through it to whatever lies beyond. Every point on
that line looks the same. With both eyes, two lines cross at the finger, and the crossing is a place.

That is the whole of this essay in miniature. A direction is a line. Two directions from two places are a point. And the
distance between your eyes, which you have never measured, is the length every place is found in.

*Demo: parallax from the corner, mode "A alone", then "both".*

## 2. What one standpoint gives

A reader that does not move has the directions to everything it can see, and the steady turn lays them as the Cauchy.
That much the earlier essays established. Three more things are worth saying about one standpoint before leaving it.

**Turning in place gives no parallax.** Turn about the point you see from, and every direction turns by the same
angle, near and far alike. Nothing shifts against anything else. Turning gives the directions, the frame and the corner,
and no distance, and it gives them the same in every world: a reader that only turns learns its own directions and
their heat, and nothing about what is out there. What meets the fingers changes as it turns, but a colour carries no place.

A step along a straight line is the other motion a standpoint has, and the first whose effect depends on the world: near
things cross the fingers faster than far ones. Any motion of a standpoint is a turn and a step together. A turn about any
point other than the standpoint carries the standpoint along a short arc, and that arc is a step; whatever it places, it
places by the step, not by the turn. So the two motions divide the work: turning teaches the reader its directions and
their heat, and walking teaches it the world.

**One stream is one relation.** Read over time from one place, a reading is a ratio of two amounts, a over b. Multiply
both by any changing factor and the reading does not change. So a thing coming nearer and a thing at rest growing at the
matching rate give the same stream, and so does every mixture of the two. What the stream does fix is its own pace: the
reading x over its rate of change, x/ẋ. For a thing approaching steadily, that is the time left until contact: its breadth
cancels, and so do its distance and its speed. David Lee proposed in 1976 that drivers use exactly this when braking,
and found a rule on it: brake so that the time to contact shrinks no faster than half a tick each tick,
τ̇ ≥ −½, and the car stops at the thing, since at τ̇ = −½ the distance it needs to stop is the distance it has. Over time,
a standpoint is given motion before it is given an object.

Three limits keep this honest. A read holds no time and holds only where the standpoint is now, so a stream, and
everything said along it, is reads joined, not a read. What the joining gives has to be named before it is classed.
The number had from two reads a tick apart is exactly the distance over the step between them, d₁/B: a ratio of two
distances, as a distance in thumbs is. Calling that number a time adds a premise no read supplies, that the steps to
come go on like the one taken; so the time to contact is not listed among the places, and its kind as a time is left
open. The braking rule, ticks per tick, is unmoved by any clock and by scaling the world, and it is no read either,
since no single read holds a rate of change. Only seconds make a write: a unit carried in from a clock. The rate at which the standpoint turns cancels from every reading,
as the thumb's length does. And a speed in metres a second is a writing twice over: a metre and a second, both carried in.

**A shared denominator gives relative payloads.** Two readings taken at the same moment against the same thing, say two
objects side by side at the same distance, have a ratio in which the distance cancels. So a neighbour at the same depth
tells growth from approach: under approach the ratio of the two readings holds still, and under growth it follows the
growth. Reading at once recovers payloads relative to each other, never their scale.

*Demos: turning the head; one moon (the detections give time to contact, ω and τ).*

## 3. The ladder

Suppose each reader reads only directions: for each place on some figure, the direction in which it stands. Then there is
a ladder of what each rung buys.

- **One reader fixes, for each place, only a line through it.** However many readings it takes, it fixes no place. Laid out
  with nothing else, its readings return the second essay's directions, the Cauchy, the same for every figure.
- **Two readers, whose placement relative to each other is known up to scale, fix every place both can see,** except
  places in line with them both, up to one common scale. Two lines from different points meet in one point; move both
  readers by a common shape-preserving change and every meeting point moves the same way.
- **One agreed length between them fixes the scale.** That is the one amount of the first essay.

This is triangulation, read as a ladder of what each further standpoint buys. That two views fix a scene up to its scale
is the basis of reconstructing a scene from two photographs, as Christopher Longuet-Higgins showed in 1981.

The figure comes back from the second standpoint. Its size comes from an agreed length. Neither comes from looking
harder from one place.

## 4. Parallax has a corner

Two standpoints a baseline B apart, both looking at a target at distance d. The angle the baseline subtends at the
target, γ, is the parallax. How well does the pair read the target's depth?

Make it a pair in the essays' sense: the half-baseline B/2 and the distance d. Their ratio, k = d over B/2, is the pair's
reading, and the pair has a corner where k = 1, where the target is as far away as half the baseline. At the corner the
baseline is seen square: the two lines of sight meet at a right angle. The places where that happens form a circle with
the baseline as diameter, Thales' circle, which the second essay met as the arc from the chord. Parallax's corner is that
circle.

Now ask how much the depth moves per unit error in the angle. The answer is exact and simple: the relative depth error
per unit error in γ is the hyperbolic cosine of ln k.

- **It is least at the corner,** where it is 1.
- **It is the same at k and 1/k.** The target at distance d and the one at (B/2)²/d are read equally well. The swap is
  the corner's symmetry again.
- **It grows on both sides, and far off it is nearly twice as large each time the target is twice as far.** With the target
  1, 2, 4, 8 and 16 half-baselines out, or a half, a quarter, an eighth and a sixteenth of one, it is 1, 1.25, 2.125, 4.06
  and 8.03. Far off, the depth error is the error in the angle times d/B, the familiar rule that stereo error grows as the
  square of distance.

This bell is not a coincidence. The steady turn lays its readings densest at the corner because there the pair's facing
turns fastest per unit of the logarithm of the pair's reading. Parallax reads depth best at the corner for the same reason.
They are one function: the rate at which the pair's facing turns.

The error has a shape too. Along the line of sight, the depth's error is least at the corner. Across the line of sight,
the error belongs to the direction, and it keeps falling with distance. Combined, the total error is least a little
beyond the corner, at √2 times the half-baseline. At the corner itself the error is round, provided the two standpoints
read equally well. Elsewhere it stretches along the line of sight by d over the half-baseline.

### A sum of two reads

There is a tighter way to say what parallax does. Let each standpoint read the target against the baseline in its h/v
chart, the cotangent of its angle: cot a from one end, cot b from the other. Then

  cot a + cot b = B / d.

Each read is taken inward, toward the other standpoint, so both are positive for a target between the two feet; in
slopes off the baseline they are 1/s₁ and 1/s₂, and the angles a and b are only the writer's names for the two
directions. The depth is the reciprocal of a sum of two reads. Those are exactly the operations the third essay found the Cauchy
closed under: a sum, which is a relay of standpoints, and a reciprocal, which is the exchange. So parallax never takes a
read out of the family.

That makes the errors exact. Suppose each read is uncertain the way a read from a height γ is, the steady turn's law
about the true direction, and the two reads' errors are independent. In the cot chart each read is then a Cauchy with
its point at w = (c + iγ)/(1 − iγc), c being the true value. The depth is then exactly a Cauchy, at every γ and not only
for small errors, and its point is the same triangulation done on the two points instead of the two values:
D = B/conj(w_a + w_b). The rays that fail to meet ahead are part of the same law, as negative depths. At the corner the
depth's point stays at the true distance, turned by twice the read's error: D = d·(1 + iγ)/(1 − iγ) (the writer's d·e^(2i·atan γ)). Its median
falls a little short, at d·(1 − γ²)/(1 + γ²) (the writer's d·cos(2·atan γ)).

Two things leave the family, and each can be seen in a line. The place across the line of sight is a ratio of two sums,
a Cauchy only for small errors: its density right over each standpoint is infinite, because it multiplies a read's
density at zero by the sum's missing mean, and a Cauchy's density is never infinite. And if each standpoint turns only a
quarter turn, its reads are half-Cauchys, which do not add to one: their sum's density falls to nothing at zero, where a
half-Cauchy's never does, so the depth's far tail thins as 1/d³ instead of 1/d², and that depth has a mean. Turned over
the whole half turn, two standpoints' crossings are a standard Cauchy in half-baselines: half meet ahead, at a median
depth of one half-baseline, the corner.

Repeating the reads shows the order again. Averaging the reciprocal depths gains nothing, since a sum of Cauchys is a
Cauchy of the same scale; the median of the depths narrows. Parallax is the Cauchy's own composition carried across two
standpoints, and the baseline is the one amount in it.

**The place never needed π.** State parallax in slopes and no angle is left in it. Two standpoints a counted step B
apart each read the target's slope off the line between them, s₁ and s₂, measured inward; the depth d off that line is
given by 1/(d/B) = 1/s₁ + 1/s₂. Slopes 3 and 6 put the target exactly 2 steps off. A writer at work, who counts the step
and joins the two reads, has the place in steps exactly, in fractions; neither read alone holds any of it. The corner
circle is the two slopes reciprocal, s₁·s₂ = 1, known without counting the step: above 1 the target is inside, below 1
outside. On it d/B = s/(1 + s²): slopes 2 and ½ give the point (1/5, 2/5), a 3-4-5 triangle. The error's curve is
rational as well, (k + 1/k)/2, the same at k and 1/k; cosh(ln k) is the writer's smooth name for it. π comes in only
where a slope is turned into an angle: the parallax angle in arcseconds, the two angles that sum to π, the π/2 at the
corner, the parsec's 648000/π astronomical units. The depth is rational, the range along a ray a square root, the
angle a limit. What never needed π is the place in steps; the step in metres is still the one amount carried in.

*Demo: parallax from the corner (the bell; round at the corner, stretched elsewhere).*

## 5. The band, and its far edge

Read the direction to a step ε, the smallest change the pair can tell (an angle, in the writer's units). The relative depth error stays below 1 for a band
reaching a factor of about 2/ε on each side of the corner, out to 2/ε half-baselines and in to ε/2 of one. At ε = 10⁻³,
that is a factor of 2,000 each side (2 to the power 10.97).

- **The far edge is near d = B/ε,** where the whole parallax is one step.
- **The near edge is its mirror,** εB/4.
- **Past the far edge a place is a ray.** The shift between the two views is less than one step, and the pair reads one
  direction, as a single standpoint does. Parallax needs situated reading, and past its band it returns to it.

The baseline works like a second zoom. Choosing B slides the band along the distances, as choosing H, the size of the
reader's thumb, slides its window in the fourth essay. The band's width, as a factor, is set by the step alone.

At published precisions:

| reader | baseline | step (as an angle) | corner | far edge |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| two eyes | 6.5 cm | about 20 arcseconds | 3.25 cm | about 650 m |
| Earth's orbit, Hipparcos | 2 AU | 2 milliarcseconds | 1 AU | about 1 kiloparsec |
| Earth's orbit, Gaia | 2 AU | 40 microarcseconds | 1 AU | about 50 kiloparsecs |

Two things follow. The eyes' corner, 3 cm off, is nearer than they can focus, so binocular depth is only ever used on the
far side of its bell. And every star is far past the orbit's corner: one parsec is 206,265 times as far as it. Stellar
parallax lives in the bell's far tail.

## 6. Working back from what astronomy already knows

Parallax is old, and astronomy has settled a set of practices around it. Read backward, each one forces the next, and
the chain ends at the essays' geometry.

1. **The distance is the half-baseline over the tangent of the parallax.** That is the ratio of two amounts: parallax is
   the reading of the pair, and its corner is where the target is one half-baseline away.
2. **The half-baseline was measured from outside.** For two centuries the astronomical unit was found by means other
   than parallax: transits of Venus, then radar. Since 2012 it is fixed by agreement at 149,597,870,700 metres exactly.
   That is the one amount carried in, and its history is the first essay's distinction between reading and writing
   played out over two hundred years.
3. **The relative distance error equals the relative parallax error.** That rule is the far-field limit of an exact
   ratio, γ/sin γ, which is the second essay's π/2, the writer's measure of the turn γ. It is exactly 1 far off, π/2 on Thales' circle,
   and grows without bound toward the baseline. Its reciprocal is the 2/π measure, and their product is 1.
4. **An intersection is best when its rays cross square.** That is the corner.
5. **Do not invert a noisy parallax.** Astronomers learned, from Thomas Lutz and Douglas Kelker in 1973 and many since,
   that once a parallax's error is more than about a fifth of the parallax, a distance must be inferred with care, not
   read as one over the parallax. The geometry says why. The distance is the reciprocal of the reading. A reading whose
   error has any weight near zero gives distances with a long tail and no mean, while the median passes straight through
   the reciprocal. Order is kept; sums are lost. It is the third essay's rule, now on the far side of a reciprocal: never
   average the inverted parallaxes; take the median, or infer.

What the chain starts from is small. One reading, the tangent of the parallax. Two standpoints that read alike. And one
amount from outside. The second is this pair's version of reading both parties at one length; the third is the one
number a situated reader lacks.

## 7. Far off: shape first, then place, then direction

A thing's shape, its outline about its own centre, needs directions from all round it. From outside, those come only from
standpoints spread round it, each giving one direction from its centre. Seen from the thing, the standpoints span its
parallax angle, about B/d.

As the thing recedes, three things are lost in order.

- **The shape goes first.** It needs a good share of the whole turn round the thing, and B/d soon falls short of that.
- **The place goes next.** It needs only two directions that differ by more than the resolution ε, so it lasts out to
  about d = B/ε.
- **The direction is left.** Past that, every standpoint reads the thing along one line.

Near, a thing gives shape and place. Farther, place without shape. Farthest, direction alone. Each loss is set by a ratio,
of the baseline or the thing to the distance, not by any amount.

Robotics meets the same order from the other side. A single moving camera recovers a scene and its own path only up to
one common scale, which stays undetermined unless a known length is supplied. Javier Civera, Andrew Davison and José
Montiel wrote in 2008 that a feature showing no parallax "cannot be used for estimating camera translation but is a
perfect bearing reference for estimating rotation". Such features are kept as a direction, with a depth that may be
infinite, until the motion gives them a place. Joining every direction from every standpoint into one figure is what
photogrammetry calls bundle adjustment.

*Demo: walking the room (a tree through the window, 2.5 m to 10 km out: both sweeps, parallax only, a ray).*

## 8. From one place: the outline is free, the face is carried in

A standpoint that does not move still sees a thing from many sides if the thing crosses its view. Each look gives the
directions that meet the thing. The two at its edges are lines that touch it. Over the crossing these lines close in from
every side the line of sight turned through, and what lies within them at every look is the thing's *visual hull*, as
Aldo Laurentini named it in 1994.

The hull contains the thing. It meets the surface wherever some edge touched it and elsewhere only spans it: a hollow no
edge reached is bridged over. It is built of directions alone, so it is the outline up to the common scale, and nothing
in it moves when the world is scaled.

The face, the surface between the edges, needs more: how far the surface lies along each direction, an amount in a unit.
A range gives it, the time for a signal to go out and come back, times the signal's speed. That speed is an amount
measured beforehand and carried in. So ranging is writing. With the ranges, every point met is held in the speed's unit,
and with it the face, the thing's distance and its speed.

From one place, then, the directions, their heat, their paint and the outline come free. The face needs an amount along each
direction, written, or further standpoints.

Say it plainly: **the reader never sees a shape.** It sees the sweep, the same directions with the
same heat for every world, and the paint on them. *The heat is given; the paint is met.* A thing is to it the directions
it paints and what it paints them with: a patch of directions, what is in front of what, a colour. How far the surface lies along each direction is an amount, and the geometry has removed every amount
before anything is read. Every way to the shape needs more than one read: further standpoints give it as places, in the
step's length; ranging and a blueprint handed over give it in a unit, as writes. Shape is never the read's. The reader has the Cauchy sweep and its paint.

That an image does not settle the shape that made it is an old observation: Helmholtz's perception as unconscious
inference, inverse optics as an ill-posed problem (Poggio, Torre and Koch 1985), Gibson's optic array. Those accounts
treat the reader's side as a shortfall, too little information, to be made up by inference. Here the reader's side is
complete on its own terms. Its geometry is fixed before anything arrives, the same whatever shape is out there, and its
law is the Cauchy. The shape was never the reader's to lack.

Holding an outline as one radius per direction, r(θ) on the unit circle, is classical. It is the outline's radial
function, its polar coordinates. So is describing it by the Fourier series of that function, or of its tangent angle along
the outline, made blind to size, position and turn by normalising (Fourier descriptors; Zahn and Roskies 1972), and so is
taking a shape as what is left when position, scale and rotation are removed (Kendall 1984). For a convex body the
support function plays the same part, and the areas carried by its outward normals fix it up to translation (the
Minkowski problem, 1903; in vision, Horn's extended Gaussian image, 1984). Planets and asteroids are described by series
of spherical harmonics in the radius.

What the reading adds is where each part of that function comes from. The directions, with their heat, are the same for every
shape. The radius along each is an amount, the payload, had only by ranging, which is writing, or from further standpoints.
Write the outline as ln r = a₀ + f(θ): the constant a₀ is the one term no ratio of radii reads, so the normalisation the
descriptors make by choice is here what reading leaves out. The terms of f, taken in order, are detail, the broad form
first. The classical treatments begin with r(θ) in hand; the reading says what it cost to get it.

*Demos: the face from home (ranges against silhouettes); one moon.*

## 9. Inside a room

A room is the place all of this is easiest to see.

From one corner the things in the room are directions, the room's layout, with no amount in it. Each direction is painted
by the thing it meets. Moving a thing changes its direction and not its shape; reshaping it changes its shape and not its
direction. Its size in the room's own units comes from neither, but from a shared denominator: the thing and the wall side
by side at one distance give the one against the other, and the distance cancels.

Take one step and the room becomes a map, every corner placed and every thing sized, in steps: places, unmoved when the
room and the step are scaled together. That was the first essay's opening scene. Walk round a thing and its shape fills in, direction by direction from its centre.

Then the first essay's distinction returns in a practical form. **To move the furniture is to write.** A reader that wants
a chair a metre from the wall, or a picture at eye level, wants a thing to be at an amount, and no reading holds that. It
must become a writer, and writing needs what reading never does: the reader must move and engage with the room
physically. A known step, a hand laid flat against the wall, a tape measure. One amount carried in writes every length of
the room in its unit, and only then can the place wanted be named. (Placing the chair three strides from the wall needs
no unit: that is a place. The metre is what makes it a write.) Reading the room again afterwards is free.

*Demos: the room; walking the room; the room in ratios; one blueprint (write the room).*

## 10. Below the resolution: the centroid first

What does a reader get from things too close together to tell apart?

Suppose several payloads lie closer together than the reader's resolution. The blurred reading has exactly the right
total and exactly the right centroid, the weighted average position, however close the payloads are. Their arrangement is
not lost entirely. It enters the spread, smaller than the blur's own by the square of the separation over the resolution,
and the higher moments, fainter still. So reading a close group as one payload at its centroid is an approximation good to
second order. It is exact only in the total and the centroid.

So there are two centres, and they must not be confused.

- **The directions' centre is the median,** an order statistic, which a mean cannot replace, and which a reader has only by
  holding its readings together.
- **The unresolved payload's centre is the centroid,** a weighted mean. It arrives first and exactly.

Fingers order; colours add.

## 11. Closing the loop

The first essay showed that a chain of readings cannot find its own errors and a second route can. With many standpoints
that becomes the main tool. A network of triangles, each corner read from several places, closes on itself again and
again. Each closure that fails says something is misread; where several fail together, they point to the link.

None of this computes anything in the reader. The projection through each standpoint divides. That directions compose,
and that routes close or fail to, is the geometry's. What the reader contributes is position: the places it chooses to
stand, and the one amount it is handed.

*Demo: the reader is a slide rule (a misread link, a loop that will not close).*

## 12. From reads to places to writes

Climb the ladder of this essay once more, and count the variables. From one standpoint there is one length, H, the thumb,
and a direction; nothing in it is a place, nothing is counted, and the reader has a vantage, not a shape (counting comes
with parallax, and one length counted makes the rationals: the fourteenth essay). A
second standpoint adds a second variable, the step between the two. Now each point, matched in both looks, is two rays
crossing, and its place comes out of the crossing's lengths and angles, sines and square roots, numbers no single length
makes. More standpoints give every place seen, in the step's length: the writer's box, its three number lines, up to its
size. Count the step in an agreed unit and the box is in that unit.

So parallax is how a reader reconstructs the writer's cube, up to its size: the passage from a read to a place. It has
moved, joined its standpoints and is holding what no one standpoint holds, every place in its own stride, and scaling the
world with the stride moves none of it. Only when the stride is counted in an agreed unit does it write. When it stops and
looks again, it reads. Reads, places and writes are not three kinds of observer; they are what one observer is doing.

The first essay's survey says the same thing in its own words: the many directions are read (the instrument writes each
down in degrees), with the base as the thumb every side is a place, and the one base counted in a unit is the write; a
surveyor does all three in one morning.

And the passage has degrees. Knowing V, the mountain in the distance, is not a switch thrown by the second standpoint; it
comes in amounts, three ways. *In what:* in the step taken, in H if the step was H (one thumb, and places come out in
thumbs, V placed as V\*/H), in metres only if the step was counted in metres, the one write. *How well:* the fractional error in the distance, and so in V, is ε·cosh(ln k) with k = d/(B/2), ε at the
pair's corner, about ε·d/B far off, and past about B/ε nothing but a ray (the fourth and fifth sections). In the mountain
example, one thumb walked toward a range ten thumbs off puts it at k = 20, an error about ten times the step's ε; a
baseline of twenty thumbs would put it at the corner. *Of which things:* the
same two standpoints give V for the near things and leave it unknown for the far ones. More standpoints, a longer baseline,
narrow the band; nothing closes it. The writer who holds V exactly, the one the papers call the writer, is the limit of
this, never a stage of it. That is the fifteenth essay's shape again: every stage of learning V is a finite approximant, as
every partial sum of π is, and holding V exactly, like holding π, would need the infinite process finished. A reader
placing V holds it to so many digits, for so many things, out to so far. V is knowable, not known: parallax places it
without V ever being known first, and holds it exactly only at the limit. The twelfth essay's four things (the universe,
an apple, a human, a sheet of paper) put the three cases side by side: V unknowable against the universe, knowable
across one hemisphere of a thing faced, known in the hand or on the paper.

One way to speed the passage is to aim it. Set H, the thumb, to the thing itself and there are two senses. Make the thing
the unit, and its V is 1 by choice; its own two ends become the baseline, and you are the point placed: ninety degrees
across a mountain range, centred, puts you half a range-width from it, from one look (`mountain.html`, "the mountain as
H"). Or keep your H and make the thing the focus: choose the baseline so that half of it equals the distance, which puts
the thing at parallax's corner, where its error is least. You need the distance roughly first, so it goes in passes, a
short step, then a longer baseline set from what the short one gave.

**In physics.** What astronomy does with parallax is this essay's chain run backwards: d = a/tan p with the astronomical
unit carried in, σ_d/d = σ_p/p the far limit of γ/sin γ, and Lutz and Kelker's bias a read inverted as if it were a
write. A distance ladder is a tower of places, reads joined across baselines and one unit at the top, each rung one factor only where it is flat against an
independent distance; on Cosmicflows-4, Hubble's 1929 rungs are not. And in a simulation of many standpoints with one
length carried in at 1% error, the shape improved as N^(−0.49) while the scale never left the 1%: the reads averaged
down, the one write did not (*Reads and Writes in Physics* (`../reads-and-writes-physics.md`), §7).

## 13. What it comes to

**Reading, placing and writing.** Standpoints are how a reader climbs from directions to places. One gives lines, two
give the figure, exact, in the length of the step between them; the observer that joined them has placed the figure. The
last rung is the agreed length that fixes the scale, the write. Astronomy's history of
the astronomical unit is that rung, measured from outside for two centuries and finally agreed. Each standpoint's
reading is reading; joining them is placing; agreeing the unit is writing.

**The Cauchy.** It appears twice here. Parallax has the corner and the bell of the steady turn, for the same reason: the
pair's facing turns fastest at the corner. And on the far side of the reciprocal, inverted parallaxes behave exactly as
the third essay said directions do. Their median survives the inversion and their mean does not exist. Astronomy's hard-won
practice of never averaging inverted parallaxes is the rule that a Cauchy is a direction, met by experience.

Back to the finger at arm's length. One eye gives a line. Two eyes give a place. The gap between your eyes, never measured,
is the length every place is found in.
