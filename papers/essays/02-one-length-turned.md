# One length turned

*The second of a series of essays on what can be known from where one stands. Draft, 28 October 2026.*

The first essay ended on a claim: a reader with no second length has no circle, yet has the circle's geometry, because its
own single length, pointed at the mountain and turned, generates it. This essay takes that claim slowly. It asks what
pointing one length gives you, why a writer watching it turn sees a circle and not some other curve, and why there is one
special direction, at 45°. It also shows how two different ways of pivoting the same length read the same point twice, once
as a ratio and once as a square.

All of it is classical geometry, most of it in Euclid. What is new is the order in which it is read. Usually the circle
comes first, as an object held whole, and turning is something done to it afterwards. Here the pointing comes first. The
circle is what a writer draws of it; the reader holds no circle, only the geometry its pointing generates.

---

## 1. An arm held out

Hold your arm out straight in front of you, level with the ground, and raise it slowly until it points straight up.
Your arm does not change length. To anyone watching, your hand traces a quarter of a circle.

From where you stand there is no circle, only your arm pointing: one length, turning, held up at the mountains in the
distance. The circle is what a writer watching draws of it. You did not need to know how far away anything was. You did not
need a second length, a radius chosen to match something else, or a centre measured out. You needed your arm and the
ability to turn it.

At each moment of the turn your arm points somewhere. Its reach has two parts: how far it reaches forward, and how far
it reaches up. Call the forward part **h** and the upward part **v**. Both are shares of the same arm, so each lies
between 0 and 1. Straight ahead, h is all of it and v is nothing. Straight up, v is all of it and h is nothing. Halfway,
pointing up at 45°, the two are equal.

The ratio v/h is what the direction *reads*: how steeply it points. It starts at 0 straight ahead, passes 1 at 45°, and
grows without bound as the arm nears the vertical. It is a slope, and it has no unit, because both of its parts are
shares of one arm. If your arm were twice as long, every reading would be the same.

The first essay called a reader's own length **H**. Your arm is a good H: always with you, never measured, and enough to
start a geometry. The later essays picture it as the thumb held out at the end of that arm, the one length the reader holds
up against the world: H, the thumb.

---

## 2. Why a circle, and not some other curve

The claim that a turned length traces a circle sounds too obvious to need defending. It is worth defending, because the
reasons show exactly what is being assumed.

"A length that does not change" depends on how length is measured. Measure it the usual way, straight across as the crow
flies, and points at a fixed length from your shoulder lie on a circle. Measure it instead by the larger of the two
parts, forward or up, and points at a fixed "length" lie on a square: forward one, or up one, whichever is bigger. Measure
it by the sum of the parts, and points at a fixed "length" lie on a straight line from straight ahead to straight up. The
circle, the square and the line are all curves of constant length. They use different rulers.

So something must pick the circle. It turns out that turning picks it, if turning means what it naturally means.

Think of the turn as a motion made of tiny identical steps. Each step does the same thing to every direction, nudging it
round by the same small amount, and the steps repeat. After enough of them the arm is back where it started, a full turn.
A quarter of the way round, all of the forward reach has become all of the upward reach. Those are the only conditions:
the same step repeated, coming back after a full turn, forward becoming up after a quarter.

From those conditions alone, there is exactly one way of measuring length that the turn leaves unchanged, up to a single
overall factor. In that measure every direction of the arm has the same length, forward and up are at right angles, and
the arm's tip moves along the circle. The square and the line fail: no single repeated step carries the square's corners
round its sides at an even pace and comes back. The turn's own ruler is the round one.

The single overall factor left free is the arm's own length, H, the thumb. The turn fixes everything about the geometry
except how big it is drawn, and that it leaves to the reader.

This matters for what follows. The circle is not assumed and then turned. The turn is assumed, a plain repeated motion that
comes back, and the circle is what a writer watching sees it trace; the reader holds the pointing, not the curve. The only
thing still taken on trust is that the motion is linear: each step mixes forward and upward reach the same way, however far
round the arm has gone. That is what "one motion, repeated" means.

## 3. The corner

On a quarter turn there is one direction that is different from all the others: 45°, where forward and up are equal and
the reading v/h is exactly 1. The essays call it **the corner**.

It is special for a reason so simple it is easy to miss. Swap the roles of forward and up, so that you read h against v
instead of v against h. Every reading s becomes 1/s. A reading of 2 becomes a half; a reading of 10 becomes a tenth.
Every direction moves, except one. At 1, s and 1/s are the same number. The corner is the one reading the swap leaves
where it was.

From that one fact, several others follow.

- **Every turn passes through it.** Starting from 0, a reading has to reach 1 before it can pass it. However the arm is
  raised, it goes through the corner on the way.
- **It splits the turn into two halves that mirror each other.** The swap carries every reading below 1 onto one above
  1, point for point, and back. Whatever is true of the near side, readings less than 1, is true of the far side, read
  the other way up. The essays call the two sides the *near field* and the *far field*.
- **It is where a reader has to change hands.** Suppose you want to keep every reading between 0 and 1, so it fits a
  fixed scale. Below the corner, v/h does that. Above it, v/h runs off to infinity, but h/v comes back between 0 and 1.
  So a reader that keeps its readings bounded must switch from one to the other, and the only place the switch works on
  both sides is exactly at 1.
- **Approximations break there.** A great many formulas are written as series in powers of the reading: 1, s, s², s³ and
  so on. Such a series behaves well for readings below 1 and blows up for readings above it. The corner is the edge of
  where they work, and past it one rewrites them in 1/s. The corner is where expansions stop.

None of this depends on how long the arm is or how far away anything is. The corner is a fact about comparing two shares
of one length. It would be the same for a thumb, a telescope or a lighthouse beam.

## 4. Why 45°, and what the chord does not have

There is a second way to see why the corner sits at 45°, and it shows what a turned length has that a straight line does
not.

Draw a straight line from a point A to a point B. Walk along it from A to B, and at every step you can say how far along
you are: a share h of the way still to go to B, a share v already come from A, with h + v = 1. Every ratio of v to h
appears once on the walk. In that sense the line, the *chord*, already holds every reading. It holds them in the way a
writer holds them, both ends at once, with a length of 1 between.

The arc holds the same readings in a different way. Turn a length from pointing along the chord to pointing square to it,
and its tip sweeps a quarter circle that bows out from the chord. The arc passes through every ratio too, but it has
something the chord lacks: a direction it *faces* as it goes. At the start the arc runs square to the chord; at the end
it runs along it. In between it turns.

Picture your arm's quarter arc against the straight line joining its two ends, from straight ahead to straight up. At
the start the arc leaves that line leaning one way; at the end it meets it leaning the other. Halfway, at 45°, it runs
parallel to the line, leaning toward neither end, and the arm itself points square to the line. That halfway point is
the corner. There the arc stands farthest from the line, by 1 − 1/√2 of the arm's length, about 0.29.

So the corner is 45° because that is where the facing is square to the line between the two parties. A straight line has
no facing, so on it nothing marks the middle except arithmetic. On the arc the middle is the place where the turning
favours neither side.

This marks a difference of vantage that runs through the whole series.

- **The writer holds the chord.** It has both ends at once, the length 1 between them, and it faces nothing. It does not
  turn.
- **The reader faces the arc.** It does not walk along the chord. It stays where it stands and turns, and the point it
  reads crosses from one end to the other.

The chord is what the writer holds, and so is the circle the arc closes into. The reader holds neither: it points its
thumb, and the arc is what that pointing sweeps. Circles and spheres are the writer's tools. A reader has a vantage, not a
shape, and walking the shape, round it or along it, is how it becomes a writer.

## 5. The arc from the chord: two rays meeting

There is a way to get the arc without turning anything, and it is one of the oldest results in geometry.

Stand at A and look toward the far side with a slope s: up s for every one along. Stand at B and look back with the
reciprocal slope, 1/s, the swap of the first. Where the two lines of sight cross, mark a point. Do this for every s, from
0 to infinity, and the marks trace a semicircle with AB as its diameter.

The reason is Thales' theorem, which Euclid gives in Book III of the *Elements*: an angle standing on a diameter is a right
angle, and conversely, every right angle on a diameter has its corner on the circle. The two lines of sight, slope s and
slope 1/s turned the other way, always meet at a right angle. So their crossing is always on the circle.

The crossing has a tidy place. Put A at 0 and B at 1. The crossing for slope s lands at

> the point (1, s) divided by (1 + s²).

Its distance from A is h and its distance from B is v, the same two shares as before. The chord, of length 1, is the
hypotenuse of a right triangle with sides h and v, so h² + v² = 1: Pythagoras, standing on the chord.

The point also cuts the chord beneath it into two pieces, of lengths h² and v², and its height above the chord is
hv. That height is the *mean proportional* of the two pieces, the number whose square is their product, which Euclid
constructs in Book VI exactly this way. When the slope is a fraction, the point has fractional coordinates. A slope of a
half, for instance, lands at (4/5, 2/5); seen from the middle of the chord it has turned to an angle whose cosine is 3/5
and whose sine is 4/5, the 3–4–5 right triangle. Every Pythagorean triple comes out of this construction.

At the corner, s = 1, both lines of sight are at 45°, and the crossing stands directly above the middle of the chord, at
the top of the arc. The swap of s and 1/s is the mirror across the line through that top.

There is a modern name for this construction. In the nineteenth century Jakob Steiner showed that whenever the lines
through one point are paired with the lines through another by a correspondence of this kind, the crossings trace a conic:
an ellipse, a parabola or a hyperbola. A circle is the case where the pairing turns each line through a right angle, and
that needs forward and up measured at one length. If someone draws the picture on a page with the upward direction
stretched by a factor c, the same lines of sight cross on an ellipse instead. The circle is the writer's drawing of one
length pointed and turned. A page with two different lengths on its two axes gets an ellipse.

One more classical fact comes out of the construction, and it is the point of the next section. As A's line of sight
turns through an angle θ, the crossing moves round the circle by twice that angle, seen from the middle of the chord.

## 6. Two sweeps, one standpoint

That factor of two is Euclid again, in Book III: an angle at the edge of a circle is half the angle at the centre that
stands on the same arc. It is called the inscribed angle theorem, and it organises everything the reader can read.

There are two natural ways to sweep the same arc.

- **From A, a point on the circle.** A ray from A turns through θ. This is the *inscribed* sweep.
- **From the middle of the chord, the circle's centre.** A radius turns through 2θ. This is the *central* sweep.

The ray at θ and the radius at 2θ end at the same point of the arc, for every θ. One point, reached two ways.

Why only these two? A sweep is useful for reading an arc when equal steps of turning reach equal stretches of arc,
wherever the sweep points. From the centre that is obviously so: each step of turn moves the radius's end by the same
step of arc. From a point on the circle it is so too, by the inscribed angle theorem: each step of turn moves the end by
twice the step of arc, always twice. From anywhere else it fails. Stand inside the circle but off centre, and a step of
turn sweeps more arc when you look toward the centre than when you look away from it. Stand outside, and the rate runs
wild as your gaze grazes the edge. So a circle has exactly two places to sweep it evenly from: its centre, and any point
on it. There is no third.

These are not two readers, and not two motions. They are one standpoint's directions, counted about the two pivots the
geometry allows. The reader stands at A, a point the writer's circle passes through. The centre belongs to the pair, A and
B, and is neither party's place.

### What each sweep reads

The two sweeps name the same point in two different ways, and the two names are of different kinds.

**The inscribed sweep reads a ratio.** Follow A's ray out until it crosses the line that touches the circle at B. The
height where it crosses is s = v/h, the reading of section 1. As the ray turns toward the vertical, that height runs off
to infinity. The readings are unbounded. This is the sweep whose readings, taken at even steps of turning, follow the
Cauchy law: half below the corner and half above, the median at 1, and no mean.

**The central sweep reads a square.** The radius at 2θ ends at a point whose coordinates are half of (h² − v², 2hv). If
you write A's direction as a single complex number, h + iv, that point is half its square. The central sweep's point is
the inscribed sweep's direction, squared. Its coordinates never exceed the radius, so they are bounded, and every average
over an even turn exists: the across part averages to 0, and the height, measured against the radius, averages to
2/π.

The factor of two between the sweeps *is* that squaring. It is why the radius turns twice as fast as the ray. It is why
one sweep's readings are ratios, unbounded and held by their median, and the other's are squares, bounded and held by
their mean.

The same doubling is how a spin's state is drawn in physics: a state at angle θ has its point on the sphere of states at
2θ. Carry a spin round a closed loop of directions and the phase it gains from the loop alone, Berry's geometric phase, is
half the solid angle the loop encloses, the half being this doubling (the third essay, section 10).

There is a pretty corollary. In complex form, the central sweep's point is half of e raised to 2iθ. At the start, zero
turn, it sits at B. At the corner, it sits at the top of the arc, in the direction i. At the end of the quarter turn,
the horizon, the radius has made a half turn and sits back at A, home. That half turn is e to the power iπ, which equals
−1. Euler's identity is the central sweep's half turn, seen from the middle of the chord, while the reader's own ray has
made only a quarter. The e and the π are only notation here: the same squaring, written with the reading s, is (1 +
is)/(1 − is), and as s runs off to infinity that runs to −1. What the identity records is projective: the reader's two
ends, zero and the horizon, land at the two ends of one diameter. Section 9 comes back to Euler's formula as the
writer's notation for the reader's turn.

**One turn, two charts.** The even turn belongs to neither sweep. Written as the inscribed sweep's ratio, it is the
Cauchy: the long tails and the missing mean come from the ratio running off to infinity. Written as the central sweep's
point, the very same turn is spread evenly along the half circle, and it has a mean because the half circle is bounded.
Neither is a bell curve. The bell, the Gaussian, belongs to what arrives, and only as a model: if the light's strengths
along two axes vary independently and look the same however the axes are turned, together they make a round bell, and
the direction of that bell's samples is an even turn, which is why its ratio is a Cauchy. That is a fact about one kind
of paint, not about either sweep. The Cauchy is the reader's distribution, a law of directions; the Gaussian is the
writer's, a law of amounts.

The bell also shows where its two constants come from. In polar form it is a turn times a length,
(dθ/2π) × (e^(−r²/2)·r dr). The turn's part carries all the π, the 2π of the circle whole; the length's part carries the e
and totals a plain 1. A writer at work holds neither constant: it holds the binomial, counts of tosses, C(n, k)/2ⁿ, with
the mean n/2 and the variance n/4 exact, and the bell is the limit of those counts (de Moivre), π entering through
Wallis's product, every partial product a fraction. The reader holds only the direction of a pair of such amounts, the
Cauchy. So the two laws are kept differently: the Cauchy by order, its exact facts the median and the equal halves; the
Gaussian by sums, its exact facts the mean and the variance.

### Direction, landing, payload

Three things meet at that one point of the arc, and the series keeps them apart.

- **The direction** is A's ray, the reading s, with its heat. It is there before anything arrives.
- **The landing point** is where the radius ends, the point the direction points at.
- **The payload** is whatever is found there: the colour, the light, the thing.

The inscribed angle theorem guarantees the direction and the landing point meet. It says nothing about what is found.
That is the world's business. The direction and its heat are given whatever arrives, and each direction points exactly at
where its payload will land. *The heat is given; the paint is met.* The third essay is about what that separation means. Here the point is only that it is built into the circle:
the reader's directions and the world's landing points are joined, one to one, by a theorem that needs no second length,
only the reader's own length turned.

## 7. π/2 and 2/π: the writer's 1, measured twice

There is a number on each side of the chord and the arc, and the two numbers are reciprocals.

Raise the arm through its quarter turn. Its tip travels π/2 of the arm's length along the arc, while its straight reach
upward goes from nothing to all of the arm, 1. Read the turn as arc over straight reach and it comes to π/2 over the whole
quarter; read it as straight reach over arc and it comes to 2/π. The first is what a writer, holding the straight reach
and the whole quarter, measures through the central sweep; the second, through the inscribed sweep. Both have π in them,
and π is not a reader's number: from one place the reader has its readings and their corner, not these.

Neither is the straight length itself. The writer, holding it whole, has it as 1, no curve involved. Each sweep gets at
that 1 through the arc, and each gets it distorted: one too long by π/2, one too short by 2/π. Only their product
gives back the 1:

> π/2 × 2/π = 1.

This is not a coincidence of numbers. It says that the sweep, faced from where the reader stands, never gives the chord's
length directly. A writer measuring it has it twice over, once from each pivot, and the two measures together return
exactly what the writer holds in one. What they return is the reader's own length measured in itself, H in H: one thumb, in thumbs. They do not
return the size of anything else.

**They are rates, not angles, and they are the writer's.** Raise the hand from horizontal to vertical, and a writer holding
the straight line from start to end measures the sweep at one of two rates against it: π/2 from the middle of the chord,
2/π from a point of the circle. Never 1: the 1 is the writer's own rate, the angle, arc per radius read against the
completed turn, had only by joining the two sweeps, which is the writer's join. Writers have angles, readers have sweeps. And because
each of these numbers has π in it, the count of a completed turn, they are the writer's account of the sweep, not the
reader's own: the writer is transcendental, the reader is not. The reader holds one length, and one length makes only
rationals; square roots need two lengths combined, and π a limit, the whole turn. The reader holds the same sweep as the
Cauchy, its readings and their corner at 1, where v fills exactly as fast as h empties. A rate a reader reads is always of
something per something, the payload per direction: the per is read, the of is the paint, met. The fourteenth essay, "Rates, not angles", takes this up, and with it why
the tangent and arctangent, which convert a direction into a share of the whole turn and back, are the writer's tools.

**Two facings and the paper.** The two numbers sit in two facings. The central one, facing across to a rim, reaches its
end at a point: π/2 is had there. The inscribed one, facing from home toward a horizon, only approaches its end: 2/π is a
limit. Neither is the writer's. The writer's vantage is the paper, both hemispheres at once, with no facing at all, and
what the paper has is their product, the 1. A person is all three at the same time: it reads the far world from home,
faces the thing in front of it across one hemisphere, and holds as paper whatever it holds in the hand or draws. So
π/2 × 2/π = 1 is not two parties meeting; it is one observer's two facings held together, and the 1 it gives back is its
own thumb.

**Which act each number measures.** The two numbers also sort by act. 2/π is what turning sees: over the quarter turn the
thumb's share h averages 2/π, the mean of what is seen of one thumb from a standpoint that never moves, and it is
approached at the horizon, never reached. π/2 is what walking counts: the quarter arc from all of h to all of v is π/2
thumbs long, and it is reached, at a point. An arc counted in thumbs is an extent, had by walking and counting; with the
thumb in metres it is a write. So, in short, reading is 2/π and writing is π/2, the write reached through its place, and
their product, 1, is the read and the walk held together. Both stay the writer's arithmetic, since the reader holds no π;
what is sorted is the act each one measures.

**Short by reading, long by walking, and whole.** Said most simply: the true size is 1, the thumb in thumbs. Reading gives
the short side of it, 2/π; walking gives the long side, π/2; together they give back the 1. The proof is one line, the
writer's. Along the sweep each step of the arc gains v at the rate h: what is read at a place is the rate at which the
thumb is gained per step walked there. Summed over the walk the gain is all of v, 1; and a sum is a mean times a length,
(2/π) × (π/2) = 1. The 1 is no property of the circle: straight across the chord the mean gain is 1/√2 over a length √2,
along the square's two edges ½ over a length 2. Only the split is the path's, and π is what the split costs when the path
is the turn; in space the same split is ½ and 2, with no π. The 1 is the thumb, not the mountain. The mathematics is old
(Cauchy's mean projection, 1841; Crofton; Buffon's needle, 1777); the assignment of the short factor to the read and the
long one to the walk is ours. A walk straight toward a thing is the square's row: far off the thing's share fills, from
the corner in the thumb's share empties by the same step each stride, and the step times the count is 1 with no π in it
(essay 19, section 8).

**π/2 of relations and four facings make a circle.** The quarter turn is every way the vertical and the horizontal can
relate, one slope from all of h to all of v; weighed by the turn's heat and summed, those ways come to π/2. A relation is
never negative, so that is all of them. The circle is the same quarter turn four times, once for each choice of sign for
h and v, joined by the writer: π/2 × 4 = 2π. The relation is continuous; the facing is a choice of signs, a second kind of
thing and not a second dimension. In space the wedge holds π/2 of directions, three signs give eight facings, and
π/2 × 8 = 4π, the sphere.

**What is free, and what π costs.** The geometry here is prior to any physics, and free: the relation, the exchange
s ↔ 1/s and its corner, the heat's equal halves, the four facings, and the 1 that the short side and the long side give
back. Nothing is computed to have them, and no time is spent. The numbers π/2 and 2/π are not free. π is a limit: no
finite count of steps gives its value, only a fraction near it. So there are three, not two. The reader has slopes, the
corner and equal halves, and neither knows nor uses π. The writer at work walks and counts, so it holds fractions, 22/7,
355/113, each nearer π and none of them π; it uses π's name and its approximants, never its value. Only the paper holds π
itself, the completed whole, and the paper is the writer at the limit, never a writer anyone is.

And the equation never needed π. Its two factors are reciprocals, and π leaves the product:

| who | what it holds of π/2 × 2/π = 1 |
|---|---|
| the reader | s × (1/s) = 1: the exchange undoing itself, no numbers |
| the writer at work | (p/q) × (q/p) = 1: exact with any fraction, 11/7 × 7/11 as well as a better one |
| the paper | π/2 × 2/π = 1: the same fact, with the turn's split named at its true value |

The 1 is exact at every stage of approximation. π is the one part of the equation only the paper holds, and the one part
the equation does not depend on: the price of saying how the turn splits the 1, which no one pays to have the 1.

*Demo: the mountain and the thumb, "the two vantages": π/2 and 2/π on the thumb's circle, their product 1.*

## 8. Other paths, and what only the circle gives

Section 2 said that the square and the line are curves of constant length under other rulers. What happens if you
sweep them instead?

Take three paths from straight ahead to straight up: the quarter circle; the straight line h + v = 1; and the two sides of
the unit square, along the bottom and up the side. Sweep each one evenly along its own length, and ask, at each moment,
what share of the sweep has been done and what direction it points in.

At three places all three paths agree.
- **At the start,** straight ahead, nothing is done.
- **At the corner,** 45°, exactly half is done, on all three.
- **At the end,** straight up, all of it is done.

In between they differ. When the direction reaches 22.5°, halfway to the corner, the circle has done exactly a quarter of
its sweep, the line about 0.293 of its, and the square about 0.207 of its. And all three share two features: half the
sweep lies on each side of the corner, and the readings v/h have no mean. Those two features need only that the path is
the same on both sides of the corner and reaches all the way to the vertical.

What only the circle gives is the exact shape of the law: the Cauchy itself, with no other choice made. The square and the
line give other laws with the same median and the same missing mean. The circle gives the one law that a further turn
carries onto itself unchanged. That is the law of the reader's own pointing, turned: the circle's geometry with no V and no
circle held.

## 9. The circle, written by a writer

Everything so far has been from the reader's side, where there is no circle. The circle is the writer's, and it is worth
seeing how the writer uses it to describe the reader's pointing.

A writer holds both lengths, the reader's H (its thumb) and the other party's V (the mountain in the distance), and can
draw them on a page with two axes. Watching the reader point its one length and turn it, the writer draws the circle the
tip would trace, sees two axes drawn at the same length and writes that down as **V = H**: the thumb held to equal the
mountain. It is the writer's name for that circle, the one-length circle. The reader never set it. It has no V to set, and
no circle.

On a page, though, two different lengths are possible. Draw the upward axis at c times the forward axis, so V = cH with c
not equal to 1. Then nothing turns all of the forward reach into all of the upward reach without stretching on the way.
The tip moves along an ellipse, not a circle. "Sweeping evenly" no longer names one sweep: even in direction, even along
the curve and even in its parameter now give three different laws, and only one of them is the Cauchy with its median
at the corner. The corner itself, where the two amounts are equal, slides off 45°.

So where does a c other than 1 come from, if the reader never chooses it? Four places, each with a signature.

- **It is writing.** Amounts are wanted in a shared unit, and c is a real ratio of units, measured on purpose. An
  equal-area map does this deliberately.
- **It is uncalibrated.** An instrument with unequal gains, pixels that are not square, or variables left in their own
  units imposes a c. The median of the readings sits at c instead of on the corner. Standardizing puts it back.
- **An estimate has been taken for the circle.** A guess at the other party's true size belongs to the amounts. Redrawing
  the circle with it confuses a size with a stretch.
- **It does not turn at all.** A motion that is not a turn has no circle to stretch. It has a different geometry, with no
  corner at its median.

Practice has found c = 1 again and again, under other names, each field for its own reason. Statistics standardizes its
variables before comparing directions. Charts that must keep compass bearings true draw them at c = 1; that is what
Mercator's projection does. Graphs of slopes are drawn with equal axes, and cameras have square pixels. The geometry gives
the one reason underneath: c = 1 is the only two-axis drawing that has the one-length circle on it.

### The circle's equation, divided by its own length

Set the writer's circle beside the reader's law and the second turns out to be the first with its lengths divided out.
The writer's circle holds amounts: a point is (H cos θ, V sin θ), the curve (x/H)² + (y/V)² = 1, round when V = H, and
then x² + y² = r² with r an amount. Divide that by x²:

  1 + s² = (r/x)² = sec²θ,  s = y/x.

No length is left: r/x is a ratio of two amounts and not an amount. Two things follow. The density of the reader's law is
the share squared, 1/(1 + s²) = (x/r)² = h², the reader's own share of H, squared. And the even turn becomes the Cauchy:
ds = sec²θ·dθ, so dθ = ds/(1 + s²). The Cauchy is the circle's equation used to carry an even turn into readings. Its scale
is 1 because every amount has been divided out, not because anyone set V equal to H. Read as the reader has it, that
density is a heat: a continuous weight over the readings, hottest at s = 0 (on a screen held square to the look, the look itself) and cooling as cos²θ toward the
horizon, with nothing counted in it, the turn per unit of the writer's grid.

Divide the two-axis curve the same way and one thing survives: s = (V/H)·tan θ, a Cauchy of scale c = V/H. The amounts go;
their ratio stays, as the law's scale. So V enters the Cauchy in exactly one place, its scale, and only for whoever holds V.
A third form puts the law on a line in the world, the lighthouse's shore: the landings follow a Cauchy with a location and
a scale that are both amounts, the foot of the lamp and its distance, and they are had only by finding the shore and
measuring along it. The reader has the law's shape; the writer adds its parameters.

In space the same division is the whole of these essays in one line. The writer's sphere, x² + y² + z² = r², holds three
amounts and a radius; kept as written, it is the writer's. Divide it by x², the length along the look taken as the thumb,
and it becomes 1 + s² + s₂² = (r/x)², two readings and no length: the denominator of the reader's law in space, the
two-dimensional Cauchy 1/(2π(1 + s² + s₂²)^(3/2)), hottest at the look, a median at the corner, no mean, its horizon where
the readings run off. The same sphere, kept and divided: the writer's and the reader's. Scaling the world moves r and every
x and leaves every s where it was, so the first test of the first essay is the difference between the two forms. And
the sphere has a shape where the Cauchy has none: a shape needs a size and a surface that closes on itself, and dividing
by the thumb removes the size and opens the surface along the rim, 90° off the look, where x is zero and the readings run
off to the horizon. The Cauchy's long tail never completes, which is why it has no edge and no mean. What the
equation leaves out is the paint, which is the second test. Said in parts: H, V and V₂ held as amounts make the writer's
sphere (an ellipsoid, unless written equal); H, H and H, one thumb crossed with itself, make the reader's Cauchy.

### Circular mathematics without V

That raises a fair question. The circle's equation seems to need V. On a page it reads (x/H)² + (y/V)² = 1, and it is a
circle only when V equals H. If the reader never knows V, how can it do circular mathematics at all?

The answer is that the circle is the writer's either way. The equation on the page describes a drawing with two axes, each
with its own size, and that drawing needs two lengths. The circle's classical definition, everything at one distance from
a centre, needs only one, and a writer watching a length turned sees its tip trace it, as section 2 showed. The reader
holds no circle; it points its thumb, and its readings need only that one length. The turn's two parts are h and v (the
writer's cos θ and sin θ), the across and up shares of the same length. So h² + v² = 1 is a fact about the shares of one length. The v in it
is the turned length's own up share, not a share of some second party's breadth. Reading it as the second is where the
confusion comes from.

And circular mathematics was always ratio mathematics. Sine, cosine and tangent are sides over the hypotenuse. A radian
is arc over radius. π is circumference over diameter, a ratio. e^(iθ) is the unit circle, any circle divided by its own
radius. The corner, π/2 and 2/π, and the Cauchy are ratios too. None of them moves when the world is scaled. But being
unmoved by scaling is needed for a reading and is not enough. √2 combines two lengths, and π, with every number that
carries it, needs the whole turn; both are made only by an observer combining lengths or holding the turn complete, which is
the writer's vantage, not a read (the fourteenth essay). So the reader has the corner, its readings and the Cauchy's shape; sine and
cosine as numbers, the radian, π, π/2 and 2/π are the writer's, scale-free as they are. Trigonometry is the writer's
record of the reader's turn.

What the reader cannot do without an amount carried in is place a point at (x, y) in metres, state the circumference as 2π
times so many metres or the area as so many square metres, or say how large another party is beside its own thumb. Those
are amounts, and amounts are writing. The reader does every part of circular mathematics except the parts that state a
size.

### Euler's formula, the writer's notation for the reader's turn

One piece of classical notation holds all of this at once: Euler's formula, written in polar form. It is the writer's
notation: e^(iθ) takes the angle as a number, and the whole turn, π, with it (the fourteenth essay). What it records of
the reader is the split of direction from amount and the composition of readings, which in the readings themselves is
tangent addition, rational:

> z = r·e^(iθ).

**The two factors are the direction and the payload.** The factor e^(iθ) has size exactly 1. It is a direction and
nothing else, held in the reader's own length, H measured in H, the thumb in thumbs. The factor r is an amount, how
far, how bright, how big, and it means something only in a unit, which only a writer can supply. Polar form has been
splitting a number into its direction and its amount since the eighteenth century. Read from a standpoint, that split is
the reader's and the writer's.

**The logarithm makes the scale test a question of which part moved.** Take the logarithm:

> ln z = ln r + iθ.

Scale the world by k and only the real part moves, by ln k. Turn, and only the imaginary part moves. So the first test of
the series, scale the world, comes down to which part of the logarithm changed: the imaginary part is a reading;
differences of the real part are places, unmoved, had by walking; the real part's starting point, tied to a unit, the one
amount that sets every size, is the writing, and no reading fixes it. And
the reader's ignorance, which turn it faces and how large anything is, is nothing more than sliding this plane up and down
and left and right. Sliding keeps area, so the measure that suits that ignorance is plain area on the logarithm's plane:
uniform in direction, and uniform in distance read as a ratio, as much room from one in two to one in four as from one in
four to one in eight (a writer counts the same stretches as one thumb to two, four to eight).

**Composition becomes addition.** In the reader's own chart, readings taken from two zeros combine by tangent addition,
(s₁ + s₂)/(1 − s₁s₂). That is awkward, and it blows up when the two turns together reach the vertical. In Euler's form,
the writer's, the same combination is e^(iα)·e^(iβ) = e^(i(α + β)): the angles just add, and nothing is special at the vertical. The
reader's awkward arithmetic is the turn's plain arithmetic, seen through the tangent. The reader uses no trigonometry: its turns compose in the slope, and every sine, tangent and arctangent
in these essays is the writer's (*Reads and Writes* §1, "The reader uses no trigonometry"). A reading has no sign, so a turn
and the same turn plus a half turn give the same reading; the reader's line closes after half a turn of its ray, and the
squaring of section 6 turns that half turn into one whole turn of the central sweep.

All of this is classical: polar form, the complex logarithm, the area that multiplication keeps, the double cover. What is
the series' own is the reading: e^(iθ) as the direction, r as the payload, the logarithm's imaginary part the reading and
its real part the places, with the writing at its zero (*Reads and Writes* §2). It is the right notation for a reader
because it keeps them apart. The reader holds the imaginary part; places are gaps in the real one, had by walking; the
writer needs its zero.

**The Cauchy in Euler's notation.** The Cauchy itself fits this notation twice over. Write where the reader stands, the
foot and the height above the line that the third essay (section 6) gives every Cauchy, as one complex number z = m +
iγ. The Cauchy's Fourier transform, the average of e^(itX), is then Euler's exponential of that point, e^(izt): the foot
sets how fast it oscillates and the height how fast it dies away. A spectral line with a Lorentzian shape is exactly
this, a turning phasor fading out. And carry the Cauchy onto the circle, the central sweep's chart, and its averages
become the powers of one point w inside the circle: the average of Wⁿ is wⁿ. The law with no mean on the line has a mean
on the circle, and that mean is where the reader stands, carried across. This is section 6's "the missing mean is the
chart's", said in Euler's notation. For the reader's own quarter turn the mean direction of the landing point is
(2/π)·i: it points at the corner, and its length is 2/π.

The division of the last subsection is here too, with the turn kept. The writer's point x + iy = r·e^(iθ), divided by the
thumb, is the reading 1 + is = sec θ·e^(iθ): the turn whole, carrying a length. Divide once more, by the mirror reading
1 − is, and the length goes as well: (1 + is)/(1 − is) = e^(2iθ), the central sweep's landing point, with home at +1, the
corner at i and the horizon at e^(iπ) = −1.

**The Cauchy's constant comes from the same turn.** Take the logarithm of that landing point, following it continuously
from home: ln w = 2iθ, and (1/2i)·d(ln w)/ds = 1/(1 + s²). The Cauchy's kernel is how fast the arc turns per unit of
reading. Sweep from home to the horizon and w runs along the upper half of the circle from +1 to −1, gathering iπ: so
∫₀^∞ ds/(1 + s²) = π/2, and the half-Cauchy's 2/π is one over the arc gathered. Over the whole line w goes once round and
the total is π, the standard Cauchy's. Euler's identity marks where the sweep ends; the logarithm records what was
gathered; the change from angle to reading makes it the kernel. Each finite read stays rational (w = (1 − s² + 2is)/(1 + s²),
and composing reads multiplies the w's, which is tangent addition); π enters only when the sweep is totalled, the writer's
act. That last sentence, and the reader standing at i where the kernel has its pole, are our reading of the arithmetic,
not something the arithmetic proves (*Reads and Writes* §9, "The Cauchy's constant, from the same turn").
The same pole gives the Cauchy's characteristic function. From a standpoint z = m + iγ, weight the kernel by a turn
e^(its) and close the line above it: the one residue, at z, is e^(izt) = e^(imt − γt) (for t < 0 the line closes below,
and together they give e^(imt − γ|t|)). At t = 0 it is the total: 1 for the normalized kernel, π for the unnormalized
1/(1 + s²) whose π the paragraph above traced to the arc; past it the foot turns the phasor and the height damps it. One pole carries both (*Reads and Writes* §9,
"One pole, the constant and the phasor").
No other familiar kernel does this: a double pole gives (1 + t)·e^(−t), two poles beat, the hyperbolic secant's
endless poles cannot total term by term, and the Gaussian has no point off the line to read from at all.
The structure was in use before it was named: the reader standing at i, a relay of standpoints adding z, parallax's
Cauchy depth, time turning e^(izt), the floor's cells at their own m + iγ. One pole is why each could carry z and nothing
else. That is a unity shown for the Cauchy, not a law for every kernel (*Reads and Writes* §9, "What the one pole already
carried").

**The two corners are two mirrors.** The plane of the logarithm also shows the corners plainly. Swap a reading for its
reciprocal, v/h for h/v, and the angle θ becomes 90° − θ: a reflection of the imaginary part, and the line it leaves in
place is θ = 45°, the reading 1, the corner in direction. Swap a distance for its reciprocal about the reader's length, d/H (the distance
in thumbs) for H/d, and ln d becomes 2 ln H − ln d: a reflection of the real part, and the line it leaves in place is d =
H, the corner in distance (the fourth essay's corner of near and far). So the reader has two corners, one on each axis of
one plane, each a mirror line. Each is moved by sliding the plane along its own axis: turning the head slides the first
across the world's directions, and choosing H, the size of the thumb, slides the second along the distances, so many thumbs
away. That is why the two corners can be set independently: they sit on different axes.

**Drawn.** The logarithm's plane can be drawn, and `euler.html` draws it with a writer's grid on it. The grid's circles
about the eye at one, two, four and eight thumbs come out as evenly spaced upright lines; its rays as level lines; every
straight line of the grid as one and the same curve, slid sideways by how far off the line is and up or down by which way
it faces; a logarithmic spiral as a straight line. Squeeze the distance axis by the thumb, (2/π)·arctan(d/H), and it
becomes ½ + gd(x)/π, x = ln(d/H), with gd Mercator's own function: distance laid by the thumb is a turn too. A writer can
say why. Wrap the plane onto a sphere so that one thumb lands on its equator: the eye is one pole, the horizon the other,
the near field one half and the far field the other. The logarithm's plane is then that sphere's Mercator chart, the
thumb's lay its chart of plain latitude and longitude, and the swap of a distance for its reciprocal its reflection in the
equator. Turning the world turns the sphere about its axis; making the thumb larger slides every latitude toward the eye,
a boost of rapidity ln k, the moving sky of the seventh essay done to distance. Both are slides on the logarithm's plane
because the two motions commute. The sphere is the writer's: the reader has the lay.

**Where it stops.** Euler's formula is a tool for the flat case, one other party and turns in a plane. With three parties
(the fifth essay's wedge, which is the writer's description of the reader's heat map), only half of it survives. The split
survives: a point of space is still an amount times a direction, scaling the world still moves only the amount, and the
scale test still asks the same question. What fails is composition as addition. Turns in a plane commute, so their angles
add. Turns in space do not: a quarter turn about one axis and then another ends somewhere different from the same two turns
in the other order. With no fixed order there is no single angle to add, and no logarithm turns combining them into a sum. Two quarter
turns land 120° apart by order, and a small loop of turns (one way about one axis, then the other, then both back) comes
home turned by about the area it encloses, 1.741° for turns of 10°, where the plane's loop leaves nothing.
Quaternions compose turns of space, but they do not commute, and no number system of more than two dimensions does both.
Each sweep on its own, altitude or azimuth, is still a plane's turn and still adds; it is putting the two together that
does not. It can look strange that so tidy a tool stops at two dimensions, but the reason is that space has more than one
way to turn, and the ways do not take turns politely.

The Cauchy's own coincidences part at the same place. In the plane one law plays four parts: it is the uniform turn
carried by a Möbius map, it is where a random walk from the standpoint first meets the circle (harmonic measure), its
averages are powers of one point, and it is the two sweeps' rates combined. On the sphere these come apart into two
laws. One, harmonic measure, keeps the powers, so its mean is still the standpoint. The other, the boosted sky of the
seventh essay, keeps the Möbius maps, so boosts still compose, but its mean is no longer a power: at rapidity η its mean
cosine is coth η − η/sinh² η, where the plane's would be tanh(η/2). The reason is old: only in the plane do the maps that
keep angles also keep the harmonic functions. So in three dimensions "the law seen from a standpoint" has to say which
of the two it means. The thirteenth essay, *Why the plane is special*, takes this apart in full.

**A law needs no memory.** Euler's formula looks as though it accumulates, a turn built up step by step round a
circle. That is the classical telling, a journey. The function holds no journey. It is a rule: what any turn does to
any direction, given for every pair at once, with nothing in it having happened. So the geometry, which keeps nothing,
can still say what a thousand turns would do. What needs keeping is only which turns a particular observer made. In
the reader's form the rule is plainer still: w(s) × w(1/s) = −1 at every slope, a pair of directions of one read, of
which the famous identity is the one member that needs the horizon and i × i = −1 the one at the corner. That is the
same relation held two ways. The algebra joining the two forms is ordinary, and they are equivalent; which one a read
holds is this series' reading. The home and horizon member is the writer's, since no read holds the horizon, and it is
the one that carries π. "Law" here means a geometric law, a relation among one read's directions; laws in general are
many things and are not a third kind beside reading and writing.

**No dial, and the quarter turn's two numbers.** The circle of this essay is the writer's in a stronger sense than
its shape. A read is one sweep from h to v on the thumb H, with no V in it; it stops at its horizon and closes nothing,
and each read stands by itself. The circle is the kept steps laid end to end. So the numbers that belong to one sweep
are the quarter turn's, π/2 and 2/π, and they are the ones to state first; π and 2π belong to the join of several. The
arctangent is the worst of the writer's tools to lend the reader, because it eliminates the sweep: it names one place
on the turn as though it could be gone to directly, where a direction is had only within its sweep, with everything
between it and home before it. The quarter turn even reaches sums that show no turn. Carried past one read by the
writer's join, the slope meets a horizon at every odd multiple of the quarter turn, and Euler's expansion of the slope
over those horizons gives 1 + 1/9 + 1/25 + … = ½·(π/2)², from which Basel's π²/6 follows as ⅔·(π/2)².

**In physics.** The one-length circle sits in relativity in plain sight. Divide E² = (pc)² + (mc²)² by E² and it is the
unit circle of (pc, mc²) in the one unit E, c the bridge: its shares are β and 1/γ, the moving clock's rate, and v/h is
the celerity γβ. Home is E = mc², the horizon E = pc, light, and the corner pc = mc², β = 1/√2, where Newton's form and
the massless form are each other's reciprocal, both 6.07% high. A qubit is the two sweeps: its state a direction at θ,
its Bloch point at 2θ, the central sweep's point, Born's rule that point's foot h². And interference has the arc's two
numbers as a bound: visibility at most 2hv, predictability |h² − v²|, V² + P² ≤ 1, full visibility only at the corner.
All textbook; what the sort adds is where each part sits (*Reads and Writes in Physics*
(`../reads-and-writes-physics.md`), §4.2, §8.1, §8.2).

**Four equations, one.** The circle h² + v² = 1, Euler's e^(iπ) = −1, the two sweeps' π/2 × 2/π = 1 and the Cauchy's
total (2/π)·∫₀^∞ ds/(1 + s²) = 1 look alike because they are one equation read four ways. Divide the writer's circle,
x² + y² = r², by the thumb: 1 + s² = 1/h², the circle in the reader's slope. Its reciprocal, h² = 1/(1 + s²), is the
reader's heat, rational in s, with no π and no root in it. Gather that heat from home toward the horizon and it is the
logarithm of w = (1 + is)/(1 − is) over 2i; at the horizon w is −1, e^(iπ), and the heat has totalled π/2. Close it with
2/π and it is 1. In one line:

> (2/π)·∫₀^∞ h² ds = (2/π)·(1/2i)·ln(−1) = 1, with h² = 1/(1 + s²) from h² + v² = 1.

The reader holds one piece of it, the heat. The horizon reached, π, and the 2/π that makes the heat a probability are the
paper's. A reader's distribution is a heat; it becomes a total only when a writer closes it.

**Four identities, one exchange.** Each of the four also has a form the reader holds: rational in the slope, with no π,
and each one a fact about the exchange s ↔ 1/s, whose fixed point is the corner.

| | the writer's form | the reader's form | what it says of the exchange |
|---|---|---|---|
| the circle | h² + v² = 1 | 1/(1 + s²) + s²/(1 + s²) = 1 | the heat in the v/h chart and the heat in the h/v chart share one whole |
| the half turn | e^(iπ) = −1 | w(s)·w(1/s) = −1, w = (1 + is)/(1 − is) | a direction and its reciprocal compose to the half turn |
| the two facings | π/2 × 2/π = 1 | s × (1/s) = 1 | the exchange undoes itself; the corner is its fixed point |
| the total | (2/π)·∫₀^∞ ds/(1 + s²) = 1 | ∫₀¹ ds/(1 + s²) = ∫₁^∞ ds/(1 + s²) | the corner splits the heat in equal halves |

The heat per unit of s is h²; turn the reading over, t = 1/s, and the heat per unit of t is 1/(1 + t²) = v². So
Pythagoras, in squares, is the two charts' heats adding to one at every direction, with no root in it; the root comes in
only for h itself. Turn s over inside w and it becomes −1/w, so the product is −1 at every s; at the corner it is
i × i = −1, the corner composed with itself the horizon, Euler's identity with no e and no π. And putting s = 1/t carries
the heat below the corner onto the heat above it: the reader has "half and half" without ever having "π/4 and π/4".

So the reader holds one fact four ways, the exchange and its corner, and the writer's four equations are what that fact
becomes once the horizon is reached and the total stated. In the plane, π is the price of turning "equal halves" into "how much" (in space the same price is 2, §7). The
first holds at every direction, so one read has it, in squares; the other three need the horizon reached, which no read
does. Squares are the reader's where roots are the writer's: w = (h + iv)² is rational in s and h + iv is not, the same
split as doubling a turn against halving one.

**The −1 never needed e or π.** The second row can be read as §7 read the third. The corner is the quarter turn,
exactly and with no root in it: w(1) = (1 + i)/(1 − i) = i. Two corners composed are the half turn, i × i = −1, a
finite and rational statement. Euler's identity is that fact with the arc given a length, π, and the composing written
as an exponential, base e:

| who | what it holds of e^(iπ) = −1 |
|---|---|
| the reader | w(s) × w(1/s) = −1; at the corner i × i = −1: the exchange, no numbers |
| the writer at work | two corner steps land on −1 exactly; finer equal steps, (1 + iθ/n)ⁿ with fractions for θ, land near it and never on it |
| the paper | e^(iπ) = −1: the limit of the finer steps, the arc named π and the base named e |

One thing differs from π/2 × 2/π = 1, where any fraction gave the 1 exactly. Here the working writer is exact only at
the corner: the only turns with a rational slope that are a rational part of the whole turn are the corner's multiples
(Niven), so equal rational steps finer than the corner never land on −1. The corner is the one finite door to it, and
everything finer is an approach. So e and π are the two parts of the identity only the paper holds, and the two parts
the fact does not depend on: π the price of saying how long the arc to −1 is, e the price of saying it as one smooth
turn where the reader has two corner steps.

**The Cauchy hardly needed π at all.** The fourth row is the cleanest case. The heat at each slope, 1/(1 + s²), is
rational; so is the ratio of the heat at any two directions; the corner is the median because the heat below it equals
the heat above; there is no mean. π comes in at one place, the constant 2/π that makes the heat a probability per unit of
slope:

| who | what it holds of (2/π)·∫₀^∞ ds/(1 + s²) = 1 |
|---|---|
| the reader | the heat 1/(1 + s²), and equal halves either side of the corner; no total |
| the writer at work | shares by count: of K equal turns, k below a slope, k/K; fractions near 2/π for the constant |
| the paper | the total closed to 1, the constant 2/π named |

A share is a turn over a turn, so counted in turns it needs no π to be defined. The corner is again the one exact door:
the only rational slopes with a rational share are 0, 1 and the horizon, with shares 0, ½ and 1 (Niven); the quartiles
are exact too, at √2 − 1 and √2 + 1, by one square root. The working writer's sum is Gregory's and Leibniz's series for
the heat below the corner, 1 − 1/3 + 1/5 − 1/7 + … = π/4: every partial sum a fraction, only the limit π/4, and the
reader's equal halves say the whole is twice it without summing it. Across the three identities the free facts are
s × (1/s) = 1, i × i = −1 and equal halves at the corner; what only the paper holds is π, e and π, and 2/π.

**The circle needed no π, and its corner flips.** The first row is different. h² + v² = 1 has no π in it: in the
reader's form, 1/(1 + s²) + s²/(1 + s²) = 1, it is rational in the slope and holds at every direction, so one read has
it and nothing is owed for it. The cost lies elsewhere, in two grades: the shares themselves are roots,
h = 1/√(1 + s²); and how much circle there is, 2π round and π in area, appears only when the four facings are joined and
the whole measured.

| who | what it holds of h² + v² = 1 |
|---|---|
| the reader | the squares, 1/(1 + s²) + s²/(1 + s²) = 1, at every direction |
| the writer at work | exact points wherever 1 + s² is the square of a fraction: the Pythagorean triples, s = 3/4 giving h = 4/5, v = 3/5 |
| the paper | the circle whole: every point, its length 2π, its area π |

In the other three the corner was the working writer's one exact door. On the circle it is the landmark a fraction
cannot reach: home and the horizon are exact, the triples give exact points as near together as one likes, and at the
corner h = v = 1/√2. The reader has it exactly in squares, ½ and ½; the writer has it as the diagonal of the square, a
construction and not a count. So there are three grades of cost: nothing, for what is rational in the slope; a square
root, a finite construction, for the shares and for halving a turn; and a limit, for how much the whole is, π and e.

## 10. What it comes to

The two unifying ideas of the series, reading against writing and the Cauchy, meet in the circle, and this essay has
been about how.

**The circle is one length's before it is two lengths'.** A writer starts with the circle as an equation in two lengths and
turns it. A reader starts with one length, points it at the mountain and turns it, and holds no circle: the circle is what
a writer draws of the turning. From one place the reader has the open quarter's directions, its readings; the circle
closed, with its π, is held by an observer that has joined its facings, the writer's vantage, and the same observer reads again
when it stands still (the sixth essay, "From reads to places to writes"; the fourteenth). The writer's equation describes, in two
lengths, a thing one length made.

**Everything special about the circle is available from one length pointed, with no circle held.** That includes the corner
at 45°, its two mirrored halves, the right angle on the chord, and the inscribed sweep's ratios and the central sweep's
squares. So is the writer's measure of the two sweeps, π/2 and 2/π, whose product returns the reader's own length. None of
it needs a second length or the size of anything.

**The Cauchy is the circle read from its edge.** The inscribed sweep, from the reader's own point, where the writer's
circle passes, reads directions as ratios, unbounded, with their median at the corner and no mean. That is the Cauchy, the
circle's law without the V. The central sweep, from the middle of the pair, reads the same points as squares, bounded, with
means. They are two names for one point, joined by a theorem in Euclid.

The third essay takes the inscribed sweep's law on its own terms: what it means that the reader's directions and their
heat are given before anything arrives, how they stay apart from what arrives, and why practice has always been right to read them by
their median.
