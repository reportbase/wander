# Three parties

*The fifth of a series of essays on what can be known from where one stands. Draft, 28 October 2026.*

So far the reader has related itself to one other thing: across and up, h and v, a quarter turn from one to the other.
The world has more than one other thing in it. This essay adds a second, v₂, and asks what changes.

Less than one might fear. The circle becomes a sphere, the quarter turn becomes a wedge, and the Cauchy becomes its
two-dimensional cousin. Every pair of parties keeps its own corner, exactly as before. One thing is new: a third
party brings a third kind of share, and the reader turns out to be "near in everything" for exactly a third of the
directions it can look in. With n other parties it is 1/(n + 1).

---

## 1. Standing in a corner

Stand in the corner of a room, back to where two walls meet the floor. One wall runs off to your left, one to your right,
and the floor runs away in front of you. Every direction you can look in, from straight along one wall, through up the
corner, to straight along the other, lies between those three edges.

That is the reader's space with three parties. Along the first wall is h; up is v; along the second wall is v₂. A
direction is the three of them together, [h : v : v₂], and like a direction in two parties it carries no distance. It
says how much of each, compared with the others.

None of the three is ever negative. The wall to your left is not "minus the wall to your right"; it is just another
wall. So the reader's space is one wedge, an eighth of the sphere of all directions: an *octant*. The other seven
octants of a full sphere are only this wedge faced another way.

*Demos: the room (home in the floor corner, the room one wedge); the wedge.*

---

## 2. Four ways to cut the wedge

A direction is a ray from the corner into the wedge. To draw it you have to cut the ray somewhere, and the essays have
met three of the four natural places to cut.

| cut the wedge where | two parties | three parties | divided by |
|---|---|---|---|
| the length is 1 | the quarter circle, length π/2 | the sphere's octant, area π/2 | the whole length |
| the largest is 1 | the square's two arms, length 2 | the cube's three faces, area 3 | the largest share |
| the sum is 1 | the line h + v = 1, length √2 | a flat triangle, area √3/2 | the sum |
| one share is 1 | the reading v/h, from 0 to infinity | the plane of v/h and v₂/h | one share: a denominator |

Every cut passes once through every ratio, and every cut meets the corner. The numbers in the table belong to the cut,
not to the relations: the quarter arc and the octant both measure π/2 because both are cut by length. Statements about
ratios hold in every drawing. Statements about lengths, angles and areas belong to one drawing, and it has to be named.

---

## 3. The sum, and where the reader stands in it

One cut is special, because it needs no standpoint: the sum h + v + v₂ = 1. It treats every party alike and uses
nothing but addition. Each share is a share of a whole, and only a view that holds the whole has shares. It is the
writer's drawing: a line for two parties, a flat triangle for three.

The triangle shows something about the reader very clearly. It holds every way three breadths can share a whole. A
system's true breadths, taken as shares of their total, would be one point of it, and no common unit moves that point.
Finding it needs the true breadths, which are amounts, and the reader does not have them.

What the reader has is its one length, H. It gives every party that same length; a writer would write V = V₂ = H. On the
triangle that is equal shares, the centre (⅓, ⅓, ⅓). That centre is the *triple corner*, where every pair of parties is
at its own corner at once. The whole of the reader's geometry follows from standing there.

The truth lies off the centre by two ratios that no reading carries: how many of the reader's lengths the second party
really is, and how many the third is. Amounts brought in from outside move the reader's amounts toward the true point.
They do not redraw anything.

*Demo: the sphere in the cube, step 7 (the triangle, the reader's drawing at the centre and a true point beside it).*

---

## 4. Two sweeps: altitude and azimuth

With two parties there was one sweep, from all of h to all of v. With three there are two angles, the same two an
astronomer uses for a sky seen from where they stand.

- **The altitude** runs from the horizontal up to the vertical: from all of h to all of v.
- **The azimuth** runs round the horizontal: from h toward v₂, square to it.

Each reads its own pair and has its own corner at 45°. The azimuth reads v₂/h, whatever the altitude. The altitude reads
v against the whole horizontal reach, whatever the azimuth. These are *horizon* coordinates: the horizon and the
vertical are set by where the reader stands. They choose an order, a forward direction and an up. The wedge itself does
not.

---

## 5. The Cauchy wedge

In two parties the reader's directions, turned steadily, gave the Cauchy. What is the steady sweep of a wedge, and what
does it give?

The natural answer is that every direction of the octant gets equal weight by area: no direction favoured over any
other. Swept that way, the readings v/h and v₂/h have a definite law, the **two-dimensional half-Cauchy**. Its density at
(s, s₂) is 2/π divided by (1 + s² + s₂²) to the power three halves. Four things follow, each checkable.

- **Every pair keeps its corner.** Take v/h alone: it has exactly the law of the one-party case, median at 1, no mean. So
  does v₂/h. So does v/v₂, the pair the reader never divided by directly. Every one of the three pairs has its median at
  its own corner.
- **The six orderings are equally likely.** Of h, v and v₂, each of the six ways to rank them takes a sixth of the
  octant.
- **The near field is a third.** The reader is near in both readings, h the largest of the three, for exactly a third of
  its directions. It is past its corner in one reading for a third, and past in both for a third.
- **The one length matters.** Draw the second and third parties on a page at c₁ and c₂ times H. Then the medians of v/h,
  v₂/h and v/v₂ move to 1/c₁, 1/c₂ and c₂/c₁. Every pair keeps its corner only when both are drawn at the reader's one
  length.

With one other party the near and far fields were halves. With two, near in everything is a third.

*Demos: the wedge (Lambert's map: six chambers a sixth each); the payload doesn't matter, in 3D.*

---

## 6. Why by area, and not by angle

"Every direction weighted equally" sounds unambiguous, but in three dimensions it has to be said carefully.

The reader's ignorance, as the essays have set it out, is that it cannot tell which way it happens to be turned. In
three dimensions the turns are all the rotations of the sphere, and only one way of spreading probability over the
sphere is unchanged by every rotation: equal weight by area. This is a classical uniqueness result, Haar's of 1933. The
exchanges of the three parties alone would not fix it: they are a finite group, and many laws are unchanged by them.

It is tempting to sweep the two angles of section 4 evenly instead, so much azimuth and so much altitude. That gives a
different law, and the difference is instructive. Each sweep keeps its own corner, but the pair that neither sweep
reads, v against h, leaves its corner: v is less than h for 0.343 of the readings instead of a half. The near field
shrinks from a third to 0.233. The rings of azimuth narrow toward the vertical, so an even sweep in altitude crowds
directions up there.

The fix is old. Archimedes proved, in *On the Sphere and Cylinder*, that a band of a sphere has area in proportion to
its height. So sweeping evenly in azimuth and evenly in *height*, rather than in altitude, is sweeping evenly by area.
That is Johann Lambert's equal-area map of 1772. Swept that way, every pair keeps its corner and the near field is a
third.

This even-in-height sweep is special to three parties. With two, the even sweep is the turn itself. With four, the
height's density on the even sweep is (4/π)·√(1 − t²), not flat.

---

## 7. What a flat screen sees

There is a familiar device that records exactly this law without anyone dividing anything: a pinhole camera.

A direction (1, s, s₂) through a pinhole lands at the point (s, s₂) on a flat screen one unit behind it. So if directions
arrive evenly over the octant, the screen records the two-dimensional half-Cauchy. Measured by the angle α off the
camera's axis, its density is (2/π)·cos³α. That is the solid angle a flat screen receives per unit of its area. It is the
geometric part of the classical cos⁴ falloff that darkens the corners of every photograph.

The one-party sweep's density is (2/π)·cos²θ. With n other parties it is the same constant form times cos to the power
n + 1: one more power of the cosine for each party added.

*Demo: the pinhole, "a uniform sky" (the share within q, 1 − 1/√(1 + q²)).*

---

## 8. More parties

Nothing in the argument depended on there being exactly two other parties. With n of them, each read on the reader's one
length, the relation lies on the sphere in n + 1 dimensions, and the reader's space is one of its 2^(n+1) orthants.

- **Every reading and every pair keeps its corner.** Integrate out all but one reading and you get the one-party
  Cauchy. Integrate out all but two and you get the wedge's. Adding a party changes nothing about the pairs already held.
- **Near in everything for 1/(n + 1).** Every ordering of the n + 1 shares is equally likely, so h is the largest for
  1/(n + 1) of the directions: a half, a third, a quarter, and so on.
- **Past the corner in k readings, for any k, equally often.** The number of readings past their corner is spread evenly
  over 0, 1, …, n.

The pairs' corners alone would not give these shares: a weight on two of the six chambers of the octant can keep every
pair's median at its corner and never have h the largest. The equal shares come from treating every turn alike.

None of this involves any party's true size. The turn of H is the geometry's whole work. Each true size is one number of
the amounts, a shift in levels of that party's amounts alone.

---

## 9. Reading and writing, with a second party

The first essay sorted readings into the writer's and the reader's by asking about the other's breadth. With a second
other party the same question is asked of each: each party's breadth is known, and written, or not, and read.

- **Both known.** The writer's view in three dimensions: the solid held whole, its corners computed, no horizon. The
  truth is one point of the triangle.
- **One known, one not.** One pair is held as a writer holds it; the other is read from somewhere. With V known, the
  corner of h and v is computed and V's amounts are exact. V₂ is read on the reader's length, its corner with h found,
  and its amounts known up to one missing ratio. Written in one freedom, read in the other.
- **Both read.** Every direction of the octant is an address [h : v : v₂], two numbers, laid evenly by area and painted
  by whatever arrives along it.

The second essay's π/2 and 2/π have their counterparts here, the writer's measures too (they come from the sphere's
totals, though the π cancels). Over a whole hemisphere, the measure from the centre is its
area over its base, 2; the measure from the edge is its mean height, ½; and 2 × ½ = 1, as π/2 × 2/π = 1 for two
parties. These are not the two-party numbers squared. They are those times 8/π² and π²/8.

---

## 10. The wedge, not the sphere

The sphere of all directions, every octant at once, is the writer's picture. A sphere that placed every place by its
direction *and* its distance would need every distance, the size of everything, which no reader at a standpoint has.

The reader's space is the wedge, given afresh at each standpoint from the relations there. The wedge needs no size: its
readings are ratios, laid by the two-dimensional Cauchy. What a blueprint would add is what it added to the circle,
once per party: the true size of each against H.

The wedge can still be placed anywhere on the sphere. One rotation positions it; the reader's two sweeps then work
inside it, in its own frame. The readings are the same at every position. Only a frame fixed outside, the sphere's,
records where the wedge points. Position, then sweep.

*Demo: the wedge, step 7 (position, then sweep).*

---

## 11. A sphere in a cube

Put a sphere, free to turn, at the centre of a cube of half-width e. From the centre, each point of the sphere goes along
its ray to exactly one point of the cube's surface, on the face of its largest component. Every point of the surface is
reached once, at every size of cube and after any turn of the sphere.

Every point inside the cube, except the centre, is some fraction t of the way out to its surface point, for exactly one t
between 0 and 1. So the pair (direction on the sphere, fraction t) addresses every point of the cube, and it is the same
pair whatever size the cube is. The point itself, in amounts, is e times it.

This is the essays' story in one object. A reader that holds the addresses and not e has the cube up to scale. It draws
the cube at its own one length, right as addresses and off as amounts by the true ratio of e to H. Each face of the cube
is the plane of readings against one denominator, so a sphere swept evenly lays the two-dimensional Cauchy on it: a
face's middle quarter, by area, takes 38% of that face's directions, and the centre of a face is 3^(3/2), about 5.2
times as dense as its corners.

*Demo: the sphere in the cube (any size, the address unchanged; the extent unknown, off by e/H).*

---

## 12. What it comes to

**Reading and writing.** The writer's drawing of three parties is the flat triangle, the sum, needing no standpoint and
holding the truth as one point. The reader's drawing is the wedge, built from a standpoint, holding every party at its
one length: the triangle's centre. The difference between them is two numbers, one per party, and both are amounts.

**The Cauchy.** It does not stay a one-party curiosity. Swept evenly by area, the wedge's readings are the Cauchy's
two-dimensional form, every pair keeps its corner, and the near field becomes a third, then a quarter, then 1/(n + 1).
The same law is what a pinhole's flat screen records of an even sky. A second party changes the shares and changes
nothing about the first.

All of this is said from home, at the centre of the directions, where every law of directions in space is the uniform
one. A standpoint moved off that centre is another matter: there the plane's one Cauchy becomes several laws, and the
thirteenth essay sorts which.

Back in the corner of the room: three edges, one direction at a time, no distance in any of them. Near in everything for a
third of where you can look.
