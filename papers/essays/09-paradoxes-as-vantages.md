# Paradoxes as vantages

*The ninth of a series of essays on what can be known from where one stands. Draft, 28 October 2026.*

Two friends stand at opposite ends of a field and look at each other. A sees B as small, much smaller than A's own hand
held up at arm's length. B, looking back, sees A as small. Both are being perfectly accurate. Now write down what each
observed, as a claim about the world:

> A: "B is smaller than me."
> B: "A is smaller than me."

Side by side, the two sentences contradict each other. Yet nobody is confused. If a child said that people shrink when
they walk away, you would explain that they only look smaller. Nothing is wrong in the field, and nothing is wrong in
either pair of eyes. Something went wrong between the two accurate observations and the two contradictory sentences.

This essay is about what went wrong, because the same thing goes wrong in a long list of famous puzzles: the clocks of
relativity, Simpson's paradox in a statistics class, the prosecutor's fallacy in a courtroom, Zeno's runner, the
two-envelope game, Benford's first digits, the dark night sky. Each has a standard resolution, worked out on its own
terms, and this essay does not offer a better one. It offers one diagnosis that fits them all. Every essay so far has
kept two things apart: what a reader reads from where it stands, and what a writer states from a vantage that holds
everything. A paradox, nearly always, is the two run together. A reading is quoted as if it were a writing, or a
writer's question is put to a reader who has nothing to answer it with.

Each entry below says which vantage each side of the paradox speaks from, what the paradox becomes once they are kept
apart, and what was already known. A caution goes with all of it. A paradox is explained by a vantage reading only if each
side can be shown to speak from the vantage claimed, in its own terms. Recognising a familiar resolution after the fact
and giving it a new name is weak. Where the vantage reading only restates a known answer, the essay says so.

## 1. Each sees the other smaller

Start with the field, because nobody needs persuading that it is no real paradox, and because everything later has its
shape.

When A sees how big B is, A has no tape measure stretched across the field. A has two things in view at once: how big B
appears, and how big something A carries appears, a hand at arm's length. A's observation is a comparison:

  r_A = (how big B appears) / (how big my hand appears).

That is a ratio, a reading in the sense of the first essay. Its denominator is A's own one length, A's H. B does the same
from the other end and gets r_B, against B's own hand. The two readings have different denominators, and each denominator
is private to its seat.

Both readings are correct, and both are less than 1. "Less than 1" means, in each case, "the other looks smaller than my
hand". There is no contradiction yet.

**Where it is made.** Write the two numbers on one line, the number line, as two dots between 0 and 1. On that line every
number is measured against the same unit, the distance from 0 to 1. So the moment both readings are written there, they
are treated as if they had been measured against one shared length. No such length was agreed. A used A's hand, B used
B's. The number line is a fine ruler, the writer's ruler and the readers' shared unit once one has been agreed; the
mistake is to write two readings on it as if the agreement had been made. Then "B looks smaller than my hand" becomes
"B is smaller", and "A looks smaller than my hand" becomes "A is smaller", and those do contradict each other.

**The exchange.** The fix is not to throw the readings away. It is to combine them so that what is private cancels. Write
out what each contains, with S_A and S_B the two true sizes, d the distance between them and d₀ the arm's length:

  r_A = (S_B/S_A)·(d₀/d),  r_B = (S_A/S_B)·(d₀/d).

Both contain the same distance factor d₀/d, and the size ratio, one way up in each. Two combinations follow.
- **The ratio of the readings cancels the distance:** √(r_A/r_B) = S_B/S_A. Who is bigger, exactly, with no ruler.
- **The product cancels the sizes:** √(r_A·r_B) = d₀/d. How far apart, in arm's lengths.

Neither seat alone holds either answer. Both are bought, at the price of one exchange: A tells B its reading and B tells
A. The `pool.html` demo plays a whole game of pool this way. Each ball reads v = r_b/d of another and is told the other's
reading h = r_a/d; v/h is their size ratio with the distance gone, and they touch at h + v = 1. Played from inside, the
game is the referee's to rounding.

So a paradox of this kind has a two-part resolution:
- **Dissolve.** Keep each claim at its own vantage: "smaller than my hand". The contradiction goes, because the two claims
  were never about the same thing.
- **Purchase.** If an absolute answer is wanted, buy a shared denominator. Exchange the readings, bring the two seats to
  one place, agree a unit, or close a loop with a third party. Each is one of the ways the first essay found for carrying
  an amount in.

The two tests of the first and third essays sort it at once. Scale the world, every length in the field multiplied by k:
r_A and r_B do not move, so they are readings. The sizes S_A and S_B move, so "who is bigger, in metres" is a writing.
And the readings' ratio and product, which do not move either, are readings again: the exchange turns two private
readings into two shared ones, without writing anything.

## 2. The denominator family

The same shape, two readings against private denominators written as if on one ruler, runs through a whole family of
named paradoxes. None is resolved differently here. Each is resolved uniformly.

**Each sees the other's clock run slow.** In special relativity, two observers passing at constant speed each measure the
other's clock as ticking slowly. Generations of students have found this impossible: they cannot both be slower. It has
exactly the field's shape. Each observer's rate is a ratio whose denominator is its own clock, a private one. Both are
correct. The contradiction appears only when the two are written on one absolute timeline, as if a universal clock
existed against which both were measured, and relativity's point is that none does.

Why did nobody ever call perspective a paradox, while the clocks spent a century with "paradox" in their name? The two
differ in their physics and not in their logic. Everyone learns in childhood that apparent size has a private
denominator, watching people walk away and not shrink. Nobody gets the same training for time, because at walking speeds
the effect is a millionth of a millionth.

**The twins.** One twin travels and returns younger. "Who is older right now?", asked while they are apart, has no shared
denominator: each age runs on its own clock, and "right now" across a distance is itself a shared ruler that was never
agreed. The twins then buy one: they reunite, bringing both ages to one place, where they can be laid side by side. The
answer is definite and no contradiction remains. Unanswerable before the purchase and definite after it is the signature of
this family.

**The ladder and the barn.** A ladder longer than a barn runs into it fast. In the barn's frame the ladder is contracted
and fits, both doors shut at once with it inside; in the ladder's frame the barn is contracted and it never fits. "Fits"
means both ends inside at the same time, a claim about two places at once, which needs one "now" across them. Each frame
has its own. The ladder fits against one denominator, and was never a fact without one.

**Bell's spaceships.** Two ships joined by a taut string fire identical engines at the same moment, and the string snaps.
"Identical" was certified in the launch frame's simultaneity, a private now, and carried silently into the ships' own
frames, where it does not hold.

**Thomas precession.** Carry a gyroscope round a closed loop of velocity changes and it comes back turned, though every
leg looked free of rotation (Thomas 1926). The eighth essay met this in the dial built from true boosts. The residue
belongs to the loop, not to any leg: the closing of a loop that the first essay used to find a misread link.

**Simpson's paradox.** In a much-taught study of kidney-stone treatments (Charig et al. 1986), treatment A succeeded more
often than B for small stones, 93% against 87%, and for large stones, 73% against 69%. Pooled, A succeeded 78% of the time
and B 83%. A treatment better for every kind of patient looks worse overall. A success rate is a ratio over a group of
patients, and the two treatments had different groups: three in four of A's patients had large stones, about one in four
of B's. Pooling adds the numerators and the denominators as if the groups were one ruler. Kept apart, by stone size, A wins
both. Bought, by recomputing both rates against one standard mix of patients, the comparison is fair.

**The prosecutor's fallacy.** An expert says the chance of this DNA match if the defendant were innocent is one in a
million. A juror hears that the chance the defendant is innocent is one in a million. Those are ratios over different
populations. In a city of ten million, about ten innocent people would match by chance, so given only the match and
nothing else, the defendant is about ten chances in eleven innocent. The legitimate exchange exists, Bayes's theorem, and
its price is the base rate, an amount carried in.

**Down 50%, up 50%.** A price falls by half and then rises by half and ends at three quarters of where it began. The
second percentage was taken against a different denominator, the shrunken price. Kept as ratios, ½ × 3/2 = ¾, nothing is
puzzling.

**Price indices.** To say that prices rose 8%, a statistics bureau compares this year's prices with last year's against a
basket of goods, and the answer depends on whose basket: last year's gives one number, this year's another, one biased each
way. Irving Fisher's ideal index takes the geometric mean of the two (Fisher 1922). That is the field's exchange: the
square root of a product of two readings taken from two seats. It is how national price statistics are computed today.

**Currency arbitrage.** Exchange rates are ratios: dollars per euro, euros per yen, yen per dollar. Honest rates multiply
round the triangle to 1. If one is off, no single rate shows it, since any one looks plausible, but the loop does: the
product comes out 1.01, and that 1% is free money until traders close the loop. A misread link is found only by a second
route, which is exactly what the first and sixth essays' closing of a loop said. Two parties alone cannot see it; a third closing
the loop can.

**What the family has in common.** In every case the claim that sounds absolute ("slower", "older", "fits",
"identical", "more successful", "one in a million", "back to even", "8%", "a fair rate") is a reading against a private
denominator, and the contradiction appears only when that denominator is dropped. In every case the answer exists and has
a price: an exchange, a reunion, a frame named, a standard mix, a base rate, a geometric mean, a loop closed. None of them
needed a new fact. Each needed the denominator put back.

## 3. Two vantages, and what the paradoxes run together

The field has a specific shape, two private denominators. The other paradoxes in this essay have a more general one, and
it is worth stating before going on.

The essays have kept apart two vantages.
- **The reader** stands somewhere. It holds one length, its H, and turns it. What it reads are ratios: directions,
  addresses, the order of things, the corner where two of its relations are equal. It holds no total and no count of
  what there is, and it meets a horizon.
- **The writer** holds the whole. It has every amount in a unit, both hemispheres, the count, the sum. It is the
  blueprint, the equation on the page, the globe. It has no standpoint, no corner and no horizon.

Neither is wrong, and each can do what the other cannot. A reader can build the writer's statement from many readings,
by standpoints, loops closed and one amount carried in (the sixth essay). A writer can state what any reader would read
from any place (the seventh essay's lighthouse drawn at any size). What no one can do is hold both at once in one
observation.

A paradox, in this account, is a question asked from one vantage and answered from the other. Usually it is a writer's
question, a total, a sum, a single length, the whole, put to a reader who holds none of them. Sometimes it is the other way:
a reader's ratio quoted as a writer's amount. The two tests find which:
- **Scale the world.** Multiply every amount by k. If a claim does not move, it is a reading. If it moves, it is a writing,
  and it needed something carried in.
- **Change what arrives.** Keep the standpoint and the turn, and change what reaches the reader. If a claim does not move,
  it is about the addresses. If it moves, it is about the payload.

The rest of the essay goes through the catalogue by kind.

## 4. The whole and the count

**Zeno's runner.** To cross a distance one must first cross half of it, then half of what is left, without end; so the
runner never arrives. The writer holds the whole series and sums it: ½ + ¼ + ⅛ + … = 1. The reader counts the steps, and
each halving is one more step, without end. The fourth essay showed both on one lay. Drawn by length, on the in-place lay,
every doubling past the corner fits into one more H, so infinitely many levels fit in a finite stretch. Counted, each level
takes the same number of addresses, so the count never ends. Finite by length and endless by count: both true, from
different vantages. The standard resolutions, the convergent series and Aristotle's distinction between potential and
actual infinity, are the two vantages named.

**Galileo's squares and Hilbert's hotel.** There are as many square numbers as whole numbers, since each n pairs with n²,
yet the squares are a vanishing share of the whole numbers (Galileo 1638). A hotel with infinitely many rooms, every one
full, still takes a new guest by moving everyone along one. The one-to-one pairing is the writer's: it holds both sets
whole and matches them. "A vanishing share" is a reader's: it counts from home and finds the squares ever rarer. Each claim
is true from its vantage. Cantor's cardinality makes the writer's answer precise, and natural density the reader's.

**The coastline.** A coastline's measured length grows without bound as the ruler shrinks (Richardson 1961; Mandelbrot
1967). "The length" is the writer's single amount. The measured length is a reader's count at its own step, and the fourth
essay's level of detail is exactly that count: a thing is held at the levels its span covers. For such a curve the
writer's single length does not exist, and what exists is the count at each step.

**A growing list looks finished.** A reader of fixed resolution watches a list grow. Past a size set by its resolution,
the part not yet arrived falls below one step: the frontier cannot be told from the horizon, and the list looks
complete. "Finished" is the writer's notion. The reader meets only the horizon closing over the frontier. In the classical
order of limits, the step taken to zero first and the count grown after, the frontier stays visible for ever, which is the
writer's assumption built into the mathematics.

**More points, less seen.** A reader that resolves directions to a step ε sees N points spread evenly round a circle from
its centre. When N is small every point is seen apart. As N grows, neighbours fall within one step of each other and merge,
and the number told apart is (N/π)·arccos(εN/2π). That rises, peaks at about 1.1222/ε, and then falls: past a point, more
points mean fewer told apart. N is the writer's count; what one standpoint resolves is the reader's. The peak is exact:
x·arccos x is largest at x ≈ 0.6522, where it is 0.5611, and twice that is 1.1222.

## 5. No scale preferred

Several famous puzzles turn on a quantity whose scale is unknown. The fourth essay showed what a reader does with such a
quantity: past its own unit it prefers no level to another, so equal ratios get equal room, the measure dx/x. The writer
holds a scale and can put a flat measure on the amounts. Running the two together makes the puzzle.

**Benford's first digits.** In many real collections of numbers, from river lengths to tax returns, the leading digit is 1
about 30% of the time, not the 11% an even spread of digits would give. The even spread assumes a writer's scale. Numbers
met with no preferred scale are spread as the far field is, equal room for each doubling, and the measure dx/x gives the
leading digit d the share log₁₀(1 + 1/d), which is 30.1% for a 1. Hill proved that scale invariance implies the law (Hill
1995). The vantage reading is his theorem said in the essays' terms.

**Two envelopes.** One envelope holds twice what the other does. Whichever you hold, the argument goes, switching gains on
average, half the time doubling and half the time halving; so you should switch for ever. The argument averages over the
amounts, which needs a total over all of them, a writer's distribution. The holder of one envelope knows neither amount and
has no scale. With none preferred the measure is dx/x, which has no total and cannot be made a probability. The averaging
step needs exactly what the holder lacks. The standard resolutions say the paradox needs an improper prior; the vantage
reading says why that prior is the one a reader with no scale would hold.

**The St Petersburg game.** A coin is tossed until it lands heads; the payout doubles with each tail. Its expected payout is
infinite, and nobody will pay much to play (Bernoulli 1738). The infinite expectation is the writer's total. The player
values payouts from where it stands. Past its own unit there is no preferred scale, so each doubling counts the same:
Bernoulli's logarithmic utility is the far field's lay. The reading adds one thing: inside the player's unit, value would be
in proportion, so the reading predicts near-linear valuation of small stakes and logarithmic of large ones. That is a
prediction, and it has not been tested here.

**Weber and Fechner.** A just-noticeable change in a stimulus is a fixed fraction of it, not a fixed amount, and sensation
grows as its logarithm (Weber 1834; Fechner 1860), except near threshold. A fixed step would be a writer's scale. The
perceiver reads from its own unit, and past it equal ratios get equal room: the logarithm. Near threshold, inside the unit,
the reading predicts proportion, which is where the law is known to bend.

**Water and wine, and Bertrand's chord.** The third essay worked these two in full. Every fair answer to von Mises's water
and wine puts the corner at a half, and the levels give ln 6/ln 9; Jaynes's ½ for Bertrand's chord is a thrower's for
whom the circle may lie anywhere, and from a standpoint the share runs from ⅓ to ½ with the circle's distance. Both are this section's
puzzle: a question about an unknown scale, answered by indifference over a writer's amounts instead of a reader's levels.

## 6. Sums and the median

**The Cauchy has a median and no mean.** A well-shaped, symmetric law whose average does not exist, and which more samples
do not help. The third essay is all about this one. The mean is a sum over amounts, a writer's total. The Cauchy is the law
of a reader's addresses, which are directions. What survives from a standpoint is what the reader can find from home: the
median, at the corner, and the symmetry under the reciprocal.

**The median survives; the mean does not.** Statistics of order, ranks, the median, the corner, are unchanged by any
increasing change of what arrives. Statistics of amount, the mean and every sum, are not. Re-timing the arrivals of a
five-lobed star moved the mean over arrivals by 27% while the address statistics stayed fixed. Order is what the reader
holds; sums need the writer's amounts. This is the third essay's second test applied to statistics: change what arrives,
and only the order stands.

**Olbers' dark night.** In an endless, unchanging universe of stars, every line of sight ends on a star, so the night sky
should be as bright as the sun's surface. The paradox sums the light of every shell of stars, each shell giving the same
light, and the sum diverges: a writer's total. A reader receives only what has arrived, and its sky fills only as far as
signals have come. The standard answer, the universe's finite age and the finite speed of light, is exactly that. The
vantage reading restates it and adds nothing to it.

**A star dims, but its surface does not.** Light falls as the inverse square of distance, yet a resolved surface looks
equally bright at any distance. The total light caught is a sum over the source, an amount. Brightness per address is what
the reader holds. Per address nothing changes with distance; distance changes only how many addresses the source fills,
until it fills less than one, and then that one dims. This is the radiance theorem, conservation of brightness along a ray,
said as address and payload.

## 7. Near and far, and a ratio read as an amount

**Near and far seem to obey different laws.** The near field and the far field of a source, a lens or an antenna behave
as if governed by different mathematics, and one expects the far to continue the near and finds otherwise. The
expectation is the writer's: one drawing, one scale throughout. The two laws are the reader's two sides of the corner,
v read against h before it and h against v past it. The plainest case is the light of a disc, which grows as x²/(1 + x²) in
its size against its distance: in proportion to x² near and saturating far, the change at x = 1. Near- and far-field
approximations are standard, and that they are one function is known; the reading says why the change comes where it
does.

**v/h read as V/H.** A geometric result seems to predict a physical amount, and then fails by a constant factor. The
reading v/h is a ratio of unit relations, the reader's. V/H is a ratio of breadths, the writer's. The bridge between them is
(v·V)/(h·H) = (v/h)·(V/H). Reading one as the other assumes the writer's breadths. With both drawn at one length the two
agree in number, which is why the confusion is easy, and why it fails by V/H when the breadths differ. These essays have
kept the case of the letters because this one mistake undid many earlier attempts, including some of our own.

**The seam in a flight simulator.** The change from ground to sky looks wrong in flight simulators however finely the
ground is drawn. The ground is modelled in the writer's metres; the eye reads it as h/v ahead. A line on the ground h ahead
lands v/h below eye level, so the corner, h = v, keeps one row of the screen at every height. Levels of detail set by
metres slide across the screen as the height changes; set by h/v they stay put. The seam is where the two disagree. That is
a reading with an illustration, the `flight.html` demo, and it is the eighth essay's depth buffer in another costume.

## 8. What the reader can tell apart

**The Gibbs paradox.** Mixing two different gases raises the entropy however alike they are, but mixing a gas with itself
does not, so the entropy jumps as the gases become identical. The writer labels every molecule. A reader that cannot tell
the gases apart has nothing to mix. Entropy counts what the reader can tell apart. That is the standard resolution, by
indistinguishability, and it is Jaynes's own account that entropy depends on the distinctions the observer makes (Jaynes
1992).

**The heap.** One grain is not a heap, and adding a grain never makes a heap; yet heaps exist. The writer's predicate is
sharp. The reader resolves "more than one" only from its own step. In a synthetic test in the working notes, a reader
with no model of what it looks at is certain of "two" once the two are one step apart, and below that is right with
probability equal to their separation in steps. The vague boundary is the reader's resolution limit, an amount carried
in with its instrument, not a fact about the heap, and not the corner. That is closest to the epistemic view of vagueness,
one theory among several.

## 9. Candidates

Some puzzles look as if a vantage reading might fit, and have not been worked. They are listed so that the list is
honest.
- **Railroad tracks** meet at the vanishing point and never meet on the ground. Projective geometry's points at infinity
  already settle it; the reader's horizon is the vanishing point.
- **The moon illusion.** The moon looks larger near the horizon (Kaufman and Rock 1962). There is no settled explanation,
  and none is offered here.
- **Monty Hall.** The host acts from a view of all the doors; the player reads the act from its own. Whether that is more
  than a restatement of the conditional probability has not been checked.
- **Zeno's arrow.** Motion at an instant: the writer's snapshot against the reader's arrivals.
- **Quantum measurement.** Whether observer-dependence there is a question of vantage at all is not something these essays
  can say. It is named only to say so.

## 10. What it comes to

**Reading and writing.** The catalogue sorts by one question: was the claim read or written? A reading is a ratio from a
standpoint, and it does not move when the world is scaled. A writing is an amount, and it needed something carried in. Every
paradox in the denominator family is a reading quoted as a writing, its private denominator dropped. Zeno, Galileo, the
coastline, the growing list and the many points are a writer's whole asked of a reader's count. Benford, the envelopes,
St Petersburg and Weber–Fechner are a writer's flat measure laid where a reader has no scale. Olbers and the dimming star
are a writer's sum asked of a reader's addresses. Gibbs and the heap are a writer's labels or sharp line asked of a
reader's resolution.

**The Cauchy.** It appears in the catalogue twice, as an entry and as the pattern. As an entry, it is the law with a median
and no mean, which the third essay explained. As the pattern, it is the reason the reader's side of each paradox has what
it has: order and not sums, a median and not a mean, a corner and a horizon and not a total. The reader's addresses carry
the Cauchy, and the Cauchy keeps exactly the statistics a reader can use.

Nothing here is a new resolution. Perspective needed no new fact; the twins needed nothing relativity did not already say;
the kidney-stone data were correct all along; Hill had proved Benford; Olbers' answer was known. Each was resolved by
finding an assumption so automatic that nobody noticed making it: a shared ruler, a total, a writer's whole. What the
essays add is that the assumption has one form, and two tests find it.

Back to the field. A sees B small and B sees A small, and both are right. Each was reading. The contradiction came from
writing their readings on one line as if they had agreed a unit, which they had not. One phone call later, they have the
ratio of their sizes and their distance apart, both exact, and still no unit.
