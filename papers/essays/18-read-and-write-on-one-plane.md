# Read and write on one plane

*The eighteenth of a series of essays on what can be known from where one stands. Draft, 7 November 2026. It gathers
the rulings of 7 November (R736–R740) on Euler's plane, the thumb's lay, the writer's sphere behind them and two turns in
space, and follows* Reads and Writes *§2 ("The plane") and §9. Brought to R788 on 10 November: up is the read, gaps across are
places, and only the across axis's zero, tied to a unit, is a write.*

Put two fingers on a map on a phone and move them. Spread them and the map grows; twist them and it turns; do both at
once and it does both, and neither gets in the other's way. The phone works out what the fingers did from one number:
the line from one finger to the other, after, divided by the same line, before. Written as a complex number that
quotient is k·e^(iα), the growth k times the turn α. Its logarithm is

  ln k + iα,

and the two parts are the two gestures: the pinch on the real part, the twist on the imaginary part. A pinch moves only
the first, a twist only the second, and since adding is the same in either order, a pinch then a twist ends where a
twist then a pinch does.

That logarithm is the subject of this essay. The series has two ideas, reading against writing and the Cauchy, and two
tests, scale the world and change what arrives. On the logarithm's plane the first idea and the first test are not an
argument but a picture: reading runs one way across it, placing the other, and writing is where the across axis has its
zero. This essay draws the picture, says what it
shows past itself, and says where it stops.

The order: one plane (§1), the writer's grid on it (§2), two corners as two mirrors (§3), the thumb's lay, distance as a
turn (§4), the sphere behind it, the writer's (§5), heat counted per chart (§6), reads counted, and the writer's limit
(§7), what the plane is not (§8), where it stops (§9), who saw which piece (§10), and what it changes (§11).

---

## 1. One plane

Stand somewhere and look out. Every point of the world in front of you is, as a writer writes it, a distance d and a
direction θ, or as one complex number z = d·e^(iθ). Hold up your thumb at arm's length: H, the one length the reader has.
Divide by it and take the logarithm:

  ln(z/H) = ln(d/H) + iθ.

The angle θ is the writer's coordinate on the direction, the one in which turns add. The reader holds the direction as
the pair [h : v], its slope s = v/h, and composes turns by tangent addition, (s₁ + s₂)/(1 − s₁s₂), with no angle (§8).

Draw that as a plane: the direction up, the distance across, the distance read as a ratio of thumbs, one in two, one in
four, or two thumbs, four, eight, each the same step across. That is Euler's plane, and `euler.html` draws it with a
writer's grid on it.

On it the two tests of the series become the two directions of the page.

**Scaling slides across.** Hold up a thumb k times smaller, or make everything but the thumb k times larger, and every
point moves sideways by ln k. Nothing moves up or down, and a slide keeps every gap across. Make everything k times
larger with the thumb, and no point of the page moves at all: a distance in thumbs is what it was. What that scaling
moves is only where the across axis's zero sits against a unit outside the world, the thumb in metres, since
ln(d/metre) = ln(d/H) + ln(H/metre) and only the last term changes. So the first test, scale the world, comes down to which part
moved: what stays is a reading (up) or a place (a gap across, a distance in thumbs, one distance against another); what
moves is the zero tied to a metre, a writing. The direction up is what one read holds. The distance across is a place:
unmoved by scaling, but held by no single read, needing a second standpoint and the step between. Where its zero falls
against a unit, the size of anything in metres, is the one number no reading or place fixes.

**Turning the world slides up or down.** Turn, and every point moves up by the same amount, and nothing moves across. A
reader's two ignorances, after Jaynes, are which way it faces and how large things are. On this plane they are two slides
along two axes, and they commute: turn then scale ends where scale then turn does. The measure that suits both ignorances,
dθ·dκ/κ, is nothing more than area on this plane. Jaynes found it by asking what the reader's ignorance leaves unchanged.
The plane shows it.

That is the picture. Reading is up and down; places are across; a write ties the across axis's zero to a unit. The
phone's two gestures are the two slides.

## 2. The writer's grid on it

Lay a writer's grid on the ground in front of you, squares one unit on a side, the eye at one corner, and carry every
point of it onto the plane. The grid is straight; what it becomes is not, and how it bends says what each act holds.

- **Circles about the eye become upright lines.** A circle of one radius is one distance at every direction. The writer's
  circles at one thumb, two, four and eight come out evenly spaced, ln 2 apart: on this plane the far field is an even
  grid, every stretch where the reading halves the same width. No circle sits inside another; nothing nests.
- **Rays from the eye become horizontal lines.** A ray is one direction at every distance.
- **Every straight line is the same curve.** A line at distance p from the eye, facing φ₀, is
  u = ln(p/H) − ln cos(θ − φ₀): the one curve −ln cos θ, slid across by how far off the line is and up or down by which way
  it faces. It spans exactly half a turn of directions, nearest the eye at its middle, running off to the far side at
  both ends. The writer has infinitely many lines; the reader's plane has one shape of line, moved.
- **A logarithmic spiral is a straight line.** A spiral whose distance grows by the same factor for every equal turn is a
  slanted straight line on the plane, its slope the spiral's growth per turn. It crosses every ray at one angle. Anything
  that keeps a fixed angle to something it is closing on, a moth to a lamp, a hawk to its prey held at one place in its
  view, travels a log spiral, and on this plane flies straight. (Tucker (2000) proposed it for raptors, whose sharpest
  vision looks sideways; the same issue's field study found wild peregrines flying curved paths that resemble it.)
- **The grid's points pile up on the far side.** Per unit of the plane's area the even grid holds e^(2u) points, so most of
  the writer's grid lies far across, toward the horizon. The writer's evenness is the far side's crowd.

## 3. Two corners, two mirrors

The series has two corners, and on this plane each is a mirror.

Swap a reading for its reciprocal, v/h for h/v, s for 1/s (the writer's θ for 90° − θ): a reflection top to bottom about
the horizontal line s = 1, the writer's 45°. That line, left in place, is the corner in direction.

Swap a distance for its reciprocal about the thumb, d/H for H/d, and ln(d/H) changes sign: a reflection left to right
about the upright line at one thumb. That line is the corner in distance, where near meets far.

Each corner is moved by sliding the plane along its own axis: turning the head moves the first across the world's
directions, and choosing the thumb moves the second across the distances. They sit on different axes, so they are set
independently, and they cross at one point: one thumb out along the look at 45°, which is where the series' heat is
hottest, the thing looked at.

**A walk on the plane.** Walk toward a mountain with the thumb held out and watch the point for the mountain's edge.
Covering a fixed share of the distance left with every step is a steady slide across the plane, the same slide each step,
and a steady slide never runs out of plane: the foot is its limit. A steady stride is not a slide here. It is even on the
writer's line instead, where thumb over mountain falls by the same amount each stride, and it reaches the foot in a
finite count. On the writer's circle, Z = (1 + is)/(1 − is), both run from 1 far off, through i at the corner, toward
−1 at the foot: one arrives by counting, the other approaches by ratio, and a turn of the look does neither (WLK, exact).

## 4. The thumb's lay: distance as a turn

The plane runs without end both ways across. The eye itself is infinitely far to the left, the horizon infinitely far to
the right. The reader's own lay of distance, the near and far fields as the thumb lays them, is bounded:

  g = (2/π)·arctan(d/H),

0 at the eye, ½ at one thumb, 1 at the horizon, never reached. Written against the plane's own coordinate, x = ln(d/H),
it is

  g = ½ + gd(x)/π,

with gd the Gudermannian function, the one Mercator's chart is built on. So the lay of distance is a turn too: the same
arctangent that lays the directions, applied to distance in thumbs. At one, two, four and eight thumbs g is 0.5000,
0.7048, 0.8440 and 0.9208, each stretch taking less room than the one before, toward half, as the fourth essay's
in-place lay does. Drawn with the turn up and g across, the lay is a square: the far field pressed against its right edge, which is
every direction's horizon at once. Drawn with g as the radius and the turn as the angle, it is a quarter disc, the eye at
its centre and the horizon its rim.

The square's two edges are worth a look. Its right edge is the line at infinity: every vertical line of the writer's grid
ends at one point of it, every horizontal line at another, and the diagonals at a third, 45°. A family of parallel lines
meets the edge at one point, its vanishing point. Its left edge is the eye, drawn as a whole side; the quarter disc puts
it back to one point.

## 5. The sphere behind it, the writer's

A writer can say why the three pictures are so alike. Take the plane of distances and directions and wrap it onto a
sphere, stereographically, scaled so that the circle one thumb out lands on the equator. The eye goes to one pole and the
horizon to the other. The near field is the half on the eye's side, the far field the half on the horizon's, and the
swap of d for H²/d, the corner in distance, is the reflection in the equator. Then:

- the thumb's lay is the sphere's latitude: g is the angle from the eye's pole over π;
- Euler's plane is the sphere's Mercator chart;
- the lay drawn square is its chart of plain latitude and longitude (the plate carrée), and drawn as a quarter disc its
  map out from the eye (the azimuthal equidistant);
- sech(ln(d/H)), the series' heat in distance, is the cosine of the latitude, and the K places a writer counts along
  distance, H·tan(kπ/2K), are the meridian cut into equal steps, half in each half of the sphere.

Turning the world turns the sphere about its axis. Holding up a thumb k times larger slides every latitude toward the
eye's pole, by tan(χ′/2) = tan(χ/2)/k: a boost of rapidity ln k along the axis. That is the moving sky of the seventh
essay, aberration, done to distance instead of to directions. A turn about the axis and a boost along it commute, which is
why both are slides on Euler's plane.

The sphere is a writer's drawing, and the read is not its even measure. Count the read per unit of the sphere's area and
it runs as 1/sin of the angle from the eye's pole, without bound at both poles; the sphere's own even spread is a
different law. The sphere has a size and a surface that closes; the reader has the lay, and never holds the sphere whole.
`euler.html`'s "the writer's sphere" turns it, and "move the thumb in and out" plays the boost.

## 6. Heat counted per chart

The series calls the read a heat map: hottest where it looks, cooling outward. On this plane it turns out that heat is
always heat per unit of something.

Per unit of turn, the directions have no heat; per unit of the reading s = v/h they fall off as 1/(1 + s²), the half-Cauchy
(the writer's cos²θ); per unit of a flat screen in space as 1/(1 + r²)^(3/2) (cos³α). Per unit of the thumb's lay, distance has no heat either; per unit of ln(d/H) it
is (1/π)·sech(ln(d/H)), the bell centred on one thumb; per unit of the writer's plane it runs without bound at the eye.
The lay square, the turn against g, is the one chart on which the read is flat both ways, every place the same room.

So the series' heat in the plane, (1 + s)²/(2(1 + s²)) · 2x/(1 + x²) with x = d/H (the writer's
cos²(θ − 45°)·sech(ln(d/H))), counts direction per unit of reading and distance per unit of
ratio. It is a good choice, the one that puts the hot spot on the thing looked at, and it is a choice: no chart gives a
heat of itself. `euler.html` paints both, the heat at each point and the heat per unit of each chart, and on the lay the
second is one flat colour.

## 7. Reads counted, and the writer's limit

Euler's formula is usually met as a limit, and the limit is a count of reads. Cut a turn θ into n reads of θ/n, each the
pair [1 : θ/n], and compose them by tangent addition; in complex numbers that is

  (1 + iθ/n)ⁿ,

exact at every n, rational whenever θ/n is. Its direction falls short of θ by θ − n·arctan(θ/n), about θ³/(3n²); its
length grows by (1 + θ²/n²)^(n/2) − 1, about θ²/(2n). Four reads of one radian fall 0.0201 short and are 1.129 long. The
direction comes right fast; the length comes right slowly, and no read ever sees it, since [h : v] divides the length out
at every step. As n grows the count becomes e^(iθ), with its length 1: the limit, held whole, is the writer's. The same
limit along the real axis, (1 + 1/n)ⁿ, is Bernoulli's e. One limit, two axes: e compounds along the thumb, e^(iθ) square to
it. `euler.html`'s "n reads" draws the polygon closing on the circle.

The count never lands on −1, at any n, with fractions for θ: the direction is always a little short and the length a
little long. Yet −1 is reached in two steps, exactly, by another road: the corner is the quarter turn,
(1 + i)/(1 − i) = i, and i × i = −1. That is the one finite door, since no finer equal step with a rational slope is a
rational part of the turn (Niven). So e^(iπ) = −1 holds three ways: the reader has the exchange, w(s) × w(1/s) = −1; a
writer at work has the two corner steps, and approximations for anything finer; only the paper, the writer at the
limit, has it with the arc named π and the base named e, neither of which the fact depends on (the second essay, §9).

## 8. What the plane is not

It is the writer's notation for the read. e^(iθ) states the turn as a number and carries π with it, and π, the turn
completed, is the writer's (the fifteenth essay). The reader holds a direction as the pair [h : v], composes by tangent
addition, and states no angle. The plane is the best map of the read, not the read.

It does not hold the second test. The paint, whatever arrives along each direction, sits on top of the plane at every
point, the same on both axes. Euler's plane separates reading from placing and writing; it does not separate the heat
from the paint.
That split stays where the third essay put it: change what arrives, holding the standpoint and the turn, and ask what moved.

And the sphere behind it is a picture, not a claim about the world. It says why the three charts agree; it is drawn
from no standpoint, so no reader stands on it.

## 9. Where it stops

The plane is the plane's. In space a point is still a distance times a direction, scaling still slides only the distance,
and the scale test still asks which part moved. The split survives. What does not survive is turning as a slide.

Turns of space do not commute. A thing at the triple corner with a small frame on it, turned a quarter about one thumb and
then a quarter about another, lands 120° from where the same two turns in the other order put it (under one of the two
orders the direction itself comes home and only its frame is turned). Round a small loop, a turn α about one thumb, β
about the other and both back, it comes home turned about nearly the third thumb by about α·β, the area the loop encloses:
1.741° for 10° and 10°, against α·β = 1.745°. On the plane the same loop leaves nothing. `euler.html`'s "in space" sets the
two side by side; the thirteenth essay takes the reason apart (Frobenius, Gauss and Bonnet, Berry's phase).

That loop is two turns about two axes. Take a turn and a scaling instead: up by α, across by λ (the thumb scaled, the
first test's own motion), and both undone. On the plane it closes because the two slides commute. In space it closes
too, since scaling by one factor commutes with every turn of space; a scaling put anywhere into a loop changes only the
loop's overall size (checked to 10⁻⁶¹, the plan BRL). The loops are compositions of motions, the writer's account, not
reads. So three loops part. A turn and a scaling close, in the plane and in space.
Two reads in different directions come home turned, by Ω with cos(Ω/2) = 1 − 2·sin²(α/2)·sin²(β/2)·sin²φ for axes φ apart,
the same turn every time the loop is repeated, so the thing goes round and never grows. A turn with a scaling in two
unequal lengths, a stretch by c along one axis (the writer's two axes drawn at V = cH), comes home squeezed: its net
map has eigenvalues e^(±ζ), cosh ζ = 1 + 2·sin²α·sinh²(½ ln c), about |α·ln c| when small, a squeeze of rapidity ζ up to a
turn, and it grows by ζ with every loop: 0.4507 a loop at α = 40° and c = 2. It closes only at c = 1. A loop of a turn and
a scaling is steady exactly when the scaling is in one length, which is the one-length circle said of a loop.
`euler.html`'s "breaths" view draws the three side by side.

Could another chart do in space what Euler's plane does in the plane? It was put to arithmetic (the plan FLC; `euler.html`'s "a flat chart in space" draws it), and the
answer is mostly an old one. The distance keeps its slide: the same logarithm of the distance in thumbs, set beside the
direction, lays space out as a straight line times the sphere of directions, and scaling the world or the thumb moves the
line's coordinate alone, commuting with every turn of space, to rounding. The heat can be laid flat too: Lambert's
equal-area map about the look, crossed with the thumb's lay g, carries the read's heat at one constant density, and every
turn of the look keeps that map's areas, so the heat stays flat wherever the look goes. What no flat chart can do is make
the turns rigid. The turn that keeps Lambert's areas bends its shapes: turn the look by φ and, in the writer's sums, the old
look's direction is stretched by 1/cos(φ/2), √2 at a quarter turn, and the directions square to both looks by up to 2; over
every direction in front of both looks the stretch never passes 2. The stereographic chart keeps every turn's angles instead and loses the heat, a
quarter as hot at its rim as at its middle. Gauss's theorem says no flat chart of a piece of a sphere keeps its distances,
and Euler (1777) that none keeps both angles and areas. The plane gets both from one chart because a line times a circle is
flat: the circle unrolls into the line of θ, and the sphere of directions does not unroll.

So the opening picture is exact in the plane and half of it holds in space. The half that holds is reading against
placing and writing. The half that goes is the arithmetic of turning.

## 10. Who saw which piece

Almost all of it is old.

- **The plane.** Euler's formula (1748) and the complex logarithm; polar form splitting a number into a direction and an
  amount. That the phone's two-finger gesture is one complex quotient is how every map on a phone already works.
- **The grid on it.** Lines as one curve moved and log spirals as straight lines are the log-polar map's, used in image
  registration and in the primate visual cortex's map of the visual field (Schwartz 1977). Halley (1696) showed that a
  rhumb line, stereographically projected, is a logarithmic spiral ("Proportional Spirals about the Pole Point", his Lemma
  III), from a lemma of de Moivre's that the projection keeps angles, and set its distances from the pole by Euclid III.20,
  the same theorem as essay 2's one read.
- **The lay.** The Gudermannian is Mercator's (1569) function, named for Gudermann (1830s). The sphere behind the three
  charts is Riemann's, stereographic projection from Ptolemy and Hipparchus; Mercator, plate carrée and azimuthal
  equidistant are its old charts.
- **The boost.** Aberration as a Möbius map of the sky's stereographic chart is Penrose's and Terrell's (1959).
- **The measure.** dθ·dκ/κ is Jaynes's (1968), from transformation groups.
- **The limit.** (1 + iθ/n)ⁿ is Euler's own route; the leftover of non-commuting steps in space is the Lie–Trotter
  formula (Trotter 1959).

What is the series' own is the reading: up as the read, gaps across as places and the across zero as the write, the scale
test as which part moved, the two
corners as its two mirrors, the thumb's lay as a turn in distance, the sphere as the writer's picture of why the charts
agree, the heat as a count per chart, and the place where it stops as the place where reading and placing stay apart but
turning stops being simple.

**In physics.** A boost along one axis is a slide on this plane: in the stereographic chart about that axis it
multiplies z by the Doppler factor k, sliding ln z by the rapidity, so collinear rapidities add as slides do, and the
velocity law (β₁ + β₂)/(1 + β₁β₂) is the writer's tanh of the reader's sum. Three stars'
directions before and after fix the Möbius map between the two looks, with no frequency and no distance; the rapidity
is that map's coordinate, inferred from its trace, 2·cosh(ζ/2), not itself a read. That is how it is sorted, not a doubt that it can be
measured: the boost is a physical change of standpoint, and the stars are the evidence for it. Two boosts along
different axes cannot be one slide, and leave a turn, the plane's limit met in space (*Reads and Writes in Physics*
(`../reads-and-writes-physics.md`), §5.3).

## 11. What it changes

It gives the series one picture. Reading against writing has been argued in words, in tests and in demos; on this plane it
is the difference between up, gaps across and the across axis's zero, and anyone who has pinched and twisted a map has
already done the two slides.

It says what the thumb is. Choosing H slides the whole plane across and moves nothing up or down: the thumb is a zoom on
distance and nothing else. On the writer's sphere it is a boost along the axis, the same act that a moving observer
performs on the sky.

It settles what the heat is. The heat is the read's measure counted per unit of a chart, and the lay square is where it is
flat. A heat map is a good picture of the read, and the picture depends on the chart.

And it marks where the clean arithmetic ends. In the plane, turns add; in space they do not, and a loop leaves a turn
behind. What survives everywhere is the part the series cares most about: the read holds directions, places hold gaps
across, the write holds amounts, and scaling the world moves only the third.

`plane-sphere.html` (the plane's one law and the sphere's three), `levels.html` (the reader's lay of distance),
`boost-sky.html` (a boost on the sky's chart), `uncompressed.html` (a writer's grid in space, compressed into the read).
