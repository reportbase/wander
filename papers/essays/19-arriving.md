# Arriving: the walk, the landing and Euler's −1

*The nineteenth of a series of essays on what can be known from where one stands. Draft, 11 November 2026. It gathers
the rulings of 11 November (R866–R874) on the walk to the mountain, and follows* Reads and Writes *§1 ("A law in place of
memory") and §6 ("Walking to the mountain", "Two ways to arrive"). Its checks are WLK, runs 1–3, exact.*

A butterfly comes down on a leaf and does not overshoot it. A walker sets out for a house at the end of a long road and
gets there. Neither is told how big the thing is or how far away. Both arrive.

This essay is about what the two have in common and where they part. What they have in common is given to anything that
looks: the thing grows in view as it nears, and it grows by a rule that asks nothing about its size. Where they part is
in what they add to the looking. The walker counts strides. The lander counts nothing and holds one reading steady. Those
are two different paces, and no pace is both.

The series has two ideas, reading against writing and the Cauchy, and two tests, scale the world and change what
arrives. Here the first idea is taken down a single road. Look, stride, look: the look is a read, the stride counted is
the smallest write there is, and the road from far off to the door is, in the writer's notation, half of Euler's
circle, with −1 at the door.

The order: the scene (§1), one look (§2), two looks and a stride (§3), the even ladder (§4), the other pace (§5), the
writer's circle (§6), a chain of looks and where the infinite is (§7), what is free, what is kept and what is carried in
(§8), in space (§9), who saw which piece (§10), and what it changes (§11).

---

## 1. The scene

Hold your thumb out at arm's length and walk toward a house. The thumb is two centimetres across and the arm sixty
long, so the thumb is one part in thirty of its distance from the eye. That one in thirty is the only number you carry
that is about yourself, and it is a ratio: it has no unit.

The house is twelve metres across and some way off. You do not know either figure, and nothing you see on the walk will
give you either one in metres. What you see is the house against the thumb.

Call the reading s: house over thumb, as they appear. Far off the house is one in eight of the thumb, then one in four,
one in two. At one place it exactly covers the thumb, s = 1. Nearer, it is two thumbs, four, eight, and at the door it
is no longer a thing in view at all. Keep the upside-down reading in mind as well, thumb over house, 1/s: it runs from
large, far off, through 1, down to nothing at the door.

## 2. One look

A single look is a read. It holds a direction, the finger the house's edge falls on, and it holds nothing else: no size,
no distance, no memory of the look before. Make the whole world ten times larger, the house, the road, your arm and your
thumb with it, and the look is the same look. That is the first test, and the read passes it.

One look does hold one remarkable thing. The place where the house covers the thumb is found by looking and by nothing
else. "Now the house is my thumb" needs no walking and no counting. The writer can say where that place is, thirty house
breadths from the door, 360 metres for this house, but the walker standing there has not been told that. It has only the
fit. This place is the corner.

A look also has no "nearer". Nearer compares two looks, and a look keeps nothing to compare with. So the walk, as a
sequence that gets somewhere, is not something a look contains. It needs something kept.

## 3. Two looks and one stride

Take a look. Take one stride toward the house. Take another look. Now there are two readings and one counted step
between them, and that is parallax, laid along the line of sight instead of across it.

In the upside-down reading the two looks give x₁ and x₂, thumb over house before and after. Two things follow, and they
are exact.

- The strides left to the door are x₂/(x₁ − x₂).
- The house's breadth, in strides, is the thumb's one in thirty divided by (x₁ − x₂).

Try it 700 metres from the house with a stride of 0.7 metres. The two readings are 35/18 and 35/18 less 7/3600. Their
difference is 7/3600. The strides left come to 999, which is right: 699.3 metres at 0.7 a stride. The breadth comes to
17 and one seventh strides, which is twelve metres.

Notice what was needed. Two reads, and a count of one. The count is a write, but of the gentlest kind: it has no unit.
Scale the world, stride included, and the count is still one, the strides left are still 999, the breadth is still 17
and a seventh strides. The series calls this a write of the relation. Metres come only if someone says how long a
stride is, and that is the other write, the write of the scale, the only place physics enters the walk.

And notice what was not needed. The stride did not have to be small. The two formulas are exact for a stride of any
length. There is no limit in them.

## 4. The even ladder

Now keep walking, and watch thumb over house.

It falls by the same amount every stride. Not roughly: exactly. Far down the road, at the corner, three strides from
the door, each stride takes 7/3600 off the reading for this house. The reading is a ladder with even rungs, and you walk
down it one rung a stride.

From the corner, where the reading is 1, to the door, where it is 0, there are 3600/7 rungs: 514 strides and two
sevenths of a stride. Multiply by the thumb's one in thirty and you have the breadth in strides; multiply by the stride
in metres and you have twelve metres. For a tower sixty metres across the count is 2,571 and three sevenths; for a
mountain three kilometres across, 128,571 and three sevenths, from a corner ninety kilometres out.

Three things about this ladder are worth saying plainly.

It is the writer's line. A ruler that is even everywhere, with no near and no far, is the number line, and that is
what thumb over house turns out to be: the distance to the door, measured in corner-distances. Walking with a counted
stride is writing, and the upside-down reading is the chart in which that writing is even.

It has no corner on it. The rung just before the corner, the rung across it and the rung after are the same size. So
the corner is not something the count can find. The corner belongs to the looks; the ladder belongs to the strides.

And the count does not land on the door. 514 and two sevenths is not a whole number. No whole count of strides puts you
exactly at the foot of a thing whose size you were never told. The last part of a stride is settled by a look, at your
own feet.

The right-way-up reading, house over thumb, is not even at all. Its steps grow at every stride and without limit as the
door nears: this is the thing looming.

## 5. The other pace

There is a second way to arrive, and it counts nothing.

Instead of taking the same stride each time, cover the same share of what is left: a tenth, say. Then house over thumb
grows by the same ratio at every step, ten ninths, and thumb over house shrinks by nine tenths. To hold this pace you
need no count and no size. You need only to keep one thing steady from look to look: how fast the thing is growing,
as a ratio. That is a rule made of reads alone.

This pace slows as it nears. From the corner of the house, fifty such steps leave less than two metres; a hundred leave
a centimetre. In the ideal it never touches: after any number of steps something is left, and the door is a limit. A real
thing that approaches this way arrives because it has legs, or a body with a size, or because it changes its rule at the
end. That last moment is its own affair and not the geometry's.

The walk is the first pace and a landing is the second. They can be told apart exactly. The stride is even in thumb over
house. The fixed share is even in the ratio. A pace that is even in both takes no step at all. So these are not two
descriptions of one motion: they are two motions, and what they share is only the reads they stand on.

Nothing here says which pace any creature takes. The claim is the smaller one: both paces are built from the same looks,
and the looks cost nothing.

## 6. The writer's circle

The writer has one picture for all of this, and it is Euler's.

Put each reading s on the number

  Z = (1 + is)/(1 − is).

Every such Z lies on the circle of radius 1. Far off, where s is near nothing, Z is 1. At the corner, s = 1 and Z = i.
At the door, where s grows without end, Z goes to −1. So the whole road, from far off to the door, is the upper half of
that circle, travelled from 1 through i to −1.

The writer names the point by an angle and writes the end of the road as e^(iπ) = −1. Read on the road, Euler's identity
is the arrival. The corner is i, and i times i is −1: the corner, taken twice, is the arrival. And a place and its
mirror, the place at distance d and the place at the corner's distance squared over d, have readings s and 1/s, and

  Z(s) · Z(1/s) = −1

for every s. None of that needed the angle. It is rational in the reading. The angle, and π with it, are the writer's
way of naming where on the circle a reading falls.

On this circle the three things a walker can do are three different kinds of motion, and the kinds are classical.

- **A turn of the look** moves every reading and leaves no reading where it was. It goes round and arrives nowhere.
- **The fixed share** leaves two readings where they were, the far-off one and the door, 1 and −1. Everything else
  flows from the first toward the second and never reaches it.
- **The stride** leaves only one, the far-off 1. Nothing holds it away from −1, so it passes through −1 after a
  finite count. Keep walking past the house and the readings come back round the lower half of the circle toward 1.
  The whole circle is the whole road, in both directions.

So Euler's −1 is reached by a count and approached by a ratio. That is the walk and the landing, in one line.

## 7. A chain of looks, and where the infinite is

A walk is parallax repeated: look, stride, look, stride, look. What do all those looks come to?

Take the change from each look to the next, as a ratio of their two points on the circle, and multiply the changes
together along the walk. Everything in the middle cancels. The product is the last look over the first. However many
strides there were, equal or not, the chain comes down to its two ends. That is exact, and it needs no small steps.

The same is true of the turn gathered. The pieces of arc between looks join, by the rule for joining slopes, into one
piece whose size depends only on where the walk began and ended. From the corner to the door the looks gather exactly a
quarter of the circle, whether you take seven strides or a thousand.

It is tempting to say that Euler's identity is what you get when the strides become infinitely small. It is not. The
strides can be any size. What the half circle needs is a walk that began without end. Start thirty corners out and you
gather all of the half circle but about a fiftieth; start a million corners out and you are short by less than a
millionth; no start is far enough to gather it all. The infinite in e^(iπ) = −1 is the horizon behind the walker, not a
vanishing step.

There is one place where a step must shrink. If, instead of joining the slopes, you add them as though they were angles,
the sum comes out too large, by an amount that falls as the square of the stride, a quarter each time the stride is
halved. The angle is that sum's limit. Joining is exact at any stride and belongs to the geometry. Adding is the writer's,
and the limit comes with it.

## 8. What is free, what is kept, what is carried in

The road sorts the series' three things cleanly.

**Free.** The looks, the fit at the corner, and the rules for what looks do together: two looks a stride apart give the
strides left; n strides are one slide of n rungs; a chain collapses to its ends; a place and its mirror multiply to −1.
Each rule is one expression that holds for every case at once. It is not worked out along the way and it does not wait
for the walk. This is what the series means by a geometric law, and it is why the geometry needs no memory: a law says
what steps do, for all steps, without any step being taken.

**Kept.** How many strides were taken. The law has an empty place in it, the n, and the geometry never fills it. A look
fills the other empty place, the reading, now. The count fills this one, so far. It has no unit, and scaling the world
does not move it.

**Carried in.** The stride in metres. Everything on the road stands without it. With it, the house is twelve metres;
without it, the house is 17 and a seventh strides, and both statements are complete.

What is still not understood sits in the middle one. Something has to hold the count, and a look holds nothing. And a
count assumes that a stride is the same stride each time, which the geometry does not supply: on the ladder one rung is
exactly like the next, and a walker's strides are only roughly so. The read is solid. The write, even this smallest
write, is where the open questions are.

## 9. In space

On a road there is one line of sight, and everything above happens along it. In space the walker has two thumbs crossed,
and a step to the side moves the reading on one thumb and not the other, which is parallax in its usual form; a step
forward scales both alike, and is this essay's walk. The even ladder, the two paces and the collapse to the ends all hold
along the line of sight wherever it points.

What does not carry over is the neatness of turning. On the road a turn of the look and a step do not interfere. In
space, turns about different axes do not commute, and a loop of turns comes back turned (essay 13). The walk to the door
is spared this only because it keeps to one line.

## 10. Who saw which piece

- **Time to contact.** That the strides left come from the reading and its change, with no distance and no speed, is
  Lee's (1976), for braking; his rule that a controlled approach keeps the rate of change of that time at or above −½ is
  in essay 6. The lander's pace, holding the rate of growth steady, is the same quantity held constant.
- **Parallax.** Two looks a known step apart placing a thing is as old as surveying; along the line of sight it is the
  same triangle, flattened.
- **The circle.** Z = (1 + is)/(1 − is) is Cayley's transform (1846), carrying a line to a circle. The three kinds of
  motion, with none, one or two fixed points on the circle, are the classical elliptic, parabolic and hyperbolic maps
  of the line of slopes.
- **The identity.** e^(iπ) = −1 is Euler's (1748).
- **The collapse.** A product whose middle cancels is a telescoping product; that slopes join by tangent addition is
  the tangent's addition law.

What is the series' own is the sorting: the look as the read, the counted stride as a write with no unit, the two paces
as two ways of adding to the same reads, the corner as the looks' and the ladder as the strides', and the infinite in
Euler's identity placed at the far start of the walk.

**In physics.** Nothing here is new physics. The quantity the walk reads, reading over its change per look, is the time
to contact in the walker's own ticks (Lee 1976), unmoved by scaling the world; seconds and metres enter only when a
tick or a stride is given a unit (*Reads and Writes in Physics* (`../reads-and-writes-physics.md`), on motion from
reads).

## 11. What it changes

It makes the write small enough to look at. The series has said that writing brings in amounts; on the road the first
write is a count of one, with no amount in it, and the amount arrives separately and last.

It separates two things that are easy to run together: finding the corner and finding the size. The corner is seen. The
size is counted. Neither can do the other's work.

It says where Euler's identity sits in the scene. It is not the limit of small steps. It is the whole road, from a start
that was never begun to the door, and any walk that actually starts somewhere holds an exact, finite part of it.

And it says what a walker and a lander share. The reads are the same for both and belong to neither. What each builds on
them is its own.
