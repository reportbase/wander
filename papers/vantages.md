# Three vantages, and the paradoxes of conflating them

*Started 11 October 2026, at Tom's word: "yes, lets start collecting those paradoxes, its not a short list." A working
paper: a catalogue to grow, revised as it is worked over, not a ruling. It takes its terms from SPN
(`papers/serial-parallel-nowhere.md`) and the review paper (`papers/observation-from-somewhere.md`). An entry goes into
those papers only once it has been worked and ruled.*

## The insight (Tom, 11 October; SPN R191)

> "the core insight in our papers is that classical math and physics assume a completed perspective. A completed
> perspective is best known as the equation or the drawing on the page. However, there are actually three perspectives,
> completed "classical', outer-hemispherical and inner-hemispherical. Each has its own geometry, and they are easily
> conflated. Many of the paradoxes of math and science are easily explained by simple correctly identifying what your
> vantage is."

## The three vantages

| vantage | what is held | its geometry | landmarks |
|---|---|---|---|
| **completed** (classical): the equation, the drawing on the page | every breadth, all four facings at once (situations 1 and 2) | the circle h² + v² = 1, indexed by x and y; totals and means exist | none: no home, no corner set apart, no horizon |
| **outer hemisphere** (parallel, situation 3): one hemisphere taken in at once | H, not V | one facing, counted at once: the bell, sharpest at the corner and halving per doubling away | the corner as the middle of what is seen |
| **inner hemisphere** (serial, situation 4): one point at a time | H, not V | one facing, swept from home: the fisheye; counted, the Cauchy | home at true scale, the corner, the mathematical horizon never reached |

**One line, three readings** (Tom, 11 October; SPN R193): "The true breadth of the line is 1. The outer hemisphere's reading of that line is PI/2, the hemisphere turns away from the reader. The inner hemispheres reading of the line is 2/PI, the hemisphere turns towards them."

| vantage | how the line faces the reader | its reading of a line of true breadth 1 |
|---|---|---|
| completed | held whole | **1** |
| outer hemisphere | turns away: read as the arc over its chord | **π/2** ≈ 1.571, longer than the line |
| inner hemisphere | turns toward: read as the average facing over the sweep | **2/π** ≈ 0.637, shorter than the line |

π/2 · 2/π = 1: the two situated readings, multiplied, give back the completed one. And the classical breadth needs both
(Tom, R194: "the "classical" breadth is 1, it requieres knonw the inside and outside hemispheres in your hand."): it is not a third reading beside the other two, but what is held when both hemispheres are in
hand at once. A reader holding one hemisphere has its own reading, π/2 or 2/π, and not the line's breadth. This is the 22 September "everywhere
equation" (archived *The Order of Limits*: "The god's eye view is defined as π/2 · 2/π = 1"), now read as the three
vantages. Each situated reading is right from its vantage; a paradox follows whenever either is quoted as the line's
breadth. *Reading (Claude's, unruled):* this is the denominator family's exchange (§F) on one line: the product of the
two private reads is the shared fact, and their ratio, π²/4 ≈ 2.47, is what each seat keeps to itself. The inner
reading 2/π is also stage 1's share in `plans/omm-plan.md` (the average fill of h over one facing's sweep).

Two lines from the archived *The Relation Space* (§6, §17) name the completed vantage's cost: **"the equation is a
third party"** (a page, a shared scale or an equation lends its terms to the answer, as a spring scale does; a balance's
pull cancels and leaves nothing), and **"a symmetric account holds the figure and cannot state the rate; an asymmetric
one states the rate and cannot hold the figure."**

The outer hemisphere is not the completed view: it sees a whole hemisphere but holds only one breadth, and does not know
N (the 22 September ruling). Neither situated vantage knows N, and both meet horizons. The same addresses can be drawn
from either situated vantage; the completed view is the one that holds the world's breadths as well.

**How to read an entry.** Each paradox is set out the same way:
- *The paradox*: as usually stated.
- *Vantages*: which vantage each side of the paradox is speaking from.
- *Reading*: what the paradox becomes once the vantages are kept apart.
- *Already known*: the standard resolution, if there is one. Most of these have one. Where it exists, the vantage
  reading is offered as an account of *why* the standard resolution works, not as a replacement for it.
- *Standing*: **worked** (worked in the corpus, with its source), **reading** (Claude's or Tom's reading, unruled), or
  **candidate** (named, not yet examined).

**A caution for the catalogue.** A paradox is resolved by a vantage reading only if each side can be shown to speak from
the vantage claimed, in its own terms. Recognising a familiar resolution after the fact and renaming it is weak. Entries
that a vantage reading predicted wrongly stay here, marked.

## From situated to completed: what memory and synthesis do

*Added 11 October, after a comment Tom shared (ChatGPT): a human can turn through 360° and, by memory and synthesis,
build a completed perspective; so "the completed perspective is therefore not necessarily a single act of observation",
and the question is whether synthesis "merely accumulate[s] information, or must it transform the observations into a
different geometric representation". Claude's reading, from SPN; unruled.*

- *A still world comes back whole.* If nothing moves, a serial reader's record equals a parallel reading, in any order
  (SPN Proposition 9.1). Memory turns the inner hemisphere into the outer one at no cost.
- *Joining the records is a change of geometry.* Each reading arrives swept from home, a fisheye in one facing; the
  completed view indexes (the x, y grid, every place reached directly, no home or horizon). "The view from somewhere
  sweeps, the view from nowhere indexes" (R179). To build the whole, the reader re-lays each swept reading as an index:
  the inverse of the sweep, the shortcut that makes sweeps act like indexes. Synthesis transforms; it does not only
  accumulate.
- *A full turn gives the whole shape, not its size.* Turning through 360° with memory collects all four facings, the
  circle's geometry, but the reader still holds only H: scaled about the reader, every reading is unchanged (SPN
  Proposition 8.1). What comes back is the completed shape up to a scale. Turning in one place gives directions, not
  distances; placing things needs a second standpoint, with the reader's own travel as the one length (`flight.html`).
- *Three gaps memory cannot close by itself.* The scale (only a bridge gives it: travel, a known constant, a known kind,
  a shared fact; SPN §8.4); what lies below the reader's step (lost at that step to every vantage); and motion (readings
  must be carried forward, with an error SPN Proposition 9.2 bounds). Hidden sides need further standpoints (SPN §6.4).
- *Both hemispheres* (R194): the classical breadth needs "the inside and outside hemispheres in your hand". Memory
  joins one reader's readings across a turn; the completed view needs the two hemispheres, the one turned away and the
  one turned toward, held together.
- *So:* the completed view is legitimate. It is what integration builds once the three gaps are filled. A paradox of
  this catalogue is, typically, the completed view used as if the gaps were already filled.

## A. In the geometry of the corpus

**A1. Near and far seem to obey different mathematics.**
- *The paradox*: the near field and the far field of a source, a lens or a measurement behave as if governed by
  different laws; one expects the far to continue the near and finds otherwise.
- *Vantages*: the expectation of continuity is the completed view's (one drawing, one scale throughout); the two laws are
  the inner reader's two sides of the corner.
- *Reading*: one geometry, read through the flip at the corner: v in terms of h before it, h in terms of v past it. The
  disc's light, x²/(1 + x²), is the plainest case.
- *Already known*: near- and far-field approximations are standard; the observation that they are one function is too.
  The vantage reading says why the change comes where it does.
- *Standing*: **worked** (SPN §3.2, Propositions 3.1–3.2; `plans/nrf-plan.md`).

**A2. The Cauchy has a median but no mean.**
- *The paradox*: a well-shaped, symmetric distribution whose average does not exist; averaging more samples does not
  help.
- *Vantages*: the mean is a census, the completed view's total; the Cauchy is the inner reader's count of its readings.
- *Reading*: from the inner hemisphere there is a reading at every distance and no total of the whole. What survives is
  what the reader can find from home: the median (the corner, for one facing) and the symmetry under the flip.
- *Already known*: the Cauchy's moments diverge; its median and the Möbius invariance of the family are classical.
- *Standing*: **worked** (SPN Proposition 1.2, "The Cauchy and the fisheye, derived"; review paper §5).

**A3. The number line's two infinities.**
- *The paradox*: the line runs out without end and divides in without end; two infinities, apparently two properties.
- *Vantages*: two properties on the drawn line (completed); one pair, seen from either side of the reader's unit (inner).
- *Reading*: the flip exchanges them, step for step; they are the far field and the near field of the reader's unit.
- *Already known*: the reciprocal map on the positive reals; the projective line's single point at infinity.
- *Standing*: **worked** (`number-line.md` §1).

**A4. v/h read as V/H.**
- *The paradox*: geometric results seem to predict physical amounts, then fail by a constant factor.
- *Vantages*: v/h is the inner reader's ratio of fill levels; V/H is the completed view's ratio of breadths.
- *Reading*: (v·V)/(h·H) = (v/h)·(V/H). Reading one as the other assumes the completed view's breadths. Under the
  calibration V = H they agree numerically, which is why the conflation is easy and why it fails by V/H.
- *Already known*: dimensional analysis and calibration, in general.
- *Standing*: **worked** (SPN §1, "H and h kept apart"; most of the working papers' corrections of 9 October).

**A5. The seam where the ground meets the sky in a flight simulator.**
- *The paradox*: the transition from ground to sky looks wrong in flight simulators however finely the ground is drawn.
- *Vantages*: the ground is modelled in the completed view's metres; the eye reads it in fill levels, h/v ahead.
- *Reading*: a ground line h ahead lands v/h below eye level, so the corner (h = v) keeps one screen row at every height.
  Levels of detail set by metres slide on the screen; set by h/v they stay put. The seam is where the two disagree.
- *Already known*: level-of-detail schemes by screen-space error are standard in rendering.
- *Standing*: **reading**, with an illustration (`flight.html`).

**A6. Children's number lines: logarithmic or linear?**
- *The paradox*: children's placements look logarithmic, then linear with age; the field disputes whether either shape
  is real (Siegler and Opfer 2003; Dehaene et al. 2008).
- *Vantages*: a bounded line, 0 to N given, is held whole (completed: endpoints and midpoint); an open line is read from
  the unit outward (outer hemisphere: proportion near the unit, compressed past it).
- *Reading*: the shape is set by the task's vantage, so the same child should show both by task.
- *Already known*: proportion judgment accounts for bounded-line placements (Barth and Paladino 2011).
- *Standing*: **reading**, partly tested. NLE run 1 *killed* the corner model's prediction on bounded lines (proportion
  judgment won), which fits the bounded line being the completed vantage but is not support for the reading. The split by
  task is detectable on synthetic readers (`plans/nls-plan.md`) and has not been run on real data.

## B. In mathematics

**B1. Zeno's dichotomy (and Achilles).**
- *The paradox*: to cross a distance one must first cross half, then half the rest, without end; so motion never
  completes.
- *Vantages*: the completed view holds the whole series and sums it, ½ + ¼ + … = 1; the serial reader takes the steps
  one at a time.
- *Reading*: drawn by length, infinitely many doublings fit in one finite stretch (the in-place lay: the whole far field
  in one more H); counted, each doubling takes the same number of addresses, without end. Finite by length, endless by
  count: both true, from different vantages.
- *Already known*: the convergent series; Aristotle's distinction of potential and actual infinity.
- *Standing*: **reading** (review paper §4, "Reality in 2H").

**B2. Galileo's paradox and Hilbert's hotel.**
- *The paradox*: there are as many squares as whole numbers, yet the squares are a small part of them (Galileo 1638); a
  full hotel with infinitely many rooms still takes a new guest.
- *Vantages*: the one-to-one pairing is the completed view, holding both sets whole; "a small part" is the serial
  reader counting from home, finding squares ever rarer.
- *Reading*: each claim is true from its own vantage; the paradox is asking one vantage to answer the other's question.
- *Already known*: Cantor's cardinality makes the completed view's answer precise; natural density makes the counter's.
- *Standing*: **reading**.

**B3. Bertrand's paradox (the random chord).**
- *The paradox*: "a chord chosen at random" has probability 1/3, 1/2 or 1/4 of exceeding the inscribed triangle's side,
  depending on how it is chosen (Bertrand 1889).
- *Vantages*: the completed view holds every drawing at once and prefers none; a situated reader's ignorance picks out a
  measure.
- *Reading*: the same as the three drawings of one sweep (review paper §2): the square, the line and the arc pass through
  the same ratios and give different measures on them. The single principle (equal addresses for what the reader cannot
  tell apart) picks the measure.
- *Already known*: Jaynes resolved it by invariance under the transformations the problem leaves unspecified (Jaynes
  1973), which is the same principle.
- *Standing*: **reading**, resting on a known resolution.

**B4. Benford's law.**
- *The paradox*: in many real collections of numbers the leading digit is 1 about 30% of the time, not 11%.
- *Vantages*: an even spread of leading digits assumes the completed view's scale; data met with no preferred scale is
  laid as the far field is, equal room per doubling.
- *Reading*: no preferred scale gives the measure dx/x, and dx/x gives leading digit d the share log₁₀(1 + 1/d).
- *Already known*: scale invariance implies Benford's law (Hill 1995).
- *Standing*: **reading**, resting on a known theorem.

## C. In probability and decision

**C1. The St Petersburg paradox.**
- *The paradox*: a game whose expected payout is infinite, which no one will pay much to play (Bernoulli 1738).
- *Vantages*: the infinite expectation is the completed view's total; the player values payouts from where it stands.
- *Reading*: past the player's own unit there is no preferred scale, so each doubling counts the same: Bernoulli's
  logarithmic utility is the far field's address space. Inside the unit, value would be proportional, so the reading
  predicts near-linear valuation of small stakes and logarithmic of large ones.
- *Already known*: logarithmic utility (Bernoulli 1738); bounded utility.
- *Standing*: **reading**; the small-stakes prediction is untested.

**C2. The two-envelope paradox.**
- *The paradox*: one envelope holds twice the other; whichever you hold, switching seems to gain on average, so you
  should switch forever.
- *Vantages*: the argument assumes a completed distribution over the amounts; the holder of one envelope knows neither
  breadth.
- *Reading*: with the amounts' scale unknown and none preferred, the measure is dx/x, which has no total and cannot be
  made a probability. The averaging step needs the completed view's total, which the holder does not have.
- *Already known*: resolutions through proper priors, and the observation that the paradox needs an improper prior.
- *Standing*: **reading**.

**C3. The sorites (the heap).**
- *The paradox*: one grain is not a heap, and adding a grain never makes a heap; yet heaps exist.
- *Vantages*: the completed view's predicate is crisp; the reader resolves "more than one" only from its own step across.
- *Reading*: GRN run 3: "two" is certain from one step across and has probability equal to the size in steps below it.
  The vague boundary is the reader's resolution limit, an amount (physics, not the corner), not a fact about the heap.
- *Already known*: many theories of vagueness (epistemic, supervaluationist, degree).
- *Standing*: **reading**, with a synthetic illustration (`plans/grn-plan.md`). It is closest to the epistemic view.

## D. In physics

**D1. Olbers' paradox (the dark night sky).**
- *The paradox*: in an endless, unchanging universe of stars, every line of sight ends on a star, so the night sky should
  be bright.
- *Vantages*: the paradox sums every shell's light, which is the completed view's total; a reader receives only what has
  arrived.
- *Reading*: each shell gives the same light, so the completed sum diverges; the reader's sky fills only as far as
  signals have come, its lit share 1 − e^(−L/λ).
- *Already known*: the universe's finite age and the finite speed of light (with expansion as a smaller effect). The
  vantage reading restates that answer; it does not add to it.
- *Standing*: **reading**, with an illustration (SPN §9.9, OLB; `sky.html`).

**D2. A star dims with distance, yet its surface does not.**
- *The paradox*: light falls as the inverse square, yet a resolved surface looks equally bright at any distance.
- *Vantages*: the total light caught is the completed view's sum over the source; brightness per address is what the
  inner reader holds.
- *Reading*: per address nothing changes; distance changes only how many addresses the source fills, until it is under
  one, when that one dims.
- *Already known*: conservation of radiance along a ray (the radiance theorem).
- *Standing*: **worked** (`papers/inverse-square.md` §3).

**D3. The relativity of simultaneity.**
- *The paradox*: two observers disagree, each correctly, about which of two distant events came first.
- *Vantages*: a single "now" for every place is the completed view's; each reader holds only the order of arrivals.
- *Reading*: without the completed view's shared clock, simultaneity is a convention of each reader.
- *Already known*: special relativity (Einstein 1905).
- *Standing*: **reading**, with illustrations in Wander's world (SPN §9.9, SIM; `pool.html` run 3, read late).

**D4. The Gibbs paradox.**
- *The paradox*: mixing two gases raises the entropy, however alike they are, but mixing a gas with itself does not; so
  the entropy jumps discontinuously as the gases become identical.
- *Vantages*: the completed view labels every molecule; a reader that cannot tell the gases apart has nothing to mix.
- *Reading*: entropy counts what the reader can tell apart: the single principle again.
- *Already known*: the resolution by indistinguishability, and Jaynes's account that entropy depends on the
  distinctions the observer makes (Jaynes 1992).
- *Standing*: **reading**, resting on a known resolution; illustrated in Wander's world (SPN §9.9, TRK).

**D5. The coastline paradox.**
- *The paradox*: a coastline's measured length grows without bound as the ruler shrinks (Richardson 1961; Mandelbrot
  1967).
- *Vantages*: "the length" is the completed view's; the measured length is a reader's count at its own step.
- *Reading*: the count is the level of detail. What the reader holds is the count at its step, set by size against step;
  the completed view's single length does not exist for such a curve.
- *Already known*: fractal dimension.
- *Standing*: **reading** (review paper §6).

**D6. Weber and Fechner: why perception is logarithmic.**
- *The paradox*: a just-noticeable change is a fixed fraction of the stimulus, not a fixed amount; sensation goes as the
  logarithm, except near threshold.
- *Vantages*: a fixed step is the completed view's scale; the perceiver reads from its own unit.
- *Reading*: past the reader's unit no scale is preferred, so equal ratios get equal room: the logarithm. Near threshold,
  inside the unit, the reading predicts proportion, which is where the law is known to bend.
- *Already known*: the laws themselves (Weber 1834; Fechner 1860); Schwartz's log-polar map of visual cortex, proportional
  near the fovea; Gilinsky's perceived distance with a finite horizon.
- *Standing*: **reading**.

## F. The denominator family

*From `papers/denominator-paradox.md` ("Each Sees the Other Smaller", a companion to an interactive demo; an older paper
of Tom's, written with Claude in an earlier session, added 11 October). It gathers eleven standard paradoxes under one diagnosis and states that none is resolved differently, only
uniformly. This section maps that diagnosis onto the three vantages; the paper itself is the source for each entry.*

**The diagnosis, in the vantages' terms.** "A denominator paradox is what you get when measurements made against
different private rulers are compared as if they shared one ruler." Each private ruler is a situated reader's own H; the
one ruler the measurements are silently put on is the completed view's single unit, the number line. So the denominator
family is the vantage conflation in its plainest form: two situated readings written into the completed view without
the bridge that would license it.

**Why the ruler is private** (archived *Browsing the Fisheye* L62–66): "the reader's capacity is the only unit in the
room"; H is the denominator because it is the one held when V arrives, and swapping them takes another experiment, not a
change of notation. *The Relation Space* §9 has the same in a picture: the bucket and the water, "whichever is greater
must be divided"; past the corner the reader cannot hand over the unit, so it keeps itself as the unit and counts.

**The two-part resolution, in the vantages' terms.**
- *Dissolve*: keep each claim at its own vantage ("smaller than my ruler"). This is the catalogue's reading step.
- *Purchase*: buy a shared denominator. Each of the paper's purchases is one of SPN's bridges: exchanging the reads is a
  shared fact; reuniting the clocks brings two readers to one standpoint; standardising a patient mix or geometric-meaning
  two baskets is an agreed unit (SPN §8.4: the unit is not geometric, and only agreement supplies it); closing a loop is
  a third reader. The number line is legitimate as a ruler agreed between readers (`number-line.md`, "a shared ruler");
  it is counterfeit when it is assumed instead.

**The exchange is already worked in the corpus.** The paper's two reads are, in amounts, r_A = (S_B/S_A)·(d₀/d) and
r_B = (S_A/S_B)·(d₀/d); their ratio gives the size ratio, √(r_A/r_B) = S_B/S_A, and their product the distance,
√(r_A·r_B) = d₀/d. In `pool.html` each ball reads v = r_b/d of the other and is told the other's reading h = r_a/d: the
exchange. Then v/h = r_b/r_a is the size ratio with the distance cancelled, and contact is h + v = 1 (`plans/pol-plan.md`,
P1–P4, not killed: the game played from inside equals the referee's). Keep the case: r_A, r_B, S, d are amounts, and
their corners are corners of named pairs. The archive has the exchange twice more: as arithmetic ("dividing the reads
cancels what the seats share; multiplying cancels what is private to each", *Degrees of Freedom* §4.1, L718–766) and
measured, as the two-group tower, where closure between two readers with different holds recovers the one factor that
crosses, the ratio of the holds (*The Order of Limits* L2494–2530; SPN Proposition 1.2, "The tower").

*What the family adds to the catalogue.* A cleaner test of a vantage conflation: find the private denominator, and ask
whether the comparison was bought or assumed. *What it does not add:* every entry already has its standard resolution;
the claim is uniformity, as the paper itself says.

## G. From the archived papers

*Pulled forward on 11 October from `papers/archive/` (`plans/archive-gems.md`). Each was worked in its paper in the
terms of its day; the readings below restate it in the three vantages. Standing: **reading** unless marked.*

**G1. A growing list looks finished** (*The Order of Limits* L279–362). A reader of fixed fidelity watches a list grow;
past a size set by its fidelity the part not yet arrived falls below one step, the frontier cannot be told from the
horizon, and the list looks complete. *Vantages:* "finished" is the completed view's object; the serial reader at fixed
step meets it only as the far horizon closing over the frontier. In the classical order (the step taken to zero first)
the frontier stays visible for ever. *Already known:* none as such; it is the order of limits met in a list.

**G2. A hemisphere's count quoted as the whole's** (*The Order of Limits* L1027–1103). Four counts, M ≤ V_ε ≤ V ≤ N:
what is met, what is resolved, what the hemisphere holds, what there is. Quoting one as another is safe only on the
circle, where nothing varies with direction. *Vantages:* N is the completed view's; V and V_ε are the outer and inner
hemispheres'. A ready source of conflations.

**G3. The median survives, the mean does not** (archived *Reader Geometry as Addressing*, Proposition 14, L884–946).
Statistics of order (ranks, the median, the corner) are unchanged by any increasing change of the payload; statistics of
amount (the mean, every sum) are not, and re-timing the arrivals of a five-lobed star moved the mean over arrivals 27%
while the address statistics stayed fixed. *Vantages:* order is what the situated reader holds; sums need the completed
view's amounts. The Cauchy (A2) is the extreme case: "an address is not supposed to have an average" (archived *One
Reader* L501–514). *Already known:* the robustness of order statistics; Jensen's inequality for the mean.

**G4. A uniform address space cannot report position** (*Browsing the Fisheye* L1008–1017). A list whose every entry
holds the same thing gives the same view at every address: from inside, travelling is indistinguishable from standing
still. *Vantages:* the completed view knows where it is by the index; the situated reader knows only by what stands at
the addresses. "What makes a list browsable is not that its addresses are distinct but that what stands at them is."

**G5. More points, less seen** (archived *One Reader* L896–909; *The Order of Limits* L1754–1845). A parallel reader of
angular step ε seeing N evenly spaced points round a circle resolves R(N) = (N/π)·arccos(εN/2π) of them, which peaks at
R_max ≈ 1.1222/ε and then falls: past a point, more points mean fewer told apart. *Vantages:* the completed view's N
against what one standpoint resolves. **exact** (the peak checked: x·arccos x is largest at x ≈ 0.6522, giving 0.5611,
so 2 × 0.5611 = 1.1222).

## E. Candidates, not yet examined

Named because a vantage reading looks possible. Each needs its vantages shown in its own terms before it is moved up.
- **The railroad tracks.** Parallel lines meet at the vanishing point; in the completed view they never meet. (Projective
  geometry's points at infinity already resolve it; the corpus places the vanishing point at home of the reading, R77.)
- **The moon illusion.** The moon looks larger near the horizon (Kaufman and Rock 1962), a perceptual paradox with no
  settled explanation.
- **Monty Hall.** The host acts from a completed view of the doors; the player reads the act from its own.
- **The liar and self-reference.** A reader reading itself (the corpus's "readers as payloads").
- **Zeno's arrow.** Motion at an instant: the completed view's snapshot against the serial reader's arrivals.
- **Quantum measurement.** Speculative, and flagged as such: whether the observer-dependence of quantum outcomes is a
  vantage question at all is not something the corpus can yet say.
