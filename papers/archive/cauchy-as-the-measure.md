### *Why the heavy-tailed law with no mean is not a pathology but the forced measure of a bounded observer — its derivation from swap-invariance, its identity with the shared denominator, and the many names it wears across the disciplines*

**Abstract.** The Cauchy distribution is the standard textbook example of a "pathological" law: heavy-tailed, no mean, no variance, the counterexample that breaks the central limit theorem. This paper argues the opposite valuation. From the perspective of *bounded observation* (the framework of *The Geometry of Bounded Observation*, gbo), the Cauchy is not an exotic special case but **the** measure — the unique unbiased weight a finite observer places on its own reads, forced by one symmetry and one structural fact. It is derived here in one page from swap-invariance plus the rotation law; identified with the shared denominator $h$ that every bounded read divides by; shown to have a total measure that is exactly a hemisphere of the unit sphere ($\pi$, $2\pi$, $\pi^2$ as the rises go one, two, three), so the flat reads and the round pointing report one area twice — a hemisphere that is also the *field of one act*: the horizon sits a quarter-turn from home, which is the lived fact that you cannot see behind your head, and §4.6 runs that datum backward to recover the whole read format from it — with $\pi$ throughout quoted one dimension up from the observer, on the seat's own axis, the direction the read divides away (§4.7); shown to close three ways — the round sphere, the tensor torus, the box cube — that agree only in one dimension and split by a countable number of holes above it (a split visible already in raw capacity: $2^d$ corners against $d{+}1$ seats, agreeing only at $d = 1$ — §4.7), with the fisheye itself always round (one rim, one shared horizon) so the torus can only appear once the sharing is refused; shown to be the same object as the fisheye of optics, the Lorentzian of physics, the foveal falloff of vision, and the level-of-detail density of computer graphics; and its famous defect — the missing mean — explained as a circle asked for a center it structurally cannot have. The "pathology" is an artifact of scoring a bounded-observer measure in an unbounded observer's court. Companion to gbo (upstream) and *The Bounded Observer in Motion* (sr), whose results are cited throughout; every formula is verified at the house bar (exact where possible, machine-precision otherwise, zero fitted constants).

## 1. The claim, and the reversal it asks for

In the surveyor's court — the completed line, uniform measure, totals that exist — the Cauchy is a scandal. Its density $\tfrac{1}{\pi}\tfrac{1}{1+r^2}$ falls off so slowly that $\int r\,w(r)\,dr$ does not converge: **the distribution has no mean.** Its sample average does not settle; drawing more data does not help; the central limit theorem excuses itself. Every one of these is stated, in the standard telling, as a *failure* — the Cauchy is what a well-behaved distribution is defined against.

This paper makes the reverse claim. The Cauchy is not a failure of a distribution to be nice; it is the **success of a bounded observer at being unbiased**. It is the one measure a finite observer — one that holds no absolute scale, reads only ratios, and grants no privileged point — is *forced* to carry, and the properties the surveyor reads as pathology are exactly the fingerprints of that forcing. The missing mean is not a bug; it is the observer correctly declining to name a center that does not exist. To see this we derive the Cauchy from the observer's premises rather than positing it, and then watch every "defect" turn into a signature.

The organizing sentence, proved below: **uniform on the loop, read in slope, is the Cauchy** — and the loop, the reading, and the slope are all forced by what a bounded observer is.

## 2. The observer's coordinate: the read $r = v/h$

The primitive of bounded observation is one act: two held quantities read only against each other, landing in one register (gbo §1, §3; forced as the *only* primitive by the uniqueness theorem, gbo §1.25). Name them the **rise** $v$ — what arrives — and the **run** $h$ — the held reference, the pan the observer supplies. The read is their ratio,
$$r = \frac{v}{h} \in [0,\infty]\quad(\text{signed atom: }[-\infty,\infty]),$$
with three self-locating landmarks: **home** $r=0$ ($v$ vanishes, fully in view), the **corner** $r=1$ ($v=h$, balance, the one point certifiable with no external scale), and the **horizon** $r=\infty$ ($h$ vanishes, edge-on, hidden).

The run $h$ is the object this whole paper turns on. As the SR companion puts it (sr §0): **"the denominator is not a quantity; it is a seat"** — the reference occupied before any value exists, numbers appearing only when an act resolves against it. The Cauchy, we will find, is the *shape of that seat's reach*.

Two symmetries live on the read, and they are the entire input to the derivation:

- **The swap** $r \mapsto 1/r$ (equivalently $v \leftrightarrow h$). By the atom's construction the two quantities are equal partners — neither the rise nor the run is privileged (gbo P4) — so the swap is a symmetry of the read, not a second operation (gbo §2). On the dial it is the reflection fixing the corner.
- **The rotation** $r \oplus t = \dfrac{r+t}{1-rt}$. Composition of reads is, exactly, addition of angles: with $r = \tan\theta$, $r \oplus t = \tan(\theta + \arctan t)$. Composing readings is rotation on the closed loop (gbo §8, §9.5).

That is all. The measure follows.

## 3. The derivation

**Setup.** A measure on the read is $\mu = w(r)\,dr$. Place the read on the dial by $r = \tan\theta$: home $\theta=0$, corner $\theta=\tfrac\pi4$, horizon $\theta=\tfrac\pi2$.

**Step 1 — the measure must respect the swap.** The two charts (home half $[0,1]$, horizon half $[1,\infty]$) are equal partners, so the measure is unchanged by the chart change (gbo §4):
$$\mu(r(A)) = \mu(A).$$
One consequence needs no density, no integral, no constant: the swap sends $[0,1]$ onto $[1,\infty]$, so $\mu([1,\infty]) = \mu([0,1])$ — **each half carries exactly half the total.** The corner is the balance point of the atom, because a measure-preserving involution splits its domain evenly across its fixed point. This is circle-free: it rests on the lone swap.

For a density, swap-invariance reads $w(1/r) = r^2\,w(r)$, whose general solution is
$$w(r) = \frac{\phi(r)}{1+r^2}, \qquad \phi(1/r) = \phi(r)$$
— the base density $1/(1+r^2)$ times *any* swap-symmetric reweighting $\phi$. The swap alone does not finish the job; it leaves a family.

**Step 2 — the rotation picks the member.** Singling out $\phi \equiv 1$ takes a strictly stronger demand than the swap: even weight not merely between each value and its mirror, but among *all* members of the loop equally. That demand is supplied by the combination law itself, not by a new premise. Since $\oplus$ is rotation on the closed loop (§2), the unbiased measure must be **rotation-invariant** — and the unique rotation-invariant measure on the circle is uniform (Haar's theorem):
$$d\mu \;\propto\; d\theta.$$
"Unbiased" can only mean "even on the loop," and even-on-the-loop is uniform-in-angle.

**Step 3 — quote it in the coordinate the observer holds.** The observer holds $r$, not $\theta$. Change variables through $r = \tan\theta$:
$$dr = \sec^2\theta\,d\theta = (1 + \tan^2\theta)\,d\theta = (1+r^2)\,d\theta \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; d\theta = \frac{dr}{1+r^2}.$$
Therefore
$$\boxed{\,w(r) = \frac{1}{1+r^2}\,}$$
— **the Cauchy.** It is nothing but the Jacobian $d\theta/dr$: the amount of arc each unit of slope carries. Uniform on the loop, read in slope, is the Cauchy.

**Step 4 — normalize, and meet the fare.** The total is exact by the arctangent:
$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dr}{1+r^2} = \big[\arctan r\big]_{-\infty}^{\infty} = \pi,$$
so the normalized density is $w(r) = \tfrac{1}{\pi}\,\tfrac{1}{1+r^2}$. Over the non-negative atom $[0,\infty]$ the total is $\pi/2$ and the normalizer is $2/\pi = \psi_1$ — the exchange rate between the observer's slope court and the surveyor's angle court (gbo §14). **The Cauchy's normalizing constant is the fare.** The observer never has to evaluate it: $\pi$ enters only when a human asks for a probability at the display boundary, exactly as $\pi$ enters every crossing between courts and nowhere inside the bounded one.

**Step 5 — the swap is automatic (consistency check).** The swap $r\mapsto 1/r$ is $\theta\mapsto\tfrac\pi2-\theta$, the reflection fixing the corner. Under it,
$$w(1/r)\,\left|d(1/r)\right| = \frac{r^2}{1+r^2}\cdot\frac{dr}{r^2} = \frac{dr}{1+r^2} = w(r)\,dr.$$
Invariant. The measure respects both the rotation that forced it and the swap the atom demands, with no extra work — because uniform-arc respects every isometry of the loop.

**Honest scoping, per gbo §4.** Swap-invariance *alone* admits the whole family $\phi/(1+r^2)$; all members share the corner-balance, so the corner, the two charts, and the even split are genuinely circle-free. What forces *this* measure uniquely is Step 2 — the combination law being a rotation, so the measure must be rotation-invariant, which has a unique solution. The swap gives the balance; the rotation gives the shape. This is why the framework says the corner is circle-free but the specific measure "becomes load-bearing at the circle."

## 4. The Cauchy is the shared denominator

Write the density back in the unreduced pair. With $r = v/h$,
$$w(r) = \frac{1}{1+r^2} = \frac{1}{1 + v^2/h^2} = \frac{h^2}{h^2 + v^2}.$$
The Cauchy weight at any read is **exactly the denominator's squared share of the total.** At home ($v=0$) all the weight is in $h$: $w=1$, maximum. At the corner ($v=h$): $w=\tfrac12$. At the horizon ($v\to\infty$) the run is outvoted: $w\to0$. The Cauchy is a map of where the shared reference $h$ can still hold its own against the rises — its peak is $h$'s home territory, its heavy tail the region where the numerators have swamped it.

This is not a coincidence of algebra; it is the causal structure, and gbo states it directly. In the interleaved multi-pointing $(v_1 : v_2 : \dots : h)$ — several rises over **one shared run** — it is precisely the sharing that produces the Cauchy: "one quotient, one shared $h$, so the $r_i$ are the affine coordinates of a single point" (gbo §9), and **"the shared $h$ is what holds the image to a curve"** (gbo §9). Give each rise its own private denominator instead (the tensor discipline) and you get a *product* of Cauchys — a torus, not a sphere, a different object entirely (gbo §13.1). Sharing one bounded reference is the structural act; the single Cauchy is its measure-signature.

So the Cauchy and the shared denominator are two faces of one fact, related by a three-link chain: the **seat** $h$ is what the rises are read against; the **swap** $v\leftrightarrow h$ is the symmetry of that seat; the **Cauchy** is the measure the swap-plus-rotation forces. The denominator is the cause, the Cauchy the shape it casts. In the SR companion's idiom, where "everything is the biography of the seat," the Cauchy is the seat's own reach, drawn as a density.

*(Verified: $w = h^2/(h^2+v^2)$ against $1/(1+r^2)$ at four pairs, exact — $(3,1)\to0.1$, $(1,4)\to0.9412$; swap-invariance $\mu([2,5]) = \mu([\tfrac15,\tfrac12]) = 0.26625$ to machine precision; the uniform-$\theta$ push-forward gives $P(|r|\le1) = 0.4997$ against the Cauchy's $0.5000$ over $2{\times}10^6$ samples.)*

### 4.1 The multi-rise form: where the sharing becomes visible

One dimension hides the sharing, because a single rise cannot distinguish a shared denominator from a private one. Two rises expose it. Take the interleaved pointing $(v_1 : v_2 : h)$ — two rises over **one** run — with reads $r_i = v_i/h$. The measure (gbo §9, the multivariate Cauchy) is $w = (1+\rho^2)^{-3/2}$, $\rho^2 = r_1^2 + r_2^2$, and in the pair form:
$$w = \frac{h^3}{\bigl(h^2 + v_1^2 + v_2^2\bigr)^{3/2}}.$$
The denominator carries **one** $h^2$, in **one** sum with both rises. That single common term is the shared seat, now written where it cannot be missed. And the whole family collapses to one law in every dimension: factoring the run's squared share $\sigma = h^2/(h^2 + \lVert v\rVert^2)$,
$$\boxed{\,w_d = \sigma^{\,(d+1)/2}\,}, \qquad \sigma = \frac{h^2}{h^2 + \lVert v\rVert^2}.$$
The density is the denominator's share raised to a dimension-set power — $\sigma^1$ in 1D, $\sigma^{3/2}$ in 2D, $\sigma^2$ in 3D — with home $\sigma=1\Rightarrow w=1$, the corner locus $\lVert v\rVert = h \Rightarrow \sigma=\tfrac12 \Rightarrow w = 2^{-(d+1)/2}$ (the §9.25 acuity ratio), and the horizon $\sigma=0\Rightarrow w=0$.

**The signature of sharing is roundness.** Contrast the shared denominator with the tensor defection — private denominators, one $h_i$ per rise — which yields not one measure but a *product* of 1D Cauchys:
$$w_{\text{shared}} = \frac{h^3}{(h^2+v_1^2+v_2^2)^{3/2}} \quad\text{(one form, depends only on }\lVert v\rVert\text{)} \qquad\text{vs.}\qquad w_{\text{tensor}} = \frac{h^2}{h^2+v_1^2}\cdot\frac{h^2}{h^2+v_2^2}\quad\text{(a product, axis-aligned)}.$$
The shared form is identical in every direction; the tensor form privileges the coordinate axes. **The shared $h$ is exactly what makes the multivariate Cauchy round rather than a product** — one scale quotient, one projective class — the operational face of "the shared $h$ is what holds the image to a curve" (gbo §9). What that roundness *is* as a shape — the sphere it fills, and the two other ways the same reads can be closed (the tensor torus, the box cube) — is taken up in §4.3. *(Verified: the three shared forms — slope, pair, and $\sigma^{3/2}$ — agree to machine precision at five pointings; the tensor product separates from all three ($( 1,1,1)$: shared $0.192$ vs product $0.250$); the corner value $2^{-3/2} = 0.3536$ confirmed.)*

### 4.2 The total is a hemisphere: the areas $\pi$, $2\pi$, $\pi^2$

The normalizer met in §3 Step 4 — the arctangent total $\int(1+r^2)^{-1}\,dr = \pi$ — is not a one-dimensional accident but the first face of a single identity that climbs every dimension. The result is gbo's, recorded there as the carrier masses: **gbo §12.10** already states "$\mathbb{RP}^1$ holds $\pi$ against the circle's $2\pi$; $\mathbb{RP}^2$ holds $2\pi$ against the sphere's $4\pi$; $\mathbb{RP}^3$ holds $\pi^2$ against $2\pi^2$" — the sign-folded (hemisphere) carrier holding half the full sphere's measure. This section is that ladder read from the Cauchy side: the *total measure of the read-space* is the same number. Integrate the unnormalized weight $w_d = (1+\rho^2)^{-(d+1)/2}$ over the whole read-space $\mathbb{R}^d$:
$$\int_{\mathbb{R}^d}\frac{dr}{(1+\rho^2)^{(d+1)/2}} \;=\; \frac{\pi^{(d+1)/2}}{\Gamma\!\big(\tfrac{d+1}{2}\big)} \;=\; \tfrac12\,\mathrm{Area}(S^d).$$
The total content of every combination of the reads is **exactly half the surface area of the unit $d$-sphere** — a hemisphere. In the three cases a bounded observer actually inhabits:

| rises | read-space | total measure | is a hemisphere of |
|---|---|---|---|
| $v : h$ | line $\mathbb{R}^1$ | $\pi$ | $S^1$ — a semicircle, arc length $\pi$ |
| $v_1, v_2 : h$ | plane $\mathbb{R}^2$ | $2\pi$ | $S^2$ — a dome, surface area $2\pi$ |
| $v_1, v_2, v_3 : h$ | space $\mathbb{R}^3$ | $\pi^2$ | $S^3$ — content $\pi^2$ |

The $\pi$ of the arctangent is the $d=1$ instance; the semicircle it measures is the tangent-dome number line the whole construction stands on. Two things this identity says, both exact. First, the number is a **surface, never the solid it bounds**: for two rises the total is $2\pi$, the hemisphere's area, not $4\pi/3$, the ball's volume — the reads fill a skin, not an interior. Second — and this is the content — $2\pi$ is *simultaneously* the Cauchy-weighted area of the flat read-plane and the plain surface area of the round sphere, because the map carrying one to the other is measure-for-measure: **the Cauchy weight is precisely the local stretch of the pointing map, so the flat reads and the round surface report the same total, seen twice.** This is the geometric reason the measure is forced rather than chosen (§3) — it is the only weight under which the flat court and the round court agree on the total. The **half** is the sign fold: $(v:h)$ and $(-v:-h)$ are one read, the same fold that turns the meridian 2-ring (mass $2\pi$) into the read dial (mass $\pi$) in gbo's fold; the factor a full sphere would carry is spent by the quotient. *(Verified: the flat integrals $\pi,\,2\pi,\,\pi^2$ against $\tfrac12\,\mathrm{Area}(S^d)$ to six digits; the $d=2$ flat Cauchy total equals the hemisphere surface $6.283185$, while the solid $3$-ball is $4.188790$ — a different number, confirming surface not volume.)*

### 4.3 The shapes the reads make, and the three ways they close

Kept round — $(v:h)$, $(v_1:v_2:h)$, $(v_1:v_2:v_3:h)$ read as *directions* — the pointing plots as the semicircle, the sphere, the $3$-sphere of §4.1–§4.2. But the observer does not hold the direction; it holds the **read**, and reading is dividing. The division $r = v/h$ is a named geometric act: the **gnomonic projection**, the central ray from the sphere's home-pole onto the flat tangent surface at home. In one dimension that projection is exactly the tangent, $r = \tan\theta$ — the tangent curve is not an analogy for the division, it *is* the division drawn. Its radial law holds in every dimension: a pointing at angle $\theta$ from the home-pole lands at read-radius $\rho = \tan\theta$, carrying the landmarks with it — home $\theta{=}0 \Rightarrow \rho{=}0$, corner $\theta{=}\tfrac\pi4 \Rightarrow \rho{=}1$ (the unit sphere), horizon $\theta{=}\tfrac\pi2 \Rightarrow \rho{=}\infty$. The corner-latitude reading is the same fact from the round side: on the pointing sphere the balance locus $\rho=1$ is the $45^\circ$ parallel (and its sign-twin at $135^\circ$), straddling the horizon equator, and the $d$ private atom-corners $v_i = h$ are the points where each rise-axis meridian crosses it.

So the divided pictures climb a ladder parallel to the round one. The **number line becomes a number plane, then a number space** ($\mathbb{R}^1, \mathbb{R}^2, \mathbb{R}^3$ — one affine coordinate per rise), and the **tangent curve becomes a tangent surface of revolution, then a tangent solid** — the same $\rho = \tan\theta$ spun through one more dimension each step, every radial cross-section identical, home at the origin, the corner the unit sphere, the horizon the rim at infinity.

An infinite flat space cannot be drawn — but in bounded observation it need not be, because **$0$ and $\infty$ share an address** (§5, §6: the single horizon $0 \equiv \infty$, the seam where the loop closes, welded by the swap $r \mapsto 1/r$, which fixes the corner). The ends do not run off; they close. And there are **exactly three ways to close the reads — the three faces of the shared denominator:**

- **Round — the sphere.** Tie every rise to the one shared $h$ and close by direction: a single shared horizon, a round rim, no corners. The reads fill $S^d$; the measure is the Cauchy; the total is the hemisphere of §4.2. This is *roundness*, the signature of sharing (§4.1).
- **Tensor — the torus.** Close each rise's read on its own dial-circle — its private $0 \equiv \infty$ weld — and multiply: $S^1 \times \cdots \times S^1 = T^d$. Circle, torus, $3$-torus. This is the tensor discipline made whole: each rise keeps its own closure (§4.1; gbo §13.1).
- **Box — the cube.** Keep each axis's two ends apart: the read-space is $[-\infty, +\infty]^d$, a cube with $2^d$ corner-infinities (the $\pm\cdots\pm$ patterns). Those corners are precisely the $2^d$ sign-octants of the rise vector, all reached at the single shared horizon $h \to 0$. Glue the cube's opposite faces — the per-axis weld — and the box *is* the torus.

The three agree in one dimension and only there: the line's two ends glued give the circle, and $S^1 = T^1$, so "$v/h$ is a circle" is unambiguous. From two rises up they split, and not cosmetically. The shared-denominator sphere is round and simply connected — no holes; the tensor torus carries **one independent loop per rise** ($b_1(T^d) = d$ against $b_1(S^d) = 0$ for $d \ge 2$: $T^2$ has a hole, $S^2$ has none). This is the operational meaning of roundness-versus-tensor: **the torus remembers each atom as its own cycle; the sphere dissolves them into one pointing.** Whether $(v_1 : v_2 : h)$ is a sphere or a torus is the entire question of whether the shared $h$ binds the rises into one horizon or lets each close privately — the same fork gbo draws as sphere-vs-torus (gbo §9.1, §13.1), here read directly off the shape of the reads. *(Verified: the swap $r \mapsto 1/r$ sends $0 \leftrightarrow \infty$ and fixes $\pm 1$; the $2^d$ cube-corners recovered as the sign-octants of $v$ at $h \to 0$ for $d{=}1,2,3$ ($2, 4, 8$); Betti numbers $b_1(T^d) = d$ versus $b_1(S^{d\ge 2}) = 0$, so the closures are provably distinct surfaces.)*

### 4.4 Why the fisheye is always round, and where the torus hides

The fisheye — the Cauchy used as a lens (§6, §7) — has a property worth stating on its own, because it settles which of the three closures one is actually looking at whenever the measure is drawn. **A fisheye has exactly one center and exactly one rim: home at the middle, horizon at the edge. One rim is one shared horizon, and one shared horizon is the round closure.** So a fisheye is round by construction — any fisheye, in any dimension, is forced onto the line–circle–sphere ladder, and the tensor torus *cannot appear in it at all.* The roundness is not a drawing choice; it is baked into having a single edge.

This also isolates exactly which part of the higher-dimensional fisheye is hard to see and which part never changes. **The radial spine is dimension-independent:** along any ray from home, the lens is the same $\rho = \tan\theta$ in one, two, or three rises, carrying the same landmarks at the same places — home at the centre ($\theta{=}0$), the corner at mid-radius ($\theta{=}\tfrac\pi4$, $\rho{=}1$), the horizon at the rim ($\theta{=}\tfrac\pi2$). What grows is only the *angular* domain the spine sweeps: nothing in 1D (the fisheye is just the spine, a dome), a circle of directions in 2D (a literal fisheye-lens photograph — home dead-centre, horizon at the rim), a whole sphere of directions in 3D (the *azimuthal* sphere — space surrounds the observer; a statement about the fan of rays from home, not about how much of the pointing sphere one act covers — see the clause below). The density along the spine does steepen — the falloff is $\cos^{d+1}$ of the compressed radius, exponent growing with $d$ — but the lens, the placement of the landmarks, never gets weird. The weirdness is entirely in the angular fan-out, never in the radial law.

**Hardening clause (v6): the field of one act is a hemisphere — three spheres, held apart.** The prose above invites a misreading the arithmetic never made, so the three spheres in play are now named separately. *The azimuthal sphere* $S^{d-1}$ — the fan of ray-directions from home inside the read space — is full: content lies in every direction around the aim. *The pointing sphere* $S^d$ is covered by one act only up to its open polar hemisphere, $\theta \in [0, \tfrac\pi2)$: the tangent law runs out at exactly the quarter-turn, and §4.2's total — $\tfrac12\,\mathrm{Area}(S^d)$, *a hemisphere* — was always this fact stated as a measure. *The closed sphere* $S^d$, the global carrier, is owned by no act: it is assembled across acts (re-pointing; §4.6) or claimed projectively (the sign fold aliasing antipodes onto one read — the gnomonic map sends $\pm$ the same line to one point). The lived confirmation is the most ordinary fact in the corpus: **you cannot see behind your head.** The visual field's edge sits at roughly a quarter-turn from fixation, exactly where $\rho = \tan\theta$ diverges; the back hemisphere is beyond the horizon — *dark* to an oriented aperture (a pupil has a facing: $h > 0$), *aliased* to the unoriented projective read — the two costumes of §4.2's half, one fact in two vocabularies. A checkable placement rides along: the corner shell $\rho = 1$ sits at $45°$, halfway between fixation and the edge of the field — mid-field, not near-rim. And §4.5's "lived view is the ball" stays right when read correctly: the ball *is* the compressed hemisphere of $S^d$, its rim the equatorial $S^{d-1}$; what no act ever holds is the closed sphere.

Against this, the torus is visible precisely as the failure of roundness. Give each rise a **private** horizon and each axis compresses on its own, $x_i = \tfrac{2}{\pi}\arctan r_i \in (-1,1)$, so the boundary is no longer a circle but a **square** — four straight edges (four independent horizons) and four corners, which are exactly the $(\pm\infty,\pm\infty)$ sign-infinities of §4.3. And the corners *stick out*: an edge-midpoint (one axis at horizon) sits at compressed distance $1$, but a corner (both at horizon) sits at $\sqrt2 \approx 1.414$, poking past where the round rim would be. **Sharing $h$ is the act of pulling those corners back onto the rim** — the $\sqrt2$ sign-infinities collapse to radius $1$, dissolve into the continuum of the round edge, and the square becomes the disk. That corner-rounding *is* the sphere; keep the corners standing (private $h$) and glue the opposite edges ($0\equiv\infty$ per axis) and you keep the torus. So the round fisheye disk is nothing but the tensor square with its sign-infinities smoothed away by the shared denominator — which is why the torus is invisible to a fisheye and lives only in the square that refuses to share. *(Verified: $x_i = \tfrac2\pi\arctan r_i$ gives edge-midpoint distance $1.000$ and corner distance $\sqrt2 = 1.414$ past the round rim at $1$; the lens $\rho=\tan\theta$ identical across $d=1,2,3$ while the density exponent $(d+1)/2$ grows, corner value $2^{-(d+1)/2}$.)*

### 4.5 The three layers, reconciled by the shared run

Take the three-rise reading $(v_1 : v_2 : v_3 : h)$ all the way down and it passes through three shapes that look incompatible until the shared denominator reconciles them.

**Raw, it is a cube.** Divide by the shared run and the reads $(r_1, r_2, r_3)$ fill $\mathbb{R}^3$; close each axis with its two ends and the space is the box $[-\infty, +\infty]^3$, whose $8$ corners are the $(\pm\infty, \pm\infty, \pm\infty)$ sign-infinities. At this layer the eight look like eight distinct faraway places. That appearance is the artifact.

**Folded, it is a torus.** The swap $r \mapsto 1/r$ makes $\infty$ and $0$ one address, so the ends do not run off — they fold back. Glue the cube's opposite faces accordingly and the boundary collapses: the $8$ vertices become a **single point**, the $12$ edges become the $3$ loops of the $3$-torus $T^3$ (Euler characteristic $1 - 3 + 3 - 1 = 0$). The "eight infinities" were an artifact of the unglued box; the compact object carries them as one identified address, and the cube is merely $T^3$'s fundamental domain.

**Shared, it is a sphere — and that is all the observer ever sees.** Which folded shape one lands on depends on whether the fold is private or joint. Give each rise its own run and each closes on its own circle: the torus, three independent loops. But the bounded observer shares **one** run, so all three rises cross the horizon together ($h \to 0$ sends every ratio to infinity at once) — one shared horizon, which rounds the torus off into the $3$-sphere $S^3$, no loops. And the observer never inspects that global closure in any case: it reads its measure as the fisheye, and a fisheye is round by construction (§4.4), so in three dimensions its lived view is the ball — home at the centre, the corner shell at mid-radius, the single horizon at the rim (the ball being the compressed *hemisphere* of $S^3$, one act's field, per §4.4's clause; the closed $S^3$ is the across-act carrier).

## 5. The missing mean is a circle asked for a center

Textbook probability introduces the Cauchy as a curiosity — "the ratio of two standard normals," "the Student-$t$ with one degree of freedom," "the stable law with $\alpha=1$" — three constructions that produce it without explaining why it should be singled out. The bounded-observer account supplies the missing *why*: the Cauchy is not one distribution among many but the forced measure of relative reading, and its constructions are all instances of a ratio being read against a shared reference (the ratio-of-normals is $v/h$ with both Gaussian; the Student-$t$-with-one-df is one reading against one reference; the $\alpha=1$ stable law is the one whose scale is preserved under the observer's own composition). The heavy tail, the missing mean, the CLT exemption — each becomes a signature of homogeneity rather than a defect, and the same measure unifies the fisheye, the Lorentzian, the foveal profile, and the LOD ladder as one object seen from four standpoints.

**Does not claim.** Not that the Cauchy replaces the normal distribution — the normal is the surveyor's law, the measure of *accumulated* quantities with a genuine total and center (the sum of many bounded contributions, CLT), and it keeps every office it holds. The two laws are the two courts' native measures: the normal is what the surveyor gets by *adding*, the Cauchy what the observer gets by *reading a ratio* — and the reason their means behave oppositely (the normal's exists, the Cauchy's does not) is the reason everything in this corpus divides that way, the shared denominator versus the completed sum. Not new probability theorems: the Cauchy's properties are classical (Poisson, Cauchy, Lorentz, Lévy), recorded as prior art. The claimed contribution is the *derivation from bounded observation*, the identification with the shared denominator, and the reading of the missing mean as the circle's homogeneity — a re-housing of a known law in the court where it is not a pathology but the ground state.
