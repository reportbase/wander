# The Radix
## What a base costs a reader that browses

*(Title provisional, and the third in a row. The first two — "What a base is, to a reader that
browses", then "Why a reader that was given nothing browses in twos" — each promised something the
paper then disproved. The binary case is not privileged and the paper says so throughout; a title
that leads with it fights its own argument. What survives is the accounting, so the title is the
accounting.)*

*A research record in the calibration-free series, drawing on Browsing the Fisheye, The Unit Rule
and The Relation Space, which it takes as read. Unreviewed; the title is provisional.*

*Revised 1 October 2026: the text is left as it was, and notes marked* **(1 Oct)** *say where the rulings of 30 September – 1 October (R124–R152 of* Reader Geometry as Addressing*) bear on it. Its accounting, the grid and the floor stand; little here conflicts with them.*

> - **Names now in use.** "Fulcrum" → **the corner**; "the view from everywhere" → **the view from nowhere**; "inside/outside" → **serial/parallel**; "density" is dropped as a framework word (the Cauchy's probability density keeps its name). Quotes keep their wording.

*A reader's radius decides how much of a list is in reach. An address written in a fixed radix has a
digit for every level of that list. This paper is about the one relation between them: the radius
selects a digit — and it selects exactly one when the address is written in the base the reader's own
GRAIN sets. The lens forces the ladder to be a ladder of ratios; the grain says which ratio; the
address either matches it or costs a table.*

*What kind of paper this is, after three adversarial readings: an INSTRUMENT paper and a piece of
accounting, not a foundational claim. The arithmetic in it is ordinary and is meant to be; what it
adds is a way of saying what a base costs the thing doing the reading. The claim, in the form it is
meant to be defended: **a bounded scale-free reader generates a
geometric ladder of the scales it can reach, and its own logarithmic grain sets that ladder's ratio.
A fixed-radix address generates a second ladder. Where the address's base equals the reader's ratio,
levels and rungs become one unit: one change of radius selects one level, and one step of the reader
is an exact operation on the address. Where the address is mixed-radix, no single grain can produce
that correspondence, and the schedule of its levels must be supplied from outside if the reader is to
move by level at all.** Two things this paper does NOT establish, and says so where it tries: that
base two is forced by bounded observation, and that the tangent is the only possible lens.*

## How this was found

Backwards, as usual, and from a demonstration rather than an argument. A dial that browses a list of
nine quadrillion records was pointed at the record's own decimal digits, eight levels of ten cells,
with the level the radius was steering drawn lit. The lit level slid: it drifted across the tower
and changed only every third rung or so, which looked like sloppiness in the drawing. It was not.
Written in tens, a rung of the dial is 0.301 of a decimal place. Rewritten in twos — the base the
dial's own ladder is in — the lit level stepped by exactly one per rung, nine times in a row without
exception. The sliding had been the base, not the dial.

That is the whole of the paper: **a base is the pitch of an address's ladder, and a reader's own
ladder has a pitch too. Where the two agree, one rung of the reader is one level of the address —
and the reader's pitch is its GRAIN, the step it already moves its held value by.**

The headline moved three times while the paper was written, and all three are kept because they are
the argument. It began as *a base is a choice, and there is one base per reader*. §5 removed the choice
— a radius slides the corner ladder and cannot re-space it — and the claim became *one base per
lens*. Then the lens claim was attacked in its turn and did not survive whole: the map forces the
ladder to be a ratio ladder, but every ratio is admissible, so what picks the ratio is not the lens.
It is the register's grain. **The matched radix is the reader's own grain, and the doublings in this
dial are there because it was built with a grain of ln 2.**

The third move is §9's, and it runs the other way. Having found that the lens does not fix the base,
the paper asked what fixes the lens — and three geometric attempts failed before the answer came from
the reader's register rather than from any geometry: **of the candidates tried, the tangent is the
only one whose query is the arrival itself, so any other costs the reader a primitive it was not
given.** Which makes the
whole chain conditional on the corpus's oldest condition rather than on a theorem about maps.

## 1. The two ladders

> **(1 Oct)** "Corner" in this paper means a rung of the dial's ladder, one unit out and each further one a ratio beyond. Since 1 October "the corner" alone means v = h, one h, the edge of the working horizon (R149). The dial's ladder of ratios is R149's back side, counted by doublings; the front side, inside the first corner, is swept in plain proportion.

A reader on a list has a **radius** and a **unit**. The radius sets the window — how much of the list
is in reach at once — and the unit is one corner out on the dial, a count of records. The corners
beyond the first stand in a fixed *ratio* to it, which is what makes the reader's ladder a ladder of
rungs. In the instrument this paper was measured on that ratio is two, and §5 says where the two
comes from: not from the lens, which fixes only that the corners be in some ratio, but from the
reader's own grain.

An address in a fixed radix has a **level** per digit. The first digit picks one of *b* regions of
the list, the second one of *b* districts inside it, and so on, where *b* is the base. Every level
is a fixed pitch, so a digit does not move when the list grows — the whole point of fixed-radix
addressing, and the reason a calendar can name a moment without renaming it later (Browsing §9).

These are two ladders over the same list, and until they are compared there is no reason to expect
them to agree. Note already where each pitch comes from. The address's base is chosen by whoever
writes the address. The reader's rungs are *ratios* because its map is scale-free, which is the
lens's doing and not negotiable (§5) — but the size of the ratio is the reader's grain, the step it
moves its held value by, and that is the reader's own.

## 2. The relation between them

> **(1 Oct)** The dial here browses a list of known length (2^53 − 1). A known list is situation 1 or 2, read through the fisheye as a simulation of situation 3 (R143); a situated reader knows neither N nor v's breadth.

The dial's unit at a radius *r* on a list of *N* is one corner out:

    unit = N^r / 2^(K+1)     records,   K the rungs the window's half is divided into

and the level an address hands the hand is the one whose place-value the unit lands on:

    level = log_b(unit)

So **one rung of the dial — a doubling of the unit — moves the steered level by log_b 2 of a place.**
That single quantity decides everything else:

| the base | a rung is | rungs per place |
|---|---|---|
| two | 1.000000 of a place | 1.000 |
| ten | 0.301030 of a place | 3.322 |

In base two the two ladders are the same ladder. In base ten no whole number of rungs makes a level —
log₂10 is irrational, so the rungs never periodically land on a digit boundary — and the steered digit
slides. (The word to avoid here is *incommensurable*: there is a perfectly precise relation between
them, and it is 3.321928 rungs to the decimal place.)

**Measured, on the page rather than on paper.** Stepping the window down one rung at a time from
full radius and reading the lit level off the drawing itself:

| rung | the unit, in records | base two | base ten |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 140,737,488,355,328 | 2^47 | 1e14 |
| 1 | 70,368,744,177,664 | 2^46 (−1) | 1e13 (−1) |
| 2 | 35,184,372,088,832 | 2^45 (−1) | 1e13 (0) |
| 3 | 17,592,186,044,416 | 2^44 (−1) | 1e13 (0) |
| 4 | 8,796,093,022,208 | 2^43 (−1) | 1e12 (−1) |
| 5 | 4,398,046,511,104 | 2^42 (−1) | 1e12 (0) |
| 6 | 2,199,023,255,552 | 2^41 (−1) | 1e12 (0) |
| 7 | 1,099,511,627,776 | 2^40 (−1) | 1e12 (0) |
| 8 | 549,755,813,888 | 2^39 (−1) | 1e11 (−1) |
| 9 | 274,877,906,944 | 2^38 (−1) | 1e11 (0) |

Nine rungs, nine steps of exactly one in base two; four changes in ten, unevenly spaced. The
prediction was written before the base was changed and the page then produced it.

## 3. From a correspondence to a control

Two ladders lining up is a correspondence. It becomes a **control** when a reader can hold a level,
step exactly one of them, and come back to the address it started from — and that is a further claim,
because a rung being *near* a step of the address is not the same as its *being* one.

Predicted from the arithmetic before it was built, and then measured on the instrument: a step of
2^b, taken at the radius that steers bit b, moves the record by exactly 2^b, and out-and-back returns
the address exactly.

| the radius | the bit it steers | the step it takes | out and back |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.00 | 2^47 | +140,737,488,355,328 | exact |
| 0.68 | 2^30 | +1,073,741,824 | exact |
| 0.49 | 2^20 | +1,048,576 | exact |
| 0.30 | 2^10 | +1,024 | exact |
| 0.17 | 2^3 | +8 | exact |
| 0.08 | 2^0 | +1 | exact |

**And the carry is the address's own event, not the dial's.** A step at bit b touches bit b and
those below it, and leaves every higher level alone — unless it carries, and a carry is exactly the
act of re-entering the level above. Which levels a single step disturbs is therefore a fact about
where the address already stood, not about how it was steered:

| the address stepped at 2^20 | levels disturbed |
|---|---|
| one and then zeros | 1 of 53 |
| a typical address | 6 of 53 |
| all ones | 33 of 53 |

This is what a reader gets from a matched radix that it does not get from an unmatched one: a step
that is a level, and a carry it can see.

## 4. What this says a base is

**First, what is not being claimed, and it should be conceded further than politeness requires.**
That log_b(e^σ) decides how many rungs make a level is ordinary mathematics: a geometric progression
is an arithmetic one under logarithms, and nobody needs this paper to know it. More than that: **once
the reader is defined to move in fixed steps of σ and the address to have fixed places of b, their
alignment is DEFINITIONAL.** The grid in §11 and the detuning table are not discoveries; they are the
arithmetic, confirmed on an instrument that could have been built wrong and was not. No measurement
in this paper could have come out otherwise unless the code was mistaken — and some of it was, which
is the only reason measuring was worth the time. The claim is not that the relation is new. It is that **the two ladders are
generated independently — one by whoever wrote the address, one by the reader's own step — and that
whether they agree is therefore a fact about the pair, not a property of either.** Separated:

| ordinary mathematics | what this paper adds |
|---|---|
| a scale multiplies a geometric ladder and preserves its ratio | so a bounded reader's accessible scales form a ladder it cannot re-space |
| a logarithmic step σ corresponds to a ratio e^σ | so the reader's own grain, fixed for other reasons, sets that ratio |
| log_b(r) rungs make one level of base b | so alignment is a **purchase**: free at one base, a table at a mixed one, and 3.32 rungs of wasted travel at ten |

Stated that way the result is an observer-model one, and the arithmetic underneath it is deliberately
ordinary. A reader who takes this paper to be announcing a new fact about logarithms has read it
wrongly; one who takes it to be about what a base costs a browsing reader has read it as intended.

A base is usually treated as a notation — a way of writing a number down, with nothing at stake but
convenience. For a reader that browses, it is not a notation. It is **the pitch of the address's
ladder**, and it either matches the reader's own or it does not.

The consequences are ordinary once said:

- **The radix belongs to the reader, not to the data.** Nothing about a list of nine quadrillion
  records prefers ten or two. What prefers two is the *reader*, whose corners are doublings because
  its grain is ln 2 — and a reader with another grain would prefer another base, exactly (§5).
- **A matched radix makes zoom into digit selection.** Closing the radius by one rung walks the hand
  down one level of the address, exactly. The gearbox and fixed-pitch addressing — Browsing §7 and
  its §9 — turn out to be one structure seen at two pitches, and this is the join.
- **A mismatched radix is not wrong, only unaligned.** Base ten works; its levels simply do not
  correspond one-for-one with the reader's, so the hand crosses three rungs to move one digit and
  spends two of them steering nothing new.

**And this is why both of the corpus's towers worked.** The calendar tower is mixed radix
(100/12/31/24/60/60) and the hash ring is base 2^32, and neither was chosen for the reader's sake.
Each works because its levels are *fixed*. What the hash ring also gets, and the calendar cannot, is
alignment: 2^32 is a power of the dial's own ratio, so its levels are whole rungs apart. §5 shows
that the calendar's are not, and that no choice of grain would make them so.

## 5. Whether the ladder itself can be mixed

> **(1 Oct)** A grain of ln 2, one doubling per rung, is the same count R147 found for v's breadth: assuming v = 2h slides the whole reading one rung, and situation 5 knows that offset within bounds (R151). The "uniform ratio ladder" is the back side's; R149 makes the front side plain proportion.

The corpus's own towers are mixed radix — the calendar is 100/12/31/24/60/60 — so the obvious next
question is whether a reader can be built whose *rungs* are those levels, rather than one whose rungs
are octaves and whose levels therefore do not fit. The answer is no, and the reason divides in two:
the lens settles what *kind* of ladder a reader can have, and the grain settles its *size*. Neither
allows an uneven one.

**What the lens forces is that the ladder is a ratio ladder.** The browse's map is p = s·tan(θ − φ),
and it has exactly one parameter, the scale s. A scale multiplies every corner by the same factor,
so a ladder survives a change of radius only if its corners are related by *ratios*: a geometric
ladder at 1, r, r², r³ keeps its ratio at any radius, and an arithmetic one at 1, 2, 3, 4 does not —
scaling by 7.3 turns its step of one into a step of 7.3. **A radius slides the ladder; it cannot
re-space it, and only a ratio ladder is slideable at all.**

**But the lens does not fix which ratio.** Corners at 1, 3, 9, 27 are as closed under scaling as
corners at 1, 2, 4, 8, and so are 1, e, e², e³. Every geometric ratio survives; the map has nothing
to say about which. So the claim that the lens fixes the base is wrong, and it was worth attacking
rather than asserting.

**What fixes the ratio is the reader's grain.** A reader that holds a value and moves it by whole
steps of σ in the log — the unit rule's own rung — has a ladder whose corners are at ratio e^σ. That
is where the doublings came from: the dial was built with σ = ln 2. Take the grain elsewhere and the
ladder goes with it, and so does the matched base:

| the reader's grain σ | its ladder's ratio | the radix it matches |
|---|---|---|
| ln 2 | 2.0000 | two |
| ln 3 | 3.0000 | three |
| ln 10 | 10.0000 | ten |
| 1 | 2.7183 | e |

Checked directly: rungs per level of the address is log(base)/σ, which is 1.000000 whenever the base
is the ladder's own ratio, and 3.321928 for the mismatch we started from (grain 2, address in tens).

**So the corrected statement is this: the lens forces a ratio ladder, and the reader's grain picks
the ratio.** Said carefully, because the two are not the same kind of thing: **the grain σ is
logarithmic and the ladder's ratio is its exponential, r = e^σ.** A reader's grain is a step in the
log; the ladder it generates has ratio r; and *when r is the base an address is written in*, one rung
is one level. The compressed form — "the matched radix is the reader's grain" — is worth keeping in
mind and not in an argument: the grain determines the radix through exponentiation and is not itself
the radix. The table's last row makes the difference plain, since a grain of 1 gives a ratio of e,
which is a perfectly good scale ratio and not a positional base at all.

**And a mixed address is beyond any grain, which is what survives of the original claim.** A single
grain gives a *uniform* ratio ladder, and the calendar's levels are not in a uniform ratio: 60, 60,
24, 30, 12. Even a reader built with a grain of sixty — matching the two sixties exactly — is left
0.224, 0.169 and 0.393 of a rung out on the day, the month and the year. At grain two the misfits
run 0.093 to 0.415; at grain ten, 0.079 to 0.477. **No choice of grain matches a mixed address**,
because the mismatch is between something uneven and something that cannot be.

**What can be mixed is the schedule, not the ladder.** Sliding is enough to put any one chosen unit
on a corner: a reader can sit at the radius whose unit is a second, another whose unit is a minute,
another an hour. Each *setting* is then a level of a mixed address, even though the rungs between
settings are not levels. Measured on a century of milliseconds, the calendar's levels sit 5.907,
5.907, 4.585, 4.907, 3.605 and 6.645 rungs apart, the worst of them 0.415 of a rung off a whole one.

**And the price is exactly the thing base two buys.** In a matched radix a level is one corner: the
hand steps and arrives, and can count its way anywhere in the address. In a mixed one the hand must
be *told* where to stop, because the gaps are not whole rungs and nothing about the ladder says
where the next level is. **The schedule becomes data** — a table the reader must be given, alongside
the address, in order to browse it — and a reader given nothing cannot browse a mixed address by
level at all.

That is the sharper result the question was worth asking for: not that a mixed address is unreadable,
but that **its levels cost a table, and a matched one costs nothing.**

## 6. Whether this is the corpus's own condition, or only its echo

§5 ends on a sentence that sounds like the framework's oldest one: a reader given nothing cannot
browse a mixed address by level. The Unit Rule's condition is that the reader has been handed no
number, holds no second value, and can run only what those two facts allow. Whether §5 is an
instance of that or a rhyme is worth settling, because the two readings put this paper in different
places.

**It is an instance, and the test is the usual one: what would have to be handed over.** To browse a
mixed address by level, a reader needs the schedule — that a minute is sixty seconds, an hour sixty
minutes, a day twenty-four hours. Those are numbers, and they are numbers *about the writer's
arrangement* rather than about the reader's own unit. A reader that has them has been handed
something, and by the Unit Rule's accounting it is no longer in the given-nothing case. A matched
radix asks for nothing of the kind: the reader counts its own rungs, and each one is a level because
its own ladder says so.

**The sharper form, and it is the same shape as the corpus's other purchases.** The framework prices
what a reader cannot have for free — a unit costs a journey, testimony costs a unit, a frame costs
three bodies. This adds a line to that list: **the levels of a mixed address cost a table**, and the
table is supplied by the writer. Where the address is in the reader's own base the same levels cost
nothing, because the reader's ladder already has them.

**Two cautions against overreading it, and the first is blunter than the framing elsewhere.** The
schedule is SIX INTEGERS. Most systems that handle calendars already carry them, hard-coded, and
nobody notices the expense. So although the accounting is right — a uniform-grain reader cannot
derive them and must be told — **the practical severity is near zero, and the words "purchase" and
"bill" make it sound larger than it is.** What the distinction buys is not a warning against mixed
radix; it is a way of saying precisely WHERE the unevenness lives, which is a bookkeeping result
rather than a prohibition. Second, a reader given nothing is not thereby
*unable* to browse a mixed address: it can still pan, zoom and read every record. What it cannot do
is move by level, which is a loss of one capability and not of access.

## 7. The floor

An address written in a fixed radix has as many levels as it has digits, and no more. A reader can
close its radius past the last of them, and then a pan moves less than one record — which is to say
it moves nothing.

Where that floor sits is a property of the number the address is *kept in*, not of the address. In a
double, the grain is one whole record up to 2^53 and coarsens beyond it:

| at | the smallest step a double can take |
|---|---|
| 10^3 | 1 |
| 10^9 | 1 |
| 4.5 × 10^15 | 1 |
| 9.0 × 10^15 | 1 |
| 1.8 × 10^16 | 2 |
| 3.6 × 10^16 | 4 |

So a list of 2^53 − 1 = 9,007,199,254,740,991 is exactly the largest whose every address a double
can still name. Past it the number itself acquires a radix point in the wrong place: the address
stops being an address and becomes a measurement, with its own grain, and the reader's rungs run out
before the address does.

**The remedy is the one fixed-radix addressing always had: keep the digits, not the number.** An
address held as a string of digits has no floor at all; an address held as a number inherits the
floor of the number.

**And this is not a corner case; it is where the work actually broke, five times.** Every one was
found by a symptom on a screen and traced back afterwards, which is worth more than the argument:

| what was built | what it did | the fault |
|---|---|---|
| a loop walking the road by `i++` | the page froze solid | at 2^53, `first + 1 === first`, so the loop never advanced |
| a pan at a fine radius | nothing moved at all | it asked for 0.034 of a record where the grain is 1 |
| a steady roll | the road stood still | a frame's walk, 0.0092 of a record, was added to the record and lost |
| the position handed over as one number | the stroll advanced in half-sleeper jumps | `index + frac` kept 0.5 of a fraction of 0.37 |
| seeds made as `record × 2246822519 + salt` | the two sides of a street were the same building | the product is 9 × 10^18, where `+3` and `+7` are the same number |

The common shape is one sentence: **a number was asked to hold something finer than its own grain — a
fraction, a salt, a step of one — and it silently did not.** And that sentence is the reason this
section belongs in this paper rather than in a note on floating point. What a double has is a GRAIN,
in the same sense the reader has one: a step below which it cannot go, coarsening as the value grows.
So the address ladder does not end at the reader's floor — it runs on until it meets **the grain of
the container it is kept in**, and everything above is a second reader, unasked for, with its own
rungs. None of the arithmetic here is novel; what it shows is the paper's own quantity turning up one
level down, where nobody put it. Each cure was the same, and each is the
paper's own rule: keep the record whole and the fraction beside it; count the marks and never the
record; split the address before hashing it. The floor is not a limit on how long a list may be. It
is a limit on what may be done to an address *as a number*, and everything above it is arithmetic
that a fixed-radix address does not need.

## 8. What it does not say

- **It is not a claim that base two is better.** It is the base that matches *this* reader, whose
  grain is ln 2. A reader with a different grain matches a different base, exactly (§5). What is not
  free is that the ladder be a ladder of ratios at all; that is the lens's.
- **And it is not a claim that the lens is proved unique.** §9 attacked that three times and failed
  each time; what it establishes instead is a price — a non-tangent lens costs the reader a primitive
  it was not given — which is weaker than a uniqueness theorem and is what the evidence supports.
- **The whole chain is conditional, and the condition is the corpus's oldest.** Given nothing, the
  tangent; given a primitive, another lens and another radix. Nothing here says a richer reader is
  impossible, only what it would cost.
- **And "given nothing" is not nothing.** It means no supplied number, no second register, no table —
  while the reader's own structural primitives are not counted as supplied. That distinction is the
  Unit Rule's and was settled before this question was asked, but this paper LEANS on it, and a reader
  who rejects it can reject the chain's first link entirely. The honest statement of the paper's
  standing is therefore: **the correspondence is forced; the particular grain, the particular lens and
  the privilege of two are all relative to the modelling of the earlier papers.** The load-bearing
  walls are visible and they are not in this building.
- **And it is one instrument.** Every measurement was taken on a single dial browsing a synthetic
  list of 2^53 − 1 records, though §11 now runs the correspondence across a grid of grains and bases
  rather than the one pair the instrument was built with.
- **Every demonstration in this series has met only data it generated.** The terrain, the districts,
  the city built on the road — all of them take their structure from the address's own prefixes, so
  finding the hierarchy there is finding what was put there. The demonstrations show that a reader
  CAN traverse such a hierarchy without a second scale system, and show what it looks like when it
  does. They are silent on whether any body of data outside a demonstration is indexed this way, and
  that question is the one an outside reader should press hardest.
- **The correspondence is representation-relative, and that is not a small caveat.** A reader that
  matches an address's base aligns with the hierarchy *of the address representation it was handed*.
  It does not thereby discover a privileged hierarchy in the data: the same bits can be grouped into
  bytes, words, pages or records, and each grouping is a different ladder. Nothing here says an
  address hierarchy and a data hierarchy are the same thing, and usually they are not. The claim is
  about navigating the former without a second scale system, not about discovering the latter.
- **And the radix is made relational by this, deliberately.** If any base can be made native by
  building a reader to match it, then a base is not a property of an address alone. What stops being
  arbitrary is the *matching*: which is the paper's subject, and why it sits in this series.

## 9. The lens, attacked — three failures and a price

> **(1 Oct)** "The tangent is not a function the reader evaluates; it is the name of what the reader receives" agrees with R148: arctan and tan are indices a description uses, and the reader's own act is to sweep and form the ratio.

The obvious next theorem is that a bounded reader has only one admissible lens, which would make the
whole chain forced: reader → lens → ladder → radix. It was attempted here and it did not come out.
The attempts are kept because each one narrowed the question, and because the failures say something
the successes would not.

**The five conditions do not separate anything.** The list a browse map might be expected to satisfy
— bounded, focused, scale-free, composable, invertible — is satisfied by the tangent, by tanh, by p/√(1+p²) and by the
logistic map alike. The reason is structural: a reader's ordinary changes are the *affine* group of
the line, since moving the focus is a translation and changing the radius is a scale, and every
candidate family is that same group conjugated onto its own rim. Closure under composition is
therefore automatic and tests nothing. Measured: all four compose to themselves to 10⁻¹⁶.

**Two further conditions were tried and killed.** *Can a change of reader move the horizon* — no, and
not for any family, because an affine change fixes the ends of the line and hence the ends of every
rim. *Do the ends of the list meet* — every bounded monotone map sends ±∞ to its two ends, so gluing
them is a decision about the rim rather than a property of the map, and once glued the inversion
p ↦ 1/p is continuous for all of them.

**What is genuinely different about the tangent, and why it is not yet a condition.** Under that
inversion the tangent's rim map is a rigid reflection: the rim coordinate's derivative departs from
one by 0.000, against 1.183 for tanh and 1.892 for the algebraic lens. So the tangent alone carries
the inversion without distortion — but *rigid* is measured in the rim's own metric, and the rim's
metric is the tangent's. The test assumes what it is meant to show.

**The attempt that did come out, and what it is worth.** The remaining candidate was not a further
property of the map but a coherence condition between the two tangents the corpus carries: the
reading is V/H, a ratio of one arrangement's two extents, a tangent by definition; the browse's map
is separate; and the query must run back through the reader's own arithmetic rather than a second one
kept for the purpose.

Written out, each candidate's query asks the reader for something. The tangent's row is written with
a π in it, which invites exactly the misreading this section is about, so it is worth saying first:
the equation is how a conventional coordinate system *labels* the relation, not an operation the
reader performs. The layout produces a pair of extents; the reader's primitive reads their ratio.

| the lens | its query | what the reader must be able to do |
|---|---|---|
| tangent | p = h · tan(u·π/2) | form a ratio |
| tanh | p = h · atanh(u) | take a logarithm — a *difference* of logs |
| algebraic | p = h · u/√(1 − u²) | take a square root of a second-order quantity |
| logistic | p = h · ln(u/(1−u)) | a logarithm again |

And what the reader has, from The Unit Rule and settled long before this question: it holds one
value, receives one magnitude at a time, and can form their ratio and round it to a rung. It has no
second register, no square root, no logarithm and no table. **So the separation is in the reader's
register rather than in the rim's metric, which is what §9's attempts could not manage.**

The turning point is worth stating carefully, because it is easy to misread as smuggling the tangent
back in. The reader does not *apply* a tangent. The layout hands it a direction, a direction is the
pair (V, H), and the reading of that direction is V/H. **The tangent is not a function the reader
evaluates; it is the name of what the reader receives.** Every other candidate would require the
arrival itself to be something else — a log-ratio, a length, a log-odds — and none of those is what
one extent read against another hands over.

**The result, at the strength it has earned, and it is worth stating in the form a referee would
accept.** NOT: the tangent is the only mathematically possible lens. That was attempted three times
and failed, and nothing here establishes it. RATHER: **of the candidates tested, the tangent is the
only one whose query is already represented by the observer's primitive, so any other costs a
primitive the reader was not given.** The difference matters because the primitive set is itself part
of the model — a sceptic can say the arrival was *defined* to be a ratio, and this paper alone cannot
answer that; what answers it, if anything does, is that the ratio was fixed by the Unit Rule long
before this question was asked, and for other reasons. That is a price rather than a prohibition, which is how this corpus settles such things
— a unit costs a journey, a frame costs three bodies, a mixed address costs a table, and a different
lens costs an operation. The chain therefore reads:

**given nothing → the tangent → a ratio ladder → the grain → the radix**

with *given nothing* carrying the weight the geometric attempts could not. Give the reader a
primitive and the first link goes, the lens becomes a choice, and the radix follows whatever grain
the new reader runs on.

It is worth naming what kind of result that is, because the corpus has a category for it. This is not
a theorem that a base is forced; it is a **bill**. Every link in the chain is something a reader
either has for nothing or must be handed: the tangent, or a primitive; a ratio ladder, or nothing at
all; a grain, which it has by being a reader; and then the matched radix, free. An address in another
base is still readable, and an address in a mixed one still browsable — each simply costs a line. The
question this paper set out to answer was what a base is. The answer it ends with is that **a base is
where a reader's own step and a writer's own levels either agree or are reconciled, and reconciling
them is always paid for by somebody.**
- **It says nothing about arithmetic.** No claim is made here about computation, representation
  error, or the merits of binary in machines. The subject is the correspondence between a browsing
  ladder and an address ladder, and nothing else.
- **The alignment is of levels, not of content.** A matched radix makes the hand's rung and the
  address's digit the same step. It does not make the contents of a level any more legible, and a
  uniform address space still cannot report position however neatly it is written (Browsing §12).
- **One dial, one list, one measurement.** Everything above was measured on a single instrument
  browsing a synthetic list. Nothing here has met a real corpus of data, and the claim is a
  structural one about two ladders rather than an empirical one about any body of records.

## 10. What it was used for

A paper in this series earns its place partly by what it lets the next build do, and this one was
put to work immediately. The road demonstration — an endless list browsed by the same dial, with
country generated either side of it — had been seeding its land from arithmetic shifts of the
record, a multiscale texture whose scales corresponded to nothing. §3's result says what the scales
should be: **the levels of the address**. Re-seeded from the prefixes — the record with its low bits
dropped, which is precisely what the tower draws as a row — the land's features acquire addresses. A
feature of level b spans exactly 2^b records and begins where that level's digit changes.

At a grain of two the two seedings are the same arithmetic, and the statistics duly do not move:
correlation 0.999 at one record, 0.834 at 256, 0.244 at 4,096. That is the point rather than a
disappointment. It means **the road's octaves and the tower's levels were always one structure**, and
what the paper supplied was the reason to write them as one.

**And the levels do make places, which is the claim's one visible test.** The city built on this road
gives a building's DETAIL from its own record and its CHARACTER from a PREFIX: a district is the
record with its low six bits dropped, a quarter with nine. Nothing is added to the generator — the
prefixes were always there, being what the tower draws as rows — and the result is that a stretch of
street shares a manner while its buildings still differ inside it. Measured over forty districts: the
spread of building heights *within* a district is 0.41 standoffs, the spread *between* districts
1.18, a ratio of 2.9. Walking far enough now puts you somewhere that looks different rather than
merely elsewhere.

**And the honest limit of that demonstration.** The terrain was generated FROM the address's
prefixes, so of course it has the address's structure — the experiment cannot show that data found in
the world is built this way, and does not try to. What it shows is narrower and still worth having:
**where content is indexed on the same ladder the reader browses, the hierarchy needs no separate
scale system at all** — no zoom levels, no tile pyramid, no level-of-detail table. The address is the
pyramid. Whether anything outside a demonstration is indexed that way is a different question, and
this paper does not touch it.

**And the ladder has a floor as well as a ceiling, which building the thing made plain.** §7's floor
is arithmetic — below the grain of the number an address is kept in, a step moves nothing. The
demonstration found a second one, in the drawing rather than the number: a construction that places
everything against a single ground line cannot draw what is beside the eye, because a wall you are
standing in fills the view. For a long time the cure was to remove near things a standoff early, so
the world evaporated at arm's length. Clipping the geometry against a near plane instead — one
interpolation, since every coordinate is linear in the reciprocal of depth — put the near field back.

The point is not the fix. It is that **the reader's ladder runs out at both ends against something
that is not the address.** At the top it meets the list's own length; at the bottom, the grain of the
number; and in a drawing, the near plane of whatever construction is doing the drawing. None of the
three is a property of the levels being browsed, and all three are mistaken for one while they are
being hit.

Which level reads is decided by the walk, not by preference. At half a standoff a second, a district
of 2^6 records takes about two minutes to cross and a quarter of 2^9 about eight; 2^14 would take
eight hours and 2^20 three weeks. **The levels a reader can cross on foot are the ones near its own
pace, and the rest are what the radius is for** — which is the same division the paper began with,
arriving from the far end.

What follows from the joining is §3's control, now in a landscape — but only after a second repair,
and the first attempt is worth recording because it looked finished and was not. Re-seeding the land
from prefixes is not enough on its own: the country was still budgeted by a formula in the *depth*
alone, which had been written before any of this and takes no account of the radius. Measured, it
gave the same country at every setting, and a step at a fine level moved the far skyline more than
the near one — the opposite of what the join is for. **The levels being the address's means the
budget must be the address's too.** The coarsest level drawn is now the one the radius can address,
since country wider than a corner is country the reader has no step for, and the finest is set by the
depth, a level being invisible once it is narrower than the eye can separate there.

With that, the claim is visible rather than merely arithmetic. A step of one corner turns over the
country at that radius's own scale and leaves the others: at full radius the corner is 3.5 trillion
records and the whole skyline changes; at a radius of 0.35 it is 5,997 records and the country of
level 13 changes; at 0.2 it is 24 records and a twenty-four-record walk moves the land underfoot. The
same movement of the hand crosses one feature whatever the radius, and what changes is which country
it is. A fixed-radix address, indexed this way, is not a filing scheme with terrain laid over it:
**it already IS the pyramid, and the radius decides which of its levels is in reach.** The clause
that does the work there is *indexed this way*, and it was true here by construction.

## 11. Commensurability, measured across a grid

The demonstrations above have an obvious weakness and it was put plainly by an outside reader: a
reader built with a grain of ln 2 was tested against a base-two address, so of course one rung was
one level. That shows consistency, not discovery. The answer is to stop demonstrating and measure a
grid.

**An audit first, because it is owed.** In the tower's control the code reads
`bit = round(log2(unit)); step = 2^bit`. The 2 in both places is the grain written in by hand, so
that implementation assumes what it tests. Written for a reader of any grain, with r = e^σ its own
ratio, the same two lines are `level = round(log_r(unit)); step = r^level` — and **no base of any
address appears in them**. The reader counts in its own ratio; whether that lands on an address's
levels is then a question with an answer.

**The grid.** Four readers with grains ln 2, ln 3, ln 5 and ln 10, and four addresses of base 2, 3, 5
and 10, chosen independently. Each reader is walked rung by rung — its unit multiplied by its own
ratio, nothing computed from the base — and the *address* is asked how many of its own levels have
gone past. Rungs per level, measured against predicted q = ln b / σ:

| | base 2 | base 3 | base 5 | base 10 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **grain ln 2** | 1.0000 / 1.0000 | 1.5869 / 1.5850 | 2.3248 / 2.3219 | 3.3278 / 3.3219 |
| **grain ln 3** | 0.6312 / 0.6309 | 1.0000 / 1.0000 | 1.4673 / 1.4650 | 2.1003 / 2.0959 |
| **grain ln 5** | 0.4310 / 0.4307 | 0.6826 / 0.6826 | 1.0000 / 1.0000 | 1.4314 / 1.4307 |
| **grain ln 10** | 0.3011 / 0.3010 | 0.4776 / 0.4771 | 0.7002 / 0.6990 | 1.0000 / 1.0000 |

Worst departure anywhere in the grid: **0.0058 of a rung**, and nothing was tuned. The diagonal is
exactly one; the off-diagonal cells are the interesting ones, and they are where the law says.

**And commensurability is a measurable condition, not a coincidence of drawing.** Detuning the grain
against a fixed binary address, and asking at which rung the reader's own step stops landing on a
level:

| the grain | the first rung that selects the wrong level |
|---|---|
| 0.90 × ln 2 | 5 |
| 0.98 × ln 2 | 25 |
| **ln 2** | **never: exact at every rung** |
| 1.02 × ln 2 | 25 |
| 1.10 × ln 2 | 5 |

A two-per-cent error in the grain survives twenty-five rungs and then fails; a ten-per-cent error
fails at five. Matching is not a matter of degree that happens to look tidy at one value.

**Which turns §5's result inside out, and improves it.** A uniform grain cannot match a mixed
address — but a reader whose grain *varies*, σᵢ = ln bᵢ, matches it exactly: measured departure from
one rung, 0.000000000, against 0.415 for the best uniform reader. So mixed radix is not unreadable
and never was. **What it requires is a variable grain, and the schedule of those grains is precisely
the table §6 said had to be supplied.** The accounting is cleaner stated this way:

> **Uniformity can be free. Variation has to be represented somewhere — in the reader's grain, or in
> information handed to it.**

That is the strongest form of the bill, and it is the form this paper ends on: not that a base is
forced, nor that one is better, but that **the agreement of two ladders is a relation between a
writer's levels and a reader's step, and every departure from it is paid for by one side or the
other.**
