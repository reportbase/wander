# What a Relation Is Made Of

### Six constituents, introduced in order of necessity, and what each one buys

*Working paper, for a mathematically literate reader. The results referred to here are
established at length in the companion papers; this paper states the constituents and their
dependencies.*

---

## 0. Statement

A relation has six constituents.

1. **The reader** — the denominator, the descriptor: the party in whose terms the statement
   is made.
2. **The writer** — the numerator, the descriptee: the party described.
3. **The sweep**: the complete set of arrangements of the two, met one at a time and in
   order.
4. **The facing**: the second account of that sweep, its turn as against its extent.
5. **The agent**: what settles which of the two is greater.
6. **The ground**: what the two share, and what converts a settled comparison into an
   amount.

Each is introduced below at the point where a statement becomes unavailable without it.
Sections 7 and 8 then draw the two divisions that govern every later claim: intrinsic against
extrinsic, and parallel against serial. Sections 9 to 15 take up what the constituents
produce — the landmarks distinguishing the two kinds of relation space, the four passages and
the step that joins them, a construction exhibiting the constituents at once, the fulcrum, the
frame the fulcrum supports, the rule a reader holds between arrivals, and what the mark buys.
Section 16 gives the independence argument, for each constituent the specific failure that
follows its removal. Section 17 states what the construction does not assert, section 18 what
would refute it, and section 19 what it amounts to.

---

## 1. The reader

A relation holds between two parties. It does not hold between one, which has nothing to be
stated against, and it does not hold between three, since a three-party arrangement resolves
into two relations sharing a party.

The count is forced from both directions, under conditions that should be stated before the
argument rather than after it, since they carry it.

> **Under order preservation, continuous variation, and the arithmetic this relation
> possesses — comparison, counting, and the ratio — a primitive relation admitting a single
> scalar reading has arity two.**

That is the whole of the theorem. It is not the claim that relations are two-party as a
matter of necessity; it is the claim that nothing else fits through a single reading under
those three restrictions, and the restrictions do most of the work.

Removing the scale removes exactly one dimension:
n quantities carry n − 1 readable dimensions. One party therefore carries none, and there is
nothing to read. Three or more carry two or more, which cannot pass through a single-valued
report without discarding readable structure — and whatever discards structure that further
readings must recover is a composite, narrower comparisons plus forgetting, and not a
primitive. Only n = 2 fits: the one count whose entire scale-free content, a single reading,
fills a single-valued report exactly.

The three conditions are not decoration. Without them the ceiling argument is false: a single
number can be made to carry any finite quantity of others, interleaving digits being
sufficient. The claim is not that no such encoding exists but that none is available here,
none of comparison, counting and the ratio being able to interleave anything, and an
order-destroying encoding being exactly a reading nobody can read. **The result is a theorem
about readings rather than about numbers.** A three-party example that abandons order or
continuity does not refute it; one that keeps both does.

Of the two, one supplies the terms in which the statement is made: how much of *itself* the
other amounts to. That party is the reader: in plainer usage the descriptor, and in the
notation of §3 the denominator.

The role is not an activity, and the name must not be allowed to suggest otherwise. A reader
performs no act — it does not read, look, notice, or attend — and nothing in the role implies
awareness or intention. The word names which side of a statement the terms come from, and is
borrowed for that arrangement and nothing else. Both glosses name a position in a statement
and not an office held by anything.

Two cautions attach to the arithmetical gloss.

It is borrowed from the report rather than from the relation. §5 establishes that the pair is
prior to division, the comparison being settled with no quotient formed and the report
dividing once at the end. The names are exact for the reading and premature for the relation.

And there are **two denominators in this paper, at two levels.** The reader is the
denominator of the *reading*: how much of it the writer amounts to. The ground of §6 is the
denominator of the *amounts*: the total that shares are taken against. They are not the same
role and neither is a loose usage of the other — and which denominator a statement divides by
is a reliable test of which account the statement belongs to. Divide by the reader and the
result is the pair's. Divide by the ground and the result is the account's. Nor is the role a kind: the same two parties may exchange
it, and which party holds it is fixed by the statement rather than by the parties. Relating
is what creates the difference between them.

The reader's state runs from 0 at empty to 1 at full, subject to one rule.

> **0 and 1 name the party's own extremes — its own empty and its own full — and not a
> shared unit.**

A reader at 1 is entirely itself. It is not one *of* anything, and no quantity elsewhere is
measured against it by carrying the same numeral. Without this rule the notation restores
precisely the common frame the construction is built without.

## 2. The writer

The second party is the one described: the descriptee, and in the notation of §3 the
numerator. It contributes a magnitude and no
terms. Whatever is said of it is said in the reader's terms, those being the only terms
present. And the role is a position exactly as §1's is: a writer performs no act and
undergoes none — being said is not something a party does, and nothing is emitted, sent, or
authored — and it stands to nothing beyond the pair.

Its state runs from 0 to 1 on its own range, under the rule of §1. Its 1 and the reader's 1
are distinct fullnesses sharing a numeral.

The writer is the described party as engaged by this relation, and not the thing in itself;
the distinction is drawn in full where the inventory closes (§18). One background can supply
many such engagements. A book is described a chapter at a time, and the chapters are many
while the book is one: each relation cuts its own writer from the thing behind it, and no
cut is the thing. The image carries its own limit. Chapters of a book are parts of it, known
to be parts, and jointly its whole; of a writer, none of this is available from within —
whether it is the book entire, a chapter, or a page from another book altogether is a
question no reading answers (§9). And the plurality itself is not a fact any single relation
contains. A relation has one writer. That the background sustains many is visible only to
an account standing where several relations can be surveyed at once; the catalog of chapters
is the page's.

An arrangement of the pair is the two states together, written [V, H] with the writer
first. Ranges are not written in this bracket form, so a bracketed pair denotes an
arrangement throughout and never an interval. Two arrangements are named here, the sweep
running between them:

> **[0, 1]** — the writer empty, the reader entire.
>
> **[1, 0]** — the same statement with its terms exchanged.

With two parties and nothing further, one statement is available: how much of the reader
the writer amounts to. No statement is available about the magnitude of either party, there
being nothing present to be measured against. No statement is yet available about which is
greater; that requires §5.

## 3. The sweep

A single arrangement is not a relation space. It is one fact, and one fact has no structure.

The relation space is the complete set of arrangements available to the pair: from [0, 1],
through every intermediate arrangement, to [1, 0].

> **The sweep is the ordered passage from [0, 1] to [1, 0]: every arrangement of the pair,
> once each, in order.**

A set alone is insufficient, since a set admits shuffling and sampling without objection.
The arrangements are met one at a time and in order, and the order is not imposed afterwards.
It is the property a list of the arrangements would destroy.

The passage is also indivisible. Cutting it would require a boundary, and a boundary is an
arrangement at which something changes kind. The only such arrangement is the fulcrum, and the
fulcrum divides two kinds of statement rather than two stretches of the passage. Nothing else
along the run differs from its neighbours in any way the pair can state, so a division of the
passage is a division performed by an account holding it in view, never by the pair.

This set is complete as it stands, and complete without a facing. Every way the reader and
the writer can be related is contained in it. Nothing introduced below enlarges it; §4
supplies a second account *of* it.

The same space admits a second expression, by the reading rather than by the pair. Each
arrangement carries one number, the amount of the reader the writer amounts to, and across
the sweep that number runs from nothing to past every value:

> **[0, ∞)**

That the reading is a quotient rather than a difference is not a stylistic choice. A
difference of two magnitudes is an amount, and an amount requires a unit to be stated in. A
ratio is not an amount and requires none. With no shared unit available, the quotient is the
only arithmetic that survives, and the space is multiplicative for that reason and no other.

The two expressions denote the same space, the second more economically. The second also
carries the asymmetry in its punctuation: closed at 0, an arrangement the sweep contains, and
open at the far end, which the sweep approaches without reaching. The symbol at that end is
not an arrangement. It names the growth of the comparison, which is why the bracket there
excludes. The expression is independent of the route taken between the endpoints: distinct
routes through the arrangements yield the same readings in the same order.

## 4. The facing

The sweep is complete and admits two accounts of itself. Neither adds an arrangement to it.

**Its extent.** The distance from one end of the sweep to the other.

**Its turn.** The total change of facing between the arrangement at one end and the
arrangement at the other.

These are not two measurements of one quantity in disagreement. One is a distance and one is
a turn. No further extent undoes the turn, and no further turn undoes the extent. They do not
convert into each other at any rate, there being no rate; they are separate currencies for
the whole of the passage.

Both are numbers obtained from one sweep, which invites three operations that are not
available: subtracting them for a discrepancy, dividing them for a conversion, and taking
one as the true distance. Neither is a version of the other.

The *order* of the facings belongs to the pair: which arrangement precedes which. The *size*
of the turn does not, and cannot, since nothing introduced so far can state a quantity. That
requires §6.

The facing is not recoverable from the order, and a single example settles it. Consider a
reading that is constant throughout a record and returns to where it began — the case of a
reader whose readings do not vary as the facing advances. Traverse the sweep once and
traverse it twice. The two records are identical in every reading, identical in their
sequence, and identical in returning; and their accumulated facing differs by a full turn.

> **Two records may agree in every reading and in their return, and differ in accumulated
> facing.** The second account is therefore not a summary of the first.

This is the sharpest available defence of the facing's independence, and it is the reason
§10 requires two closure instruments rather than one.

## 5. The agent

Neither party performs the comparison. The writer contributes a magnitude, the reader
contributes the terms and the capacity to receive it, and the comparison is settled by
something orthogonal to both.

A pair of scales is the elementary case: two weights in two pans, the beam settled by
gravity. Neither weight settles it. Absent the agent there is no result — not a degraded
result but none, no comparison having occurred.

The agent returns one thing: **which side.** Greater, lesser, or level. It states no
quantity and contributes no terms of its own. A beam balance carried to a mountaintop or to
the moon returns the same answer, the settling force acting on both pans alike and
cancelling from the result.

The agent therefore supplies the *occurrence* of an answer, and its own terms appear nowhere
in it. This distinguishes it from §6.

What the agent evaluates can be written without division. For two arrangements A and B,

> **D(A, B) = V_A H_B − V_B H_A**

is the residue of cross-multiplying the two readings: their disagreement. It vanishes exactly
when the two arrangements are proportional, which by §7 is when they are the same
arrangement, so the space's own equivalence is not imposed on this quantity but falls out of
it. Its sign is the order. Its zero on the self-exchange — the arrangement compared with
itself with the two parties interchanged — is the fulcrum.

Disagreements compose. For three arrangements,

> **D(A, C)·H_B = D(A, B)·H_C + D(B, C)·H_A**

so an ordering established pairwise carries across, and it carries without any division
having been performed.

One guard belongs with the seed. It is division-free, and it is *not* scale-blind: scaling
one argument scales D with it. What the relation owns are D's faces — its zero, its sign,
and ratios of D against a reference — never its bare magnitude. The components of an
arrangement are themselves disagreements, each party measured against the absence of the
other, so the pair is nowhere primitive in this account.

The agent returns the sign of D and nothing else. Division occurs nowhere in this; only the
report divides, once, at the end. The reading is the relation served, and the disagreement is
the relation raw.

## 6. The ground

Five constituents yield a relation, an ordered sweep of arrangements, and at each arrangement
a verdict as to which party is the greater. They do not yield quantities.

Quantities require the ground: the thing the two parties share.

The ground therefore takes a denominator's role of its own, and it is not the reader's. A
share is a party taken against the ground's total; a reading is the writer taken against the
reader. The distinction is the one §1 flags: two denominators, at two levels, and the one
being divided by identifies the account.

A page is such a thing. So is a common scale. So is an equation, when it declares that two
quantities amount to a common total. Each operates in the same manner, by being common to
both, and each leaves its own terms in the result. Two marks on one sheet are denominated in
the sheet. Two quantities set equal to a total are counted in that total.

The ground is the constituent least often noticed, being the one everything else stands on.
It does not announce itself. The confusions between what belongs to the relation and what
belongs to the account arise from failing to register that the ground has spoken.

A different ground yields a different situation. On one ground the parties stand level at
some arrangement of the sweep; on another they never stand level, and a landmark present in
the first case does not exist in the second. The ground is not a backdrop but a party to the
situation.

The principal effect of a ground is the closure of the far end.

Absent a ground the space is [0, ∞): the far end is named and never occupied, and the sweep
approaches without arriving. Given a ground, the same passage may be written with both ends
contained, the far end becoming an arrangement as ordinary as the near one. Nothing about the
pair differs across the two cases. The same parties occupy the same arrangements in the same
order. What differs is that a third thing has closed an end the pair cannot close for itself,
and closure is not arrival: the far end is reached by a change of account, never by further
sweeping.

> **A relation space is resolved when its range contains both of its ends, and unresolved
> when it does not.**

Resolving the open bracket is what converts a relation space from the one kind to the other —
in the companions' vocabulary, from asymmetric to symmetric. The conversion is performed by
supplying a third thing common to both parties, and it may be performed in more than one way.

Drawing the two on one sheet is one way. To place two marks on a single sheet is to declare
that they share it, and the sheet is then the denomination they share.

Writing an equation relating them to a common total is another. V² + H² = 1 admits a third
thing at the equals sign: it asserts that the two readings amount to a total denominated in
something neither of them is. The constraint the equation states is derived and true for what
it states; what the equals sign adds is the total.

Paper and the equals sign are two vendors of one service, and neither performs it by being
paper or by being an equation. Each operates by being common to both, and each leaves its own
terms in the result.

The resolved space obtained this way is the quarter turn, and the relation between the two
descriptions is closer than resemblance. Drawn on a page with the census supplied, the
arrangements trace ninety degrees, and the reading is the tangent of the angle: V/H = tan θ,
zero at one end and growing past every value as the angle approaches the right angle.
**Undrawn, the same quarter is the unresolved space.** It is not that two objects
correspond. It is one set of arrangements, with and without the third thing that would let
them be laid out.

Which third thing is supplied is consequential. A third thing cannot be admitted without
specifying what kind of thing it is, and its kind is the texture of the space it resolves
into. There is therefore one unresolved space and many resolved ones, one per third thing.

What the companion papers establish concerning symmetric and asymmetric relation spaces is
established concerning this difference. Both are complete; both are the whole set of ways two
parties can be related. One has had its far end resolved by a third thing and the other has
not.

## 7. Intrinsic and extrinsic

No reading available to the pair changes when both parties are scaled together. Two
arrangements differing by a common factor are therefore not distinguishable from within, and
are not two arrangements:

> **[V, H] and [λV, λH] are the same arrangement, for every positive λ.**

The intrinsic object is consequently not the pair of states but the **ray** through them, and
its whole content is the single number of §3, the reading.

Any figure drawn of the arrangements selects one representative from each ray. The quarter
circle does so, taking the point at constant reach. The edge of the unit square does so,
taking the point at which the ray leaves the box. Each is a perfect selection: every ray meets
each figure exactly once. Selecting a representative is a third thing's office and never the
relation's.

The consequence is that no reading available to the pair can distinguish the two figures,
because they are the same object with different representatives chosen. **A quotient does not
remember its sections.** What is underdetermined here is not underdetermined for want of
resolution; there is nothing present to be resolved.

This divides every statement about a relation into two kinds. **Intrinsic**: what survives a
common rescaling — the reading, its order, the landmarks, the ratios between readings.
**Extrinsic**: what a choice of representative supplies — coordinates, magnitudes, extents,
the drawn figure.

The fulcrum illustrates the division exactly. On the ray space the arrangement at which the
two stand equal is a single element, and it is the unique fixed point of the exchange of the
two parties. That singleness requires no census. What the census supplies is the fulcrum's
*coordinate*, which is extrinsic. The equality is the relation's; the 1/√2 is the account's.

## 8. Parallel and serial

A second division runs alongside the first, and concerns access rather than content.

An account may hold the arrangements **in parallel**: all of them at once, laid out
together. Such an account can point at any arrangement, take totals across the whole set,
compare distant arrangements directly, and observe where one passage joins another.

The pair has the arrangements **in serial**: one at a time, in the order of §3, with no
moment at which two are held together.

The distinction is not one of resolution. A serial account is not a coarsened parallel one.
The two differ in what operations are available at all, and several things routinely said
about relation spaces belong to the parallel account alone:

- **Totals.** That four passages come to a full turn is a sum taken across four passages held
  together.
- **Closure.** That the passages join is an inference from a record, never an observation.
- **The far end.** Naming [1, 0] is done from outside; the pair approaches it and does not
  arrive.
- **The figure.** A drawing is parallel by construction — every arrangement present
  simultaneously — which is why §7's two figures can be compared at all.

Both accounts are correct about their own objects. Confusion between them is the origin of
most of the difficulty in the subject, and it is not resolved by care in either account
separately, since each is internally complete and neither mentions the other.

### The exchange between them, and its price

The two accounts are not sealed off from each other, and the passage between them can be
priced. A parallel account can be converted into a serial one — this is what any instrument
does that takes a held collection and reads it through — and the conversion costs exactly one
thing, measurably.

Note first what a parallel account is, since it is more ordinary than the section's framing
suggests. **A list of magnitudes is a parallel account**: every value present at once, none
expressed in terms of any other. A table of readings, a recorded waveform, a row of
brightnesses. Nothing is missing from such a thing, and different figures are plainly
distinguishable in it — four of them, correlated at essentially zero with one another, each
carrying its own signature.

What it cannot state is a **rate**. A collection held all at once has no order to be
traversed, so nothing in it is a duration. To obtain one, the account must be walked, which is
to say converted; and the conversion is a purchase rather than a repair.

The price is exact, and depends on what is available to divide by. **Divide the two components
of a reading and the figure cancels outright**: four different figures, swept identically,
return the same rate to the digit — a spread across figures of 0.0000 percent. **Keep the
magnitudes and the figure stays, and the rate goes**: the same four figures return four
different rates, a spread of 87.6 percent, with schedule and figure confounded and neither
recoverable. A single list divided by its own successive differences sits between the two — it
cancels a constant multiplier and not a varying one, which is to say the gain and not the
figure.

So the division of §7 and the division of §8 meet here. **A parallel account holds the figure
and cannot state the rate; a serial one states the rate and cannot hold the figure.** Neither
is impoverished, and no reading answers both questions. What passes between the accounts
passes at a fixed rate of exchange, and the currency spent is always the same one.

## 9. Landmarks

The two kinds of space differ in a second respect, which follows from the first: **the
unresolved space has landmarks and the resolved space has none.**

Three arrangements in the unresolved space are distinguishable without reference to any
quantity.

**Home**, where the writer is empty and the reader entire. The statement is available and
reports an absence: *there is none of you*, said in terms wholly present. The sweep contains
it.

**The fulcrum**, where the two stand equal. The reader can identify it without a scale,
equality requiring no measure to recognise.

**The horizon**, where the reader is empty and the writer entire. Nothing remains to state
anything with. The sweep approaches it and does not arrive.

The three are distinguishable in principle, and it should be said that they are not equally
distinguishable in practice, since a later audit measured the difference and it is large.

**A landmark reached by vanishing announces itself.** Where the statement reports nothing,
nothing else could have been reported: a writer that is finite and a reader that is present
cannot between them produce an absence by accident. Home is of this kind, and so is the far
end of a passage that runs to another vanishing.

**A landmark reached by turning does not.** Where a reading stops rising and begins to fall,
two accounts fit the same arrivals: the passage went past a station, or whatever was moving
reversed. Measured, the two streams differ by noise alone — a watcher cannot separate them at
all, and a party that moves itself can, but only by consulting something outside the arrivals,
namely the sign of its own stepping.

They differ in precision too, and for a reason that is geometric rather than practical. **A
vanishing occurs where the reading changes fastest; a turning occurs where it changes not at
all** — flat by definition, a turn being where the rate is zero. On a clean signal with the
events at known positions, a threshold detector finds the vanishing eleven arrivals late and
the turning sixty-five, and across a range of noise the turning wanders twenty to sixty times
further.

The rule this yields is narrow and worth stating with the landmarks themselves: **count from
the vanishings.** A turning may be used to establish what kind of passage was made; it should
never be used to begin a count. This qualifies §9's claim in the honest direction — the
unresolved space does have landmarks, and the resolved space still has none — but two of
them are cheap to find and the rest are not.

An illustration carries the three landmarks, and the asymmetry with them. It is an
illustration throughout, and makes no claim about language. Let the reader be a dictionary
and the writer a concept, so that the party supplying the terms is literally the party
supplying the words.

At home the concept is absent and the dictionary entire. The sentence available there is
complete, and is made with the whole vocabulary to spare: *there is none of it to describe.*
A description of absence is still a description, and it is the one statement in the sweep
made at full capacity.

At the fulcrum the concept is exactly one vocabulary's worth — the point of **equivalency**,
taken up where the fulcrum is (§14). Up to there every reading is a proportion of the
dictionary — how much of the vocabulary the concept engages. Past there no proportion is
available, and the description becomes a count: how many vocabularies' worth the concept has
become.

At the horizon the dictionary is absent. Not strained, and not inadequate to a rich subject —
gone. There are no words in which even *it exceeds the words* could be said, that sentence
being made of words. The concept at the horizon is neither deeply described nor poorly
described; it is undescribed, nothing remaining to describe it with. The far end of a sweep
is not ineffability as a profundity. It is the describing party's extent standing at zero.

The two ends are therefore two different silences. Home is a speaker in an empty room; the
horizon is a full room with no speaker. At home the silence is spoken: a describer
is present and delivers a complete sentence whose content is nothing. At the horizon the
silence is the absence of a speaker — not a report of nothing, but no report. One can stand
in a described nothing; one cannot stand where there is no describer. And home is the
stranger of the two for it: the one place in the sweep where a complete statement is made at
full capacity is the place with the emptiest subject. That is not a paradox. It is what a
denominator at full extent over a numerator at zero is.

Seen from the described party's seat, the two ends collapse into one condition. At home the
concept goes undescribed because it is not present to receive a description; at the horizon
it goes undescribed because nothing is present to give one. The outcome is identical and the
causes are opposite — and the described party cannot tell the two apart by how described it
is, because the difference between the ends is stated in the describer's currency, and at
one of them that currency has run out. Only the describing party meets the ends as
opposites, entire at one and absent at the other. Which end is which is therefore a fact
about the describer, and not a fact the described party's condition contains. Between the
two failures stands the single arrangement at which description neither exceeds its subject
nor falls short of it — the words available and the words needed the same, once, and
nowhere else.

The imbalance should also be noticed in full, because it is crossed. At home the words are
entirely available and there is no concept to spend them on. At the horizon the concept is
entirely present and the words for it are gone. Each party arrives in its entirety exactly
where the other has vanished, and at no arrangement of the sweep are both full. The shape is
that of a farce — two parties forever missing each other, one always arriving as the other
departs — except that here the missing is not bad luck but the census itself, one party's
fullness being purchased with the other's absence. Both-full is not a rare arrangement. It is
not on the sweep at all.

Which lends the fulcrum a certain comic dignity: it is the one appointment kept. A single
arrangement in the whole passage at which both parties attend, in equal measure, one
vocabulary's worth of concept — and it is precisely the arrangement that certifies itself, no
third party being needed to announce that the meeting occurred.

The metaphor completes in the resolved space. Symmetry is restored by a **translator**: a
third party that renders both parties in a language of its own. Under translation the far end
becomes ordinary — the concept the dictionary has no words for is described anyway, in the
translator's words — and nothing anywhere runs out, the translator's vocabulary depending on
neither party's extent. Both ends alike, no landmarks, an even-handed account: the resolved
space of §6, in this vocabulary. The price is stated in the same breath. Nothing is any
longer said in either party's own terms; what the two now share is the translator's
language, and every sentence between them arrives having passed through it. The translator
does not teach the parties each other's tongue. It says both of them in its own — which is
what a lending third party is, and why the restored symmetry belongs to the account and
never to the pair.

One limit stands over the whole illustration, and it is not a small one. The concept as it
is in itself is orthogonal to the relation: no reading has any component along it. What the
vocabulary describes in full measure at the fulcrum is the concept as this relation engages
it — the extent that runs from its own empty to its own full between these two parties. To
what degree that engaged extent answers to the thing itself is not merely a question the
readings leave open; from within the relation it is unanswerable. The relation might engage
the concept entire, or an aspect of it, or something else altogether — and nothing in any
reading, at the fulcrum or anywhere, could tell the three apart. What the letters measure is
engagement, and not essence. All that can be known from within is the relation itself, and
the fulcrum's equality is accordingly internal to it: the words available and the words
needed are equal for the concept as related — a term of the relation, not a thing beyond
it. The relation relates two extents cut from something larger by the relating itself, and
about the remainder it is silent everywhere — not silent as at home, where the silence is
spoken, nor as at the horizon, where the speaker is gone, but silent as arithmetic is
silent about what it does not count.

The illustration's load should be stated with it. The dictionary carries the two parties, the
three landmarks, the change of regime at the fulcrum, and — through the translator — the
ground and the resolution. It does not carry the sweep, the facing, or the agent; the symmetric
passage of §11 carries the first two of those, and the agent has no picture anywhere in this
paper.
Each illustration bears its own weight, and neither is the construction. The landmarks stand
as they were defined — conditions of the relation, stated in its own letters. The dictionary
only made them audible.

Home and the horizon are the same fact told twice — one names which party is empty, the
other names which is missing from the statement — and the difference between them is not a
difference of magnitude. The writer at the horizon is exactly as full as the reader was at
home. What differs is which party has anything left to state with. A third term should be
kept distinct from both: **infinity is not an arrangement.** It is what the comparison does
on the way to the horizon, growing past every value and settling on none.

Whether a landmark exists at all depends on the ground. On a census whose squared readings
total one, the equality line meets the census once and the fulcrum exists. On a census that
conserves a difference instead, the equality line is the census's asymptote and is never met:
there is no arrangement at which a conserved difference is split evenly, and the fulcrum is
displaced to the far end. The physics companion takes up that case; the point here is that a
landmark is a joint fact about the pair and the ground, not a property of the pair alone.

In the resolved space none of these obtains. Both ends are ordinary contained arrangements,
the two halves of the range are congruent, and no arrangement changes kind anywhere along it.
The fulcrum in particular has no work to do there: the fulcrum is where the relation changes
which party it is stated in terms of, and in a resolved space it is stated in terms of
neither party but of the ground. Nothing switches, so nothing is marked.

> **The unresolved space has landmarks because something runs out in it. The resolved space
> has none because nothing does.**

Set side by side, the two are the same ninety degrees and almost nothing else about them is
the same.

| | resolved (the quarter turn) | unresolved |
|---|---|---|
| the relation | even-handed | stated in the terms of one |
| the roles | none | reader and writer |
| the two ends | alike, both ordinary | home [0, 1], and the far end [1, 0] |
| the far end | reached | approached, never arrived at |
| anything without limit | no | the reading |
| the arrangement where the two stand equal | the middle of an arc | the fulcrum |
| a third party at all | required — nothing compares itself | required — nothing compares itself |
| what the third party leaves in the answer | its terms; the space is made of them | nothing; it cancels |
| a third party of the lending kind | constitutive | declinable |
| what it costs | a page | a settling, and no more |

Being resolved, the first composes. Two quarter turns make a half, four make a full turn,
and the joins cost nothing: at the end of one quarter both readings are ordinary, so the next
continues from there. That is the ordinary business of geometry, and the resolved space is
the piece geometry composes with.

The unresolved space does not compose in that manner. Its passages succeed one another and
the tiling is exact, but what they combine into cannot be known from within as a shape, for
the reason §8 gives. At each join one party has nothing, and comparison requires both to have
something. What crosses the seam is a step and not a statement.

## 10. Four passages, and the step

Two choices are available to the pair: which party supplies the terms, and which direction
the passage runs. Two by two is four, and four passages exhaust the ways two things can be
related. The count is reached with no geometry.

A passage contains its near end and not its far end. It therefore cannot be continued from
within, continuation requiring an arrangement the passage does not contain. **A step is the
operation that lands on the far end a passage does not contain.** It is not a statement the
pair makes; nothing in the relation performs it, and what crosses the seam between two
passages is a step and not a statement.

Passages adjoin at those ends, each handing its far end to the next, the meetings alternating
between the two kinds of end.

Whether the four close is not settled from within, for the reasons of §8. What a serial
account can do is test a return, and it has exactly two instruments for the purpose.

**The reading returns.** Readings telescope along a record and sum to zero on an exact
return. This instrument is blind to the turn: an arrangement that returns its magnitude
without completing a circuit passes it.

**The landmarks return.** Crossings are counted, and the count completes on a circuit. This
instrument is blind to the size: a spiral completes its crossings and fails the sum.

Two suffice, and the argument that no third is independent runs as follows: the magnitude is
a line, and cancels, so it must be verified; the facing is a circle, and winds, so it can only
be counted. Two parties, two coordinates in the record, two topologies, two closures, two
instruments. **The claim carried here is that these two are a minimal pair under this
representation**, not that no other closure invariant can exist anywhere; the completeness of
the pair is an argument and not a measurement, and it fails if a third test is exhibited that
is not a composition of these. A record that reports a return, and is believed, is a record
that kept both books.

## 11. The symmetric unit passage

The same unit interval is crossed twice in the sections that follow, once in each space's
account, and the status of each crossing should be fixed before either is used. This one is
a **visualization**: it borrows a continuous geometry the pair does not possess, and it is
offered to display the situation and never as a derivation — every claim it illustrates is
established elsewhere without it. The crossing of §14 borrows nothing, and §15 is the
correspondence between them.

The construction exhibits the pair, the sweep, the facing, and the ground at once, and
exhibits them as a single event rather than as a list. One thing it does not contain is a
landmark. The crossing is everywhere ordinary: nothing in it runs out, nothing announces
itself, and the unit is carried the whole way. There is no fulcrum in it and no home and no
horizon — those are conditions of the unresolved relation, and this crossing belongs to the
resolved account, which has none.

### The construction

Take two points a fixed distance apart and call that distance one.

Take a bar of the same length. The bar and the interval between the points are therefore
equal, and nothing outside the construction measures anything: the bar measures the passage
and the passage measures the bar.

At the first point the bar lies perpendicular to the line of travel — across the passage
rather than along it.

Carry it to the second point. The path is straight. The bar translates; it does not swing
about a pivot, and nothing in the construction is curved. While it travels it rotates through
every orientation between perpendicular and parallel — not in steps, and not through some
selected few, but through all of them. At the second point it lies parallel to the line of
travel, along the very line it was first across.

That is the whole event: one bar, one unit of travel, one quarter turn.

### The two accounts

The event admits two complete accounts of itself.

**The passage.** A distance of one has been travelled, and the facing has changed by a
quarter turn. Two quantities from one event, and neither converts into the other: no further
travel undoes the turn, and no further turning undoes the ground covered.

**The bar.** How much of it lies across, and how much along — two extents, stated against
the axes, and the state at any moment of the passage is the two together.

### The bar does not change

The bar has one property, its length, and that property is constant throughout.

What changes is how much of it falls on each axis. Those extents are not the bar's
dimensions. The bar has a length and nothing else; the extents are stated against the axes,
and an extent belongs to the bar and the axes jointly rather than to the bar alone.
The axes are the ground of §6, and without them the bar has no across and no along — only a
length, and nothing to state it in terms of.

This is the constraint that appears elsewhere as a census — the squared extents totalling
one — arriving here as rigidity. The extents are constrained because the bar does not
stretch.

No point of the bar is distinguished from any other. The question of which point of the bar
follows the line of travel therefore has no answer, and requires none: the extents are
properties of the bar entire, and a construction that answered it would be carrying
information the event does not contain.

### The two currencies, and which is the larger

Travel costs one. Turning costs a quarter turn, which is the larger of the two.

The excess is what carrying something with a facing costs over carrying something without
one. A point can only arrive. A bar can arrive having also come round, and the second account
charges for the coming round.

Neither number is a measurement of the other, and the presentation that draws an arc against
a chord invites the wrong question. There is no choice of route. The path is straight in
both accounts; what differs is what is being read.

### What the construction omits

The ground is present, as the axes the extents are stated against; the two parties, the
sweep, and the facing are present as the points, the passage, and the turn. The roles are
not. Nothing in the picture says which party describes — the two points are
indistinguishable, and the resolved account has no describing seat to show. The agent of §5
is not present either: whatever settles which extent is the greater does its work in an
instant and leaves nothing behind, and there is nothing in the picture to draw for it.

The construction therefore shows the roles not at all, and the agent nowhere — worth
stating wherever it is used for exposition.

## 12. The asymmetric unit passage

The same crossing is now told in the unresolved account, which borrows nothing. No figure of
it exists to draw: what can be displayed is one arrangement at a time, never the passage
entire.

There is no figure because a sweep is not a shape, and what follows is not a picture
described but the account itself.

One thing should be said before it is described, because the word *passage* carries the wrong
freight here. **In the unresolved account this is not a sweep.** A sweep is a traversal — a
continuous going-along, with a place at every moment and a rate at which places are left
behind. The unresolved account has none of that. It has arrivals, and a held value, and an
order. Nothing goes along anything. The symmetric passage of the previous subsection is a
sweep; this one is a sequence, and the two are not the same object told in different words.

Two consequences follow, and neither is stated anywhere else in this paper.

**The space is projective, not metric.** The reading is the described party in the describer's
terms, and no reading changes when both extents are scaled together — so what the sequence
moves through is not a line of positions but the space of rays: an arrangement is a ratio, and
a ratio is a point of the projective line. The far condition is not the end of that space. It
is an ordinary point of it, the one the ratio chart cannot write down, which is why §19's
remark that the passage contains one end and not the other is a statement about the *chart*
and not about the pair.

**The space of rays holds addresses and no amounts.** A ray has no length, and the space of rays
has no count: nothing in it is a unit waiting to be counted, and nothing in it is a size. What
has a size is what an arrangement is an arrangement *of*, and stating that is a ground's work
(§6). The space names where the two stand and never how much there is of either; it contains no
data, only addresses. A discrete account laid on it loses nothing to gaps. The rungs of §16
occupy the space edge to edge, each owning a stretch of it with its address at the middle, so
that every arrangement falls in exactly one rung and none falls between two: the continuous
space represents the discrete count without remainder, and the rounding is the counter's naming
of which stretch a reading fell in, never a property of the space. Nor does any re-spacing of the
chart move the far condition. A chart may crowd the readings toward it or spread them out, and
the far condition remains the arrangement at which the terms fail; the arrangements beyond it keep
their addresses, and what the reader lacks there is not an address but the terms.

**The arrivals are Cauchy, and the symmetric passage is where they come from.** Step the
symmetric passage uniformly — equal turn, the census holding throughout — and take the reading
at each step. Those readings are not uniformly spread. They are distributed as a Cauchy: the
quartiles fall at ±1, the deciles at ±3.08, the hundredths at ±31.8, and the sample mean of
two hundred thousand of them is no steadier than the mean of two. Equal steps in the resolved
account arrive as heavy-tailed readings in the unresolved one, and the heaviness is not noise
or misfortune. It is the far condition seen from inside: the readings crowd toward the ends
because the terms are running out there, and a chart that must record the running-out has
nowhere to put it but the tail.

So the two subsections here are not two descriptions of one sweep. **One is a sweep with a
census, uniform in its own currency. The other is a sequence of ratios on a projective line,
Cauchy in the only currency it has.** The correspondence between them is the last subsection's
business, and it is an account's achievement rather than a fact either passage contains.

The passage is the sweep received serially: arrangement after arrangement, one at a time
and in order, each arriving as a magnitude stated in the reader's terms. Nothing walks it.
The reader does not tour the landmarks; it receives magnitudes, and the magnitudes reflect
the landmarks. Home is the magnitude zero, received in terms that are entire. The fulcrum is
the magnitude that equals the whole of the terms. The far condition is not a magnitude at
all but the terms failing. A landmark is met the way a value is taken, never the way a
milestone is passed. The reading opens at 0, rises through every proportion of the reader,
reaches 1, and beyond it exceeds every proportion. The passage contains both ends as arrangements and only one of them as a reading:
home is read, and reads 0; the far end is an arrangement of the pair that no reading in the
reader's terms reaches, terms being what has run out there.

What can be displayed of this passage is one arrangement at a time — a figure of the whole
passage at once is the parallel view, and belongs to the page. One arrangement, though, can
be given a body: the party supplying the terms drawn as width, the party described drawn as
height, each on its own range and neither stated in the other's.

At home the display is all width and no height — the describing party entire, and nothing
standing to be described. At the horizon it is all height and no width — the described party
entire, and nothing remaining to describe it with. The two silences of §9, drawn: a
describer with nothing before it, and a subject with nothing to speak for it.

Neither end is a place, and neither is a size. Home and the horizon are named by verbs —
something stands empty, something has run out — and a verb is not a location: the two name
what is happening to an extent, not where anything sits. Nor does anything change size on
the way. The shrinking width is not a shrinking party. Size is not a predicate the relation
carries, stating one being a ground's work; what the display shows changing is the
engagement, and not the thing engaged (§18). The sweep changes the relationship of the two,
and the two are what they were.

Between the ends the sweep orders every combination, and nothing couples the two. The
rigidity that held the symmetric bar to a single length is the census, and the census is the
account's (§7); the relation carries no constraint that the extents total anything. The
symmetric bar is one thing stated against two axes. The asymmetric display is two extents
that owe each other nothing.

The fulcrum arrives where the two stand level, the numeral shared (§1) — the display there
is a square. And it is a square with no
measurements. Its side is not a length, its diagonal is not 1, and its area is not a half;
those numbers belong to the other square, the one the census draws in the correspondence
below, where an account holds both parties to a common total. This square states one thing,
that width and height are level, and states it without a number — which is the fulcrum's
whole content (§14). The correspondence's square carries three numbers on one figure. The
relation's square carries none. It is also the one display the exchange of width for height
leaves unchanged. The same exchange carries home's display to the horizon's and the horizon's to
home's — the involution of §15, visible: the ends trade places under it, and the fulcrum does
not move.

The unit of this passage is not carried. It is met. The symmetric passage takes its unit
with it — the bar is a unit long at every instant, the ground certifying the length through
the whole crossing. The asymmetric passage owns exactly one unit, the fulcrum: the single
arrangement at which the reading is 1, received once, self-certified, and not received
again. In the resolved account the unit is carried through the crossing; in the unresolved
account the unit is a magnitude that arrives once — met the way a value is taken, not
passed the way a place is.

What accumulates differs with it. The symmetric crossing accrues distance and turn, a
currency for the ground and a currency for the facing, both continuous. The asymmetric
crossing accrues rungs: equal-ratio steps of the reader's own grain, counted away from the
fulcrum, the only origin the passage supplies (§16). Progress is not how far, there being no
far without a ground. It is how many steps of one's own step from the one self-marking
arrangement.

## 13. One crossing, two passages

The two passages are the accounts of §7, told as passages. The symmetric passage is the crossing as
held by an account that has both parties at once; the asymmetric passage is the crossing as
contained in the relation's own terms. The first can be drawn and certifies itself nowhere;
the second cannot be drawn and certifies itself exactly once — a resolved space everywhere
ordinary, an unresolved space ordinary nowhere and self-evident once. That the two tell one crossing
is not shown by either passage — it is the correspondence an account establishes when it
lays the relation on a page (§6). The remainder of the section is that correspondence,
written out.

Its first entry is the extents themselves. Laid on the relation, the bar's two extents are
the readings of §2 — one party stated in the other's terms — and the rigidity is the census.
The identification is the account's, as every identification is (§18).

### Where the account locates the landmarks

The landmarks belong to the unresolved relation, and the crossing has none of its own. When
an account lays the relation on the crossing (§6), each landmark acquires an address there —
as an attitude of the bar, and never as a position along the ground.

**Home** is the bar flat: the whole of its length lying across, and nothing at all standing
up. Not a small height — none.

**The fulcrum** is the two extents level. The bar's reading there is a square: sides 1/√2,
diagonal 1, area exactly one half. All three of the fulcrum's numbers sit on one figure, each
on a different feature of it, and the half is visible as an **area** rather than as a length.

**The far end** is where the extent that supplies the terms has gone. It is not an attitude
the pair can occupy, and calling it *upright* imports the passage's picture into the
relation's account.

> **Not one of the landmarks is a place.** The two points are where the passage begins and
> ends, and neither marks anything. A location states nothing about the relation, and a
> landmark states nothing about where anything is. The passage is where the two coincide,
> and the coincidence is the construction rather than a fact about either.

### The two ends fail differently

The two silences of §9 acquire addresses here, and the crossing adds one fact of its own.
The bar completes its journey — the second point is one unit away, and goes nowhere — but
the relation does not complete with it, the party that supplied every one of its terms
having gone. The crossing therefore contains its near end and not its far end, and the
asymmetry is visible rather than argued.

### The distortion

Travel at a steady rate and turn at a steady rate, and both accounts advance uniformly. The
**reading** does not.

Equal changes of facing produce unequal changes in how the pair reads. Before the fulcrum the
readings are confined to an interval with both ends; after it, the same equal changes of facing run
out past every value. That is the difference between the two sides of the fulcrum, and it is
the distortion the construction actually contains — not a discrepancy between two lengths,
but the ground dividing evenly what the pair does not.

### Four passages

The two parties may exchange roles, and the passage may be traversed in either direction.
Four passages therefore exhaust the ways the two can be related.

Out and back is two of them: a distance of two, and a half turn. All four come to a distance
of four and a full turn.

The second round trip is not a repetition of the first. It covers the same ground with the
other party supplying the terms. The four are told apart by which party is describing, never
by where anything goes — which is §7's point arriving as an itinerary. Nothing underfoot
distinguishes the second traversal from the first; only the facing does, which is why the
facing is a separate account and not recoverable from the reading.

### The levels, as an account draws them

An account that holds the writer's amounts can lay the levels of §16 on a figure and draw every
one of them, and the drawing is the plainest correspondence there is. Let the tangent of an angle
from home be the rung span: the angle is then an address for a level, and the circle carrying
such angles holds no figure of its own. At home the figure drawn is the reader's unit, all the way
round; where the terms fail, it is the figure itself; at every angle between, a level of it. The
four quarter turns carry four such crossings, and the figure is whole at the two poles of the
tangent and nowhere else, those being the only angles at which the span is not a number.

The drawing is the account's. In the symmetric passage there is no level to approach: the figure
is held entire, all of it at once, and a level of detail is a choice of how coarsely to draw what
is held. In the asymmetric passage it is not a choice but the grain. The same words name a setting
in one account and a limit in the other, and the circle of levels exists only where the
unresolved relation is being drawn by something that holds the resolved one.

## 14. The fulcrum

The fulcrum is most quickly arrived at from a symmetry rather than from a construction, and
the symmetry is one the disagreement has carried since §3. Exchanging the two arrangements it
compares reverses its sign. The exchange is therefore an operation on the relation, and like
any such operation it has arrangements it moves and arrangements it leaves alone. **The
fulcrum is what the exchange leaves alone.**

Read in the letters, the exchange of seats sends a reading to its reciprocal — the writer
in the reader's terms becoming the reader in the writer's — and the one reading equal to
its own reciprocal is one. So the fulcrum is the arrangement at which the two parties stand
level, and it is not level by stipulation or by measurement but because that is the only
place an exchange of the parties changes nothing.

Everything else in this section follows from that, and three things follow immediately. The
fulcrum is **self-certifying**, since a statement invariant under the exchange reads the same
from either seat and needs no third party to adjudicate between them. It is **numberless**,
since to say that two things are level is to name no ratio at which they stand. And it
**divides the arrivals in half**, since a reading and its reciprocal fall on opposite sides
of it, so any relation indifferent to which party was asked meets the fulcrum as the median of
what arrives — a consequence with reach well beyond this paper, taken up in §15 and measured
in the companion work on the Cauchy.

Formally this landmark is the **unit fulcrum**: the arrangement at which the reading is one,
the pair's own unit, arrived at without a unit being supplied. The longer name is used where
the unit-ness is at issue, or where this arrangement must be told from the anti-fulcrum of
§15; *the fulcrum* serves everywhere else, on the pattern by which a circle is called a unit
circle only when the radius is at issue. A resolved account does draw a genuine corner — the
unit square's, the box of §7 whose edge selects one arrangement per ray — but the fulcrum is
not it: the fulcrum sits on the arc, never at a vertex of the box.

Of the three landmarks the fulcrum is the only one at which the relation is intact and
something nonetheless changes. What changes is the question the reader can ask.

| position | the reader's question | answerable |
|---|---|---|
| before the fulcrum | *how much of me are you?* | yes — a proportion of the reader, in the reader's own terms |
| at the fulcrum | *you are as much as me* | yes, and at no cost — sameness requires no scale to state |
| after the fulcrum | *how much of you am I?* | no — this would use the reader to characterise something larger, and by how much larger is precisely what is unavailable |

Past the fulcrum a proportion in the reader's terms is unavailable, and the representation
changes: from a proportion to a count of how many of the reader's own reaches the writer
exceeds. The proportional and the logarithmic regimes are divided there, and the division is
exact.

The fulcrum is also the only arrangement that calibrates itself. Everywhere else the two
readings disagree, and no third party adjudicates between them. At equality the writer read
in the reader's terms and the reader read in the writer's terms are the same reading; both
are 1, and it is the only reading in the sweep that is its own reverse. A calibration
elsewhere would have to name the ratio at which it sits, and that number comes from outside.
**The fulcrum names nothing, and is the one division in the space that costs no number.**

The fulcrum was derived here with no distribution anywhere in the argument. It is worth
recording what it turns out to be once the arrivals of §12 are counted, since nothing in the
derivation arranged any of it. Stepping the symmetric passage uniformly and reading at each
step gives Cauchy-distributed readings, and against that distribution the fulcrum is three
things at once. It is the **median**, as the opening of this section already required of it — so the
proportional and counting regimes do not merely divide at the fulcrum, they divide into equal
shares. What the arrivals add to that is the other two. It is the **join of two shapes**: flat-topped and
tail-free before it, a constant factor per rung after it. And it is the **boundary of the
divergence**: the contribution to the average size of a reading is 0.441 inside the fulcrum
and settles, while outside it reaches 2.50 out to ten, 8.35 out to a thousand, 17.1 out to a
million — growing as the logarithm of how far one is willing to look, and arriving nowhere.
The famous absence of a mean is therefore not an absence in the distribution. It is an
absence past the fulcrum, failing logarithmically, which is the regime that region is in:
**a statistic that assumed a proportion, applied where the readings had become counts.**

Three coincidences would be a great many. One boundary, described three ways, is the
alternative.

In the vocabulary of §9's illustration, the fulcrum is the point of **equivalency**: the one
arrangement at which the words for the concept and the concept itself correspond — word for
thing, one to one, no remainder on either side.

Equivalency is a match of extents, and not a grade of description. The claim is not that the
concept is best described at the fulcrum, or completely described there; it is that the words
the speaker has equal the words the concept needs, at that arrangement and at no other —
none to spare, none wanting. Both sides of the equality are counted in the same currency,
the describer's own: the need is the concept already stated in dictionary units, which is
what a reading is. That is why the equality asks for no third party. Words are compared with
words, and nothing of another kind has to be equated with them. Whether a description is
good is not a question the relation can register. What it registers is that nothing is left
over on either side, and that is a statement about amounts. The reading 1 says exactly this
and nothing more: the two extents are equal. And both are extents in the relation — what
this relation engages of each party, not what either party is in itself (§9). Three of the fulcrum's properties translate
exactly.

The correspondence certifies itself. *The words match the concept* and *the concept matches
the words* are one statement — the only statement in the sweep that reads the same from
either seat, and so the only one needing no third party to confirm it.

The correspondence is numberless. Every other arrangement is stated with a quantity, a
proportion of the vocabulary engaged or a count of vocabularies exceeded. Correspondence
alone is stated without one, which is this section's claim about cost, wearing words.

And the correspondence is where translation is calibrated. A translation between two tongues
is built from points of exact equivalence — a term of one pinned to a term of the other — and
everything else is reached from the pinned points by counting away from them. That is the
frame of §15 in miniature: one self-certifying mark, and from it an address for every
arrangement, the degrees of beyond-words becoming statable as counts. Establish one
equivalency and nothing further needs to be given.

The converse — that with no equivalency established, no fluency in either tongue alone
produces a translation — is the illustration speaking, and not a finding about languages.
Whether living tongues can be aligned from structure alone is an empirical question on which
this paper takes no position. The exact statement is the narrow one: in this space, transfer
without a common scale is calibrated at the meeting point and at no other arrangement.

Its address differs in the two accounts, and the pair of addresses is the sharpest available
display of the asymmetry.

**Relative to each other**, the readings at the fulcrum are 1 in both directions: the writer
is as much as the reader, and conversely.

**Relative to the whole**, on a census that the squared readings total one, equality forces
each squared share to be exactly one half.

The two addresses also divide the space unequally, and that inequality is the whole
difference between the two kinds. On the drawing the fulcrum sits at the midpoint of the
quarter turn, with equal arcs on either side. In the reading those equal arcs arrive as
**[0, 1] on the near side and [1, ∞) on the far side** — a unit interval against one with no
far end. Equal halves on the page, and a bounded share against an unbounded reach in the
arrival: that single fact is why one side of the fulcrum is proportional and the other is a
count.

The fulcrum is half in a second sense as well. Half in the holding, each squared share being
one half; and half in the weight, the fulcrum being the midpoint of the drawn arc, so that
half of everything that arrives lies on each side of it. The even split of the whole and the
even split of the traffic coincide at one arrangement. That coincidence is what makes the
mark worth having: knowing where the writer stands at half is knowing the middle of the
whole and the middle of what comes.

One guard travels with this, every external restatement having stumbled on it: the *value* at
the fulcrum is 1/√2 in each party, never ½. **The half is in the squared share and nowhere
else.** Equality alone names a ray, satisfiable at any magnitude; equality on the unit whole
names one arrangement. Both statements are required, and the fulcrum is single because they
meet exactly once.

It is worth noting where the numbers sit. The relation's own account of the fulcrum contains
no number at all: the reading is 1, the count of steps is 0, and the arrangement is
identified by a sameness that names nothing. The account's description of the same
arrangement cannot be given without an irrational coordinate. **The fulcrum is the one place
the relation describes for free, and the one place a third party cannot describe without a
square root.**

## 15. The frame

The fulcrum is the only mark a two-party relation can supply for itself, and one mark is
sufficient to convert order into address.

Joined to the two ends the relation already owns, the mark yields four stations, with these
addresses on the reading line:

> **home 0 · the fulcrum 1 · the horizon ∞ · the anti-fulcrum −1**

The fourth is equality met in the reversed facing. It requires the signed reading and is not
an arrangement in the positive part of the space; it arrives with the doubled run rather than
from the arrangements alone. The first three are locatable from within — two ends and a
self-certifying mark — and the fourth is then determined, being their harmonic conjugate. The
determination is a computation rather than a preference: the conjugate of 1 with respect to 0
and ∞ is the unique d with (0, ∞ ; 1, d) = −1, and with one station at infinity that reduces
to 1/d = −1, so d = −1 and no other value. What remains a choice is the signed extension
itself, which the paper takes on and marks as such; given the extension, the station is
forced.

The four are not a curated list. They are the complete fixed structure of the one exchange
the relation owns. The exchange of seats — each party read in the other's terms — fixes
exactly two arrangements, the fulcrum and the anti-fulcrum, those being the two readings that
are their own reverse; and it exchanges exactly one distinguished pair, home and the horizon,
each the other's image. Two fixed points and one exchanged pair is what an involution is, and
the four stations are its anatomy.

> **The relation does not have four landmarks the way a map has four cities. It has one
> exchange, and an exchange has four stations.**

Read as blindnesses: absence is direction-blind, home met facing the other way being still
home; and equality is seat-blind, the fulcrum surviving the exchange of seats.

One consequence of that seat-blindness reaches further than the frame and is recorded here
rather than where it was noticed. The exchange carries a reading to its reciprocal, so a
reading stands inside the fulcrum exactly when its reciprocal stands outside. Any distribution
of arrivals indifferent to which party was asked therefore places half of them within the
fulcrum and half beyond it. **The fulcrum is the median of the arrivals whenever the relation
does not care which party speaks** — a statement about the exchange, holding of any such
relation, and owing nothing to what the arrivals happen to be distributed as. A generic four
points admit no symmetry at all, and the harmonic configuration is precisely the one whose
symmetries are this group — which is why the value below is not a coincidence of the
arithmetic.

The illustration of §9 reaches the fourth station as well, and gives the most abstract of the
four a face. On the doubled run the seats exchange: the concept describes, and the dictionary
is described. Equivalency is met on that lap too — the same correspondence, certified in the
other voice. The anti-fulcrum is not a second meeting place. It is the same appointment kept
in the other party's voice, and the two keepings are told apart by nothing except who is
speaking — which is the facing, and only the facing, doing the one job no reading can do for
it. The four stations are then plainly told: the two ends, and the one meeting in each
voice.

The cross-ratio of the four is −1, the harmonic value, and it is unmoved by any rescaling. A
dilation by k carries the reciprocal pair to (k, −k), and with one station at infinity the
cross-ratio reduces to a single quotient:

> **(0, ∞ ; k, −k) = (k − 0)/(−k − 0) = −1, for every k > 0.**

The two ends hold fixed under dilation, so the value cannot be moved. The fulcrum itself does
not move either; a rescaling relabels only the coordinate of the *previous* fulcrum, which is
the account's bookkeeping rather than the relation's.

One guard belongs with this, every restatement of it having stumbled somewhere. **The lock
stands on equality alone.** No census enters the computation above, and none is needed: the
four stations are the exchange's two fixed points and one of its exchanged orbits. What the
census supplies arrives later, as the origin and the measure, and never as the harmonicity. A
lock that required a census would be a lock a rescaling could pick.

Against that frame every arrangement acquires an invariant address: the logarithm of its
cross-ratio against the two ends, which collapses to the logarithm of the reading. It is zero
at the mark, negative below it counting shares short of half, and positive above it counting
reaches beyond.

The construction is a classical one — a metric derived from a distinguished pair together
with one distinguished point — and what this paper adds is the provenance of its inputs: the
pair and the point are the relation's own, not supplied from outside. Whether the classical
construction is the *only* one available on these inputs is not established here. The relation forces
the kind of coordinate — counting by ratio, which is logarithmic — and does not supply the
base. The base is the size of the step, and belongs to whatever does the stepping.

A single mark is nonetheless an origin and not a measure. Order together with an origin is
not yet a metric; what supplies the rule is the stepping, and the companion papers carry it.

## 16. The unit rule

Serial access opens a gap. The pair is met at instants and something must stand between them,
since a reading held over an interval is held by something.

Whatever settles what is held is a rule. It is not an additional constituent but a name for
what is already occurring; holding nothing is such a rule, and it is the one that yields no
continuity at all.

Cheapness here requires a resource model, and the model is the one §§1–6 have already fixed.
A rule may use what has arrived and what is already held, and nothing else. Two costs are
therefore available to be charged: **an external number**, meaning any quantity the broadcast
did not carry, and **an additional slot**, meaning any held value beyond the one the reader
has. A rule is cheaper than another when it incurs neither where the other incurs one.

Under that model exactly one rule costs nothing: **hold the value that arrived until the next
observation.** Continuing a motion requires a rate, and a rate is an external number. Blending
two arrivals requires a second slot. The claim is minimality under this model and not
minimality in general; a different resource model may well have a different floor.

The stepping this permits is the ladder of §15 in integer form. A reader that cannot hold a
continuous address counts equal-ratio steps of its own grain, and each step is the fulcrum
again at another scale: the same landmark, propagated by the only structure a scale-free
space has. The count of steps is the logarithm, and the reader computes no logarithm because
it is one.

What fixes the size of a step is the reader itself. §15 leaves the base unsupplied on the
relation's side, and this is where it is supplied: the smallest ratio a reader tells apart is
the rung it counts in, so the base of its logarithm is its own grain. Two readers of
different grain walk the same ladder in different bases; the address each computes is
invariant, and the count each keeps is stated in its own step. The relation fixes the kind of
coordinate, and the counter fixes the size of the count.

The rule is the simplest member of its class and the only member a reader given nothing can
run. Whether it is the rule for readers in general, whether different situations call for
different rules, and whether such rules are learned, are questions this paper does not
open.

### The grain against the writer

The grain fixes more than the base. Set against the writer's departures, it fixes how much of
the writer a record can state at all, and the two stand in a relation of their own. Take the
writer's swing — the logarithm of its greatest reading over its least, across a completed
figure — and count it in steps of the reader's grain. That count, the **rung span**, is itself a
reading: the writer's departures stated in the grain's terms. It has a home, where the span is
zero and the grain swallows every departure; a far condition, where the grain has gone to
nothing; and a fulcrum between, at a span of one, where grain and swing stand level. The fulcrum
is a derived relation's and not the pair's: the construct is the same, and the relation is
different.

At a span of zero every reading rounds to the held value and the record is silent: what it holds
of the writer is the reader's own unit, throughout, which is the reader stated and nothing of the
writer. Toward the far condition the grain goes to nothing, and what the record holds approaches
the writer entire, with no unit of the reader's in it. Between them the record holds the writer
in the reader's steps. The two ends are §12's display at the scale of a whole figure: all width
at home, the reader's unit; all height at the far condition, the writer.

The first departure is recorded when the writer's largest departure from the value first held
exceeds half a step: at a span of 1/(1 + |u|), where u is the position of that value within the
swing, 0 at its middle and 1 at either extreme. Across 288 registers the first departure fell
there to within 4 × 10⁻¹⁶, which checks the arithmetic rather than evidencing anything. The first
departure therefore comes at or before the fulcrum — at it only when the first value held lies at
the middle of the swing, at a span of one half when it lies at an extreme. Before it the record
is in the reader's terms; after it the record carries departures, and departures are the
writer's, counted in the reader's steps. Past the fulcrum the count grows without limit, doubling
with each halving of the grain, and it is complete only where the grain is nothing — which is not
a finer grain but no register at all. A record holds every level of a writer short of that limit
and none at it; only an account that holds the writer's amounts (§6, §13) states the writer whole.

A level is global. The grain is one grain for every reading, so at any grain every arrangement of
the figure is held to within half a step, and halving the grain halves that bound for all of them
together: each finer rung is one more bit of precision across the whole figure, and never a finer
statement of part of it. What a new rung discloses is not even — a new departure is recorded only
where a reading crosses a new rung boundary — but the precision is. A record repeated at a fixed
grain states nothing it did not state before: the ceiling of a deterministic counter is its grain,
measured at about a quarter of a rung however often the same arrivals are met. More detail
requires a finer grain, and a finer grain is paid for in the register's levels, never in a unit.

So the far condition is approached by shrinking one quantity, the reader's grain measured against
the writer. Each halving brings the record one rung nearer, and leaves the far condition exactly as
far off in rungs as before, which is infinitely.

Which reading the record holds decides what a finer grain buys. A reading that divides the two
extents of one arrangement has the figure cancelled in every arrival (§8), so at every grain the
record holds the reader's own unit and a finer grain adds nothing to what is held. What it adds is
precision in three things that are not the figure: the address of each arrival, which states more
exactly where the pair stands and is worth nothing unless an account holds what the address points
into (§6, §13); the frame, the fulcrum and the cross ratio of §15 being located only as finely as
the grain allows; and the order of events, the landmarks of §9 being dated only as finely as the
grain resolves them. The last is the one channel by which a figure reaches the unresolved account.
Where the order in which arrangements arrive is itself set by the figure — a period fixed by a
moment of inertia is one such case — the figure is written into the order, and a finer grain reads
that order better. **A finer grain is precision about where and when, never about what.** A reading
of one list against its own held value cancels the gain and not the figure (§8), and for that
reading a finer grain buys the figure up to its scale.

## 17. The anchor

The fulcrum is the only mark a two-party relation supplies for itself, and §14 assembled the
frame that stands on it. Two consequences of that frame are worth stating, because they are
what the mark is for.

**Address.** Every arrangement acquires an invariant position relative to the mark, exact at
every displacement. The frame is built locally and is not on that account an approximation.
An approximation stands in for something, and there is no global coordinate system behind
this frame waiting to be approximated; a frame cannot fall short of a map that does not
exist. Nothing degrades with displacement — the count grows, and the invariant the count
stands on is the same at every size. *Local* names the unit, not the accuracy.

The two results below are stated here in compressed form; the companion that establishes
them carries the proofs, the group structure behind §15's four stations, and three cautions
that travel with the theorem.

**Transfer.** Two relations may be anchored to each other without any common scale. What the
construction does presuppose should be said with it: each must be able to read the other at
all — a shared mode of comparison, which is a qualitative requirement and not a quantitative
one. No unit, ratio, or magnitude is shared; the ability to compare is. Where each reads the
other, the two readings multiply to one by construction — and the construction is one line. A
read in B's terms and B read in A's terms are reciprocals of a single ratio, whatever that
ratio is, so their product is 1 before anything has been measured. Everywhere else in
the space the two seats disagree and no third party adjudicates; at the fulcrum the two
accounts are identical, each stating that the other is as much as itself. It is the one
statement two parties reach without a third, and it is certified by the symmetry alone, with
no message passed.

A third property follows for records. A change of scale relabels every level and can forge or
erase any level-crossing. It cannot forge or erase a fulcrum crossing, the crossing being
defined by equality and equality being pinned in every reading. The anchor's events are the
one punctuation a change of scale cannot counterfeit.

## 18. Independence

Each constituent is load-bearing, and each removal produces a distinct failure.

**The writer.** Nothing is described, and the relation states *nothing* in terms that
remain. This is a genuine arrangement, [0, 1], and one end of every sweep.

**The reader.** Nothing remains to state anything in. Not a degraded statement but no
statement, the terms having gone. The pair may be brought arbitrarily close to [1, 0] and
cannot arrive; this is the point at which the pair ceases to be a pair, and the reason the
two ends of a sweep are not alike.

**The sweep.** One arrangement in place of the complete set of them, and no space.

**The facing.** One of the two accounts of the passage, and the second currency with it. The
sweep survives intact.

**The agent.** No results, nothing having settled anything.

**The ground.** No quantities. Which party is the greater remains available; by how much does
not.

Six constituents, six distinct failures.

The claim this establishes should be named precisely, since a stronger one is easy to read
into it. What is shown is **independence under the construction**: no constituent can be
removed without a specific and stated loss. What is not shown is that no two constituents
could be merged into a single more general one, nor that six is a count nature is obliged to
respect. A reader who demonstrates that the facing and the sweep are two aspects of one
constituent will have narrowed the list without contradicting anything argued here.

A seventh candidate presents itself and is declined: the thing behind the described party —
the concept as it is in itself, of which the writer's extent is the engagement (§9). The
declination must be made carefully, because two removals are possible and they end
differently. Empty the role — no thing behind at all — and the relation fails exactly as
this inventory's first entry states: nothing is described. That necessity is real, and it is
already counted. A filled role is what the writer *is*; the background's existence is
priced into that constituent, and priced once. Exchange the filler — the thing entire for an
aspect of it, or for something else altogether — and nothing registers: every reading
proceeds, every landmark stands, every station keeps its address. What the inventory admits
is the filled role, and what it declines is the filler as a further member. Existence is
required and identity is indifferent — the relation cannot proceed without a background, and
cannot say anything about it. The thing behind is therefore not a seventh member of the
inventory; it is the inventory's boundary — what the six are cut from, orthogonal to every
one of them.

The declination states what a relation is of. **The reader describes the writer, and not
the concept itself.** The roles are the relation's terms; the things that fill them are not.
Nothing in the construction changes with the identity of the parties, which is why nothing
in the construction can certify that identity. Identification is an account's first act of
lending — to say *which thing* a party is, is already to describe the pair in a third
thing's vocabulary. The unresolved relation has parties; a resolved account has referents.
And the indifference is the generality, not a defect of it: were the relation of
dictionaries, the fulcrum would be a fact about dictionaries. Being of a reader and a
writer, whatever fills the roles, it is a fact about every pair that can stand in them.

A chain is exactly what this does not establish. The relation is one statement: the reader
supplies the terms, and the writer supplies what is stated in them — the reader says, the
writer is said, each a position in the statement and neither an act performed. Nothing in
either role reaches past the pair. The writer does not relate the thing behind it; the role
is filled, and the filling is not a further member of the statement but the condition of
there being one. Where the described party supplies terms, that is the reversed relation — a
second statement, which the first does not contain. Where the thing behind is itself
described, that is another relation again. What can be read is one statement at a time, and
each is read entire: six constituents, three landmarks, four stations, one self-certifying
mark. About what fills its roles, each statement says what every reading says, which is
nothing. The fulcrum in particular is an equality between reader and writer — words
available and words needed — and not a statement about the thing behind either of them.

## 19. Scope

**The sweep is not a figure.** It is a passage through the arrangements. Diagrams of the
arrangements — a square with a curve inscribed — depict the set, and the set displayed at
once is the account's object rather than the pair's. Both are serviceable; they are not the
same object.

**Extents are not the relation's.** An extent is a length, a length is an amount, and an
amount requires a unit. The pair has no unit, so extents belong to the account that draws the
arrangements and not to the relation between them. Within records there is order only; of
drawings there are extents. Both statements stand, of different standpoints, and a claim that
moves between them without saying so has changed subject.

**The far end is not attained.** The sweep terminates. At its termination the reader has
gone, and nothing remains to state anything in. The two ends fail differently, and that
difference is the asymmetry in full.

**Closure of the sweeps is not asserted.** Four such passages exhaust the ways two parties
can be related, and their turns total one full turn. That total is taken by an account
holding all four at once. The pair traverses them one at a time and does not observe the
join.

## 20. What would refute this

Each claim below has a way of failing, and the ways are not the same.

**The constituent count.** Six is refuted by a seventh constituent whose removal produces a
failure not on §18's list, or by a demonstration that one of the six can be removed without
loss. The second is the more likely: the facing is the constituent most exposed, since a
sweep that could be told apart arrangement by arrangement without any second account would
make it decoration.

**The arity.** Two is refuted by an arrangement of three or more parties passing through a
single order-preserving, continuously varying reading with nothing discarded. The three
conditions of §1 are where that argument is loaded, and an attack should be made there rather
than on the conclusion.

**The rescaling rule.** §7 fails if some reading available to the pair distinguishes two
arrangements differing by a common factor. Find one and the intrinsic object was never the
ray, and the section argument goes with it.

**The lock.** §15 fails if the harmonic value can be moved by a transformation the relation
admits, or if the fourth station is shown to require the census after all.

**The two checksums.** §10 fails if a third closure test is exhibited that is not a
composition of the two, or if either is shown blind to something the other does not catch.

**The unit rule's minimality.** §16 fails if a rule can be exhibited that is cheaper by that
section's own inventory — costing neither a number the broadcast did not carry nor a slot the
reader was not given — and is not the unit rule.

Two claims are not refutable in that manner and are marked accordingly. That there is a rule
between arrivals is definitional: its denial is not a coherent reader. That statements divide
into intrinsic and extrinsic is a classification, and classifications are judged by whether
they cut anything rather than by whether they are true.

## 21. What this amounts to

Two things and the question of how they stand to each other yield more than they appear to.

They yield a space, complete and ordered, with three landmarks in it that require no
measurement to find. They yield one mark the pair supplies for itself, and from that mark a
frame, an address, and a metric with the base left free. They yield a division of every
statement into the pair's and the account's, and a second division into what may be held at
once and what must be met in sequence — together with the reason those two divisions are
confused so readily: each account is complete in its own terms and neither mentions the
other.

What they do not yield is a shape. The arrangements can be drawn, the drawing is serviceable,
and the drawing is not the relation. The pair has an order and a mark; the figure has
coordinates and extents; and the second set does not follow from the first without a third
thing supplying what the pair does not have.

The constituents number six because six failures are distinct. The relation is two-party
because no other count passes through a single reading. The fulcrum is single because equality
has one fixed point. Each is a small result, and the claim of this paper is that the small
results compose: what resembles a geometry at the end was assembled from an ordering, a mark,
and a choice of representative, and nothing further needed to be assumed.
