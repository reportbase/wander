## Abstract

**Four situations** (§2.4, R126–R143). Everything in this paper sits in one of them. What a reader knows of the breadths sets four situations: the circle (known and
the same in every direction; symmetric; no g), all other shapes (known but varying; asymmetric; g the ratio of the circle
to the shape), the fisheye (h's breadth known, v's existing but unknown; neither symmetric nor asymmetric), and the
impossible (neither known). Shapes are views from nowhere; only the fisheye is a view from somewhere, and only it has a
front and a back. The Cauchy is what situation 3 must use, and situation 3 is a shape of situation 2 read through
situation 1's assumption with situation 4's unknown on the v side. Tested on ellipses (item 328), the corner is the middle of a lay
only when the breadths are equal; laid as the circle is, the middle sits at v/h = b, the breadth ratio, which a situated
reader reads from its arrivals.

**The headline result: observation from somewhere is not dimensional** (R111, R125). The view from nowhere has x, y and
z, three orthogonal dimensions of the world, any one of which a rotation turns into another. Observation from somewhere is
not built that way, and is not a fixed number of anything; it is what it is, made of the things this paper names. Two
magnitudes v and h, divided, leave a **relation**, the turn from horizontal to vertical; laid as a turn from a home, in a
facing, the relation is an **address**; the **world** is breadth, and **g**, on [0, 1], is in situation 2 the ratio
of the circle to the shape, 0 the circle and 1 the shape (R140), and in situation 3 a count toward an end the reader cannot
see (R141); the unit circle, read uniformly with no situated observer, is
the view from nowhere, not the reader (R127); and each address points to a **magnitude**, which
is the world's (R115): not a sweep of possible values but, at each address, the one specific value the system being
observed defines (R116). The relation and g are swept; the magnitude is met. Each is reached through the one before, so
no rotation turns one into another. (Earlier drafts called these three dimensions, nested; that was fitting observation
to the box of x, y and z, and is withdrawn.) The reader **divides**, **sweeps** and **points**. It divides v by h, which removes their common scale and leaves the relation, on [0, ∞); it sweeps that
relation, laid by the Cauchy as a turn from its home one step at a time, with g the count toward an end it cannot see; and
each address it sweeps points to a magnitude, the scale the division took out. The division and the sweep are addressing
and are laid before anything is there; the magnitude is the world, a specific value the reader meets and does not
choose. Relation, g and magnitude are each unbounded in their own terms, [0, ∞), and the Cauchy's turn is how a
reader holds any of them in a finite span (R108–R110). What found them is **g's connection to the Cauchy** (R92–R94, reread under the four situations). Written with g, the
Cauchy is g · (2/π)/(1 + s²): the span stays π/2, the height at home is g · (2/π), and the total is g. In situation 3,
where the Cauchy is forced (R138), g is the count toward an end the reader cannot see, g = (2/π) · arctan(n · step) [the shortcut, not the sweep: swept from home, g is the count of steps taken (R148)],
rising toward 1 and never reaching it (R96, R141). The classical account writes only g = 1, where the factor simplifies
away and 2/π reads as a normalisation: it writes the situated reader's lay as if the count had arrived, as if v's breadth
were known. **The classical Cauchy is situation 3 with its far end assumed.** Its scale γ reads as the placeholder for v's
breadth, the standard γ = 1 setting the corner (R138; the γ reading is Claude's). The unit circle is not this lay's g = 1 rim, as earlier
drafts had it: it is situation 1, both breadths known and equal, with no g at all (R134, R140), and the disc that drew g as
a radius under it is withdrawn. **What the Cauchy sets up is the sweep** (R100–R101): laid by it, a situated reader sweeps
a system in relation (the turn) and in g (the count) at once, the Cauchy crowding the addresses along the turn, where an
index sweeps breadth, the extent, cell by cell. (The corpus called g a density; the word is dropped, R124. The reading of
the classical Cauchy as situation 3 with its far end assumed is Claude's, made on Tom's go-ahead of 1 October.)

We study geometry as the sweep of addresses that point to a magnitude, and observation as what a reader, an observer
that stands somewhere, can hold of a world through its addresses. The addressing is the reader's and says nothing about
what the magnitudes are; the magnitudes are payload, and it is they that make the first border and so everything hidden,
blocked or behind (R91). Shape is recovered from payloads, as far as a ladder of sources allows. Geometry tells a reader where and how to look; it does not
tell it what it will find. The rest of the paper is the consequences of that split. A reader has
addresses laid in advance over its front half and, along each, the first thing met and how near it is, its **nearness** on [0, 1]. Two
kinds of reading are distinguished. A **parallel** reading establishes, all at once, the range of addresses an
observation consists of, and through them points to the magnitudes, which stay in the system: it copies nothing (R113).
A **serial** reading works inside that range, one address at a time in an order of its own: it holds ratios, over time. A third view,
the **view from nowhere**, has no standpoint at all (R127): it is the circle and every other shape, read uniformly, with no
home, horizon or corner (R131) and no front or back (R139). It holds what lies behind a situated reader and what its
blocking withholds: the hidden and the blocked.

The results fall into five parts. (i) **How addresses must be laid.** The even lay of the front half is the Cauchy
family, one reading per dimension: in one, two and three readings the share of addresses within the corner (v = h) is
1/2, 1 − 1/√2 and 1/2 − 1/π, with medians 1, √3 and ≈ 2.264. Laying the readings crossed instead puts poles in the lay:
up and down for the two across readings, and at the reader's own point once depth is included. Laid in turn, the
forward reading has its corner at the reader's own unit D1, and nearness is exactly that reading taken from its far end and
clipped at the corner. (ii) **What passes between readers.** Relations among readings of shared things cross between
standpoints (one number on a row, two on a plane, a line per reader in space), exactly for what holds still and to
within how far things move between readings for what moves; raw addresses and nearness do not.
(iii) **A ladder of what fixes what.** The world up to a projective map comes from relations between two readers; the
shape up to a scale from readers' own lays (two readers passing turns) or one reader's own nearness; the rotation from a reading of something far; the count of
turns from a carry; the place from closing a loop; and **the unit from agreement only, which is not geometric**. (iv)
**Time and records.** A serial reader receives a still world exactly in any order; its clock carries straight motion to
the present; hiding takes a thing's middle but not its place, which is in its outline. A stored record passes only what
it stored, but a record of many standpoints gives back depth through relations between them. (v) **The balance.** Every
measured weakness of one view is covered by a strength of another: the parallel reader has magnitude without time, the
serial reader time without magnitude, and the view from nowhere holds what lies behind both readers. We state a conjecture: serial
and parallel readers, in company, recover everything of the world's geometry that some standpoint reads at some moment,
up to a scale and to the grain. Two probing tests (a reader that is both; things that deform, evenly and lopsidedly)
found no residual the argument did not predict. The lopsided case locates one limit exactly: a growing thing's two ends
carry correctly, but which point is its place is not in the outline and comes only with identity.

**A working reader that only receives** (§8.5). The simulations above ran on a game loop: one shared now, a snapshot
every frame, the engine holding everything. One demo, *Wander*, was rebuilt without it. Every system keeps its own time
and sends signals; signals travel; the reader holds only what has reached it. Each point arrives as two unit pairs
(h, v) on h² + v² = 1, each the representative of a direction, not a normalization of breadths (which the fisheye cannot
have, R135), so no distance comes with it; v over h is the address and the pair is then discarded; the reader's
range keeps at that address only an address back to the system, and the system is read only by following it. g is
measured from the arrivals themselves. A full three-dimensional world of moving, orbiting solids is seen this way.
Which of two arrivals on one address is blocked is now decided by the reader's own distance to each (§8.5). And the reader's own travel gives an address back to the magnitude: from two addresses of a still point, its
distance over the reader's own step (Prop. 8.6). **3d is the reader's addresses of direction plus that one relation** (R119, said as "the sphere of addresses"; the reader's addresses are its fisheye, R129).
With it the reader goes on, in its own lengths and seconds, to a body's size, its shape in the round (§8.8), a star's weight from what goes round it,
where a body will be, which bodies belong together, and the speed of the signals themselves, with no shared clock
(§8.7). The shape comes two ways, side by side: whole, by following one address back to the system, and up to a
scale, rebuilt from arrivals whose surface points the range already names; the reader's own place and the body's
travel drop out (Prop. 8.8). The weight, orbit and signal-speed results are Kepler's, Newton's and Rømer's, reached by the route they took; that shows the framework's
assumptions suffice for them, not that the assumptions are true (§11).

## 1. [0, 1]

Everything below is built from one object: **a share of all of something, none of it, or anything in between**. Two such
shares, each of its own all, are unitless, and neither knows the other's all. The fraction of a tank filled and the
fraction of a day gone; the share of a push that is vertical and the share that is horizontal; the part of a road
behind and the part ahead. Drawn geometrically the two shares are **h** (horizontal) and **v** (vertical), magnitudes on
[0, 1] (R37: magnitudes, never signed; direction comes from how the pair is read, not from a negative v or h).

Read as a pair, h and v give the **reading** s = v/h ∈ [0, ∞]. It has three marks:

- **home**, s = 0;
- **the corner**, s = 1, where v = h;
- **the horizon**, s = ∞.

Reading the same pair the other way, h/v (a change of **facing**), sends s to 1/s.

The reading and its marks are the situated reader's, situation 3 of §2.4: h's breadth known and v's not, so v is had only
in terms of h (R132). The view from nowhere does not divide: h and v are each read relative to the other, and there is no
home, corner or horizon (R131).

**Proposition 1.1 (three landmarks, of two kinds; R88).** The facing fixes the corner and exchanges home with the horizon.
*Proof.* s = 1/s with s ≥ 0 has the single solution s = 1; the map s ↦ 1/s sends 0 to ∞ and ∞ to 0. ∎

So a reading has three marks, of two kinds: one the facing leaves where it is, and a pair it exchanges.

Two infinities are kept apart (R77). The **horizon** is the reading's infinity, which a forward-facing reader finds at its
sides. The **vanishing point** is where distance collapses, straight ahead; it sits at home.

**The even lay of one reading.** Lay addresses evenly in turn, ψ ∈ [0, π/2), and read each as s = tan ψ. The addresses
then fall on the reading with probability density

  f₁(s) = 2 / (π(1 + s²)),  s ≥ 0,

the (half-)Cauchy. Half the addresses lie within the corner, and the median reading is 1, under the standard placeholder
γ = 1; arrivals from a shape whose v breadth is b times h's have their middle at v/h = b, not at the corner (R142). We call this the fisheye in
ordinary prose: fine near home, spreading without bound toward the horizon, yet reaching it within a finite turn.

## 2. g in the Cauchy, and the sweep of g

*The discovery (Tom, 14:07): "discovering its connection cauchy is the discovery here." And (13:51): "the way cauchy hides this fact by simplying g away at the limit, but g is very
much present as the sweep over a system, but not the system but the address to the system." And what it is for (14:44):
"the cauchy sets up the ability to sweep the systems breadth and density."*

### 2.1 The Cauchy hides g

The relations between h and v, counted evenly in turn, span

  ∫₀^∞ ds / (1 + s²) = π/2,

π/4 on each side of the corner (§1). Now let **g** be the share of N addresses in use (R93). The count of addresses
across the relations is

  gN · (2/π) · ds / (1 + s²),

so the Cauchy with g is

  **f_g(s) = g · (2/π) / (1 + s²).**

- **The span is π/2 at every g** (R92: "it is always PI/2 at every g"). g never cuts into the breadth.
- **The height at home is g · (2/π)**, and height times span is g · (2/π) · (π/2) = g: the share expressed.
- **The shape is the same at every g**: the corner is the median (gN/2 addresses on each side of v = h; under the standard
  placeholder γ = 1, R142), the facing
  s ↦ 1/s maps f_g to itself, and the tail is fat, the share beyond s falling only as (2/π) arctan(1/s), with no mean.

The classical Cauchy is **the g = 1 case** (read below, under the four situations, as situation 3 with its far end
assumed), and there **g simplifies away** (R92: "at g = 1, it simplifies away"). What is
left looks like a probability density with a normalising constant: 2/π reads as bookkeeping, and (2/π) · (π/2) = 1 as a
fact about normalisation. With g in place, 2/π is the height of full expression and the 1 is one value of g (item 235).
The same constant sets the extent of a standard fisheye layout, N · unit · 2/π, again at g = 1 (§2.5). The classical
account keeps the shape and drops the sweep.

**Under the four situations** (1 October). The Cauchy is situation 3's, forced because v's breadth is not known (R138),
and situation 3's g is the count toward an end the reader cannot see: g = (2/π) · arctan(n · step) [the shortcut, not the sweep: swept from home, g is the count of steps taken (R148)], rising toward 1 and
never reaching it (R96, R141). So g = 1 is not a case a situated reader ever stands in. Writing it is assuming the count
has arrived, that v's breadth is known: **the classical Cauchy is situation 3 with its far end assumed**, and g simplifies
away because the assumption removes the one thing g measures, how far the reader has got toward an end it cannot see.
The share of N in use, used above, is that count written against an N assumed known. (This reading is Claude's, made on
Tom's go-ahead of 1 October.)

### 2.2 g is a sweep over addresses, not a value, and not the system

g is never chosen: "you don't pick a g of 0.5 … it is all g between 0 and 1"; "g is never chosen, it is the sweep over
the full expression of N to empty expression of N" (R93). So the addresses sweep two ways at once:

- **the turn**, the angle, spanning π/2 across the relations;
- **g**, from empty (g = 0) toward full expression (g = 1).

**The disc, withdrawn** (R140). Drawn with the turn as angle and g as radius, the sweep was said to fill the unit disc,
with the unit circle as its g = 1 rim, "what the sweep of g closes at full expression". Under the four situations that
picture does not stand. The unit circle is situation 1, both breadths known and equal, and has no g (R134, R140). g does
work only in situation 2, as the mix r(θ) = (1 − g) · 1 + g · B(θ) between the circle and a shape (Definition S2), and in
situation 3, as the count toward an unseen end (R141). Every slice at a single g (§4.3) is a stage of that count, not a
ring of a disc.

**Addresses, not data.** "g is simply how much of N is being used. but its addresses, not data." The lay is the address
scheme; it says nothing about what the addresses point to. **Whatever system is observed is overlaid onto this addressing
scheme** (R93), and the rest of the paper is what a reader holds of a system overlaid on it.

**Where the reader meets it.** Along each address's forward sweep, **nearness** D1/d runs from 1 at the corner to 0 at
the vanishing point (Prop. 4.2). (Earlier drafts called nearness g and read the forward sweep as a sweep of g; it is a
different quantity from the g of the four situations, and is renamed throughout, §13.) (Placing each point at its
direction with radius its nearness is inversion in the corner's sphere, x ↦ x/|x|² in units of D1: space beyond D1 fills the unit ball, the corner is
its rim and the vanishing point its centre. An observation, not ruled; item 236.)

**Any linear system, any N** (R95). "this is the addressing scheme to any linear system of data, irregardless of its
peridocity, or facings … the system itself, can be of any size N." The span, 0 to π/2, is fixed; N sets only the spacing,
span/N between neighbouring addresses, and can grow without limit, the addresses packing toward the continuum, where the
address map is lossless. The data may close into a circle or not; the scheme is indifferent to completion everywhere
except at the horizon, which for data that closes is a seam where it comes back round.

**N unknown** (R96). "If N is not know, things get interesting … the addresses get arranged differently." The lay
above divides the span by N, so it needs N. Without N (and the corpus's standing ruling is that any horizon means N is
unknown) there is nothing to divide by: each address must be placed as its item arrives and then left alone. Addresses
that stay put, keep the list's order and stay inside the span must crowd toward a limit, so they cannot also be even
(Prop. 2.3). The lay that keeps order and place is a fixed **step** in reading, counted out from home: item k at reading
k · step, turn arctan(k · step), crowding toward the horizon, which holds whatever has not yet arrived. g changes role
with it, and under the four situations it is this second role that stands: N unknown is situation 3 (R126, R128), and
the running total below is its g (R141). With N known, g **multiplies** the Cauchy, thinning addresses across the whole span. With N unknown, g is the
Cauchy's **running total** up to the reach, g = (2/π) · arctan(n · step) [the shortcut, not the sweep: swept from home, g is the count of steps taken (R148)]: a sector filling out from home. It reaches ½ at
the corner, where n · step = 1, so the step, the unit (for a reader alone, the reader itself; R89), decides where the
corner falls. The share left at the horizon, 1 − g ≈ (2/π)/(n · step), is the fat tail, and g = 1 is the horizon itself,
never reached. The two cases are laid out side by side in §2.5. The line between addresses and payload holds here too:
g(n) counts arrivals, not what they carry, and not how fast they come. An arrival claims the next address, exactly as an
address in use does when N is known; the difference is only who sets the count. What does change is that the sweep is
unfinished: with N known every address is laid; with N unknown what is laid is the sweep from 0 to g(n), and the rest of
it is the horizon.

**The unit circle and the unit sphere are addressing schemes** (R97). "seems like this captures the unit sphere and circle
perfectly. they are just an addressing schemes that you can lay any system of any size onto them." Neither holds data;
each is situation 1, both breadths known and equal (R134), and a system is laid onto it, never read out of it. Three things the
scheme does not do, each already in the record. **On the sphere, the lay is even only nearly.** The circle divides
exactly for any N (span/N); the sphere does not, since among spheres only S⁰, S¹ and S³ carry a group structure and the
only exactly even point sets on S² are the Platonic five. Lays for any N exist (the Fibonacci lattice, used throughout
our code) but are near-even, and a crossed lay puts in poles (§4). **With N unknown, the whole is never laid**: the lay
grows out from home and the rest is the horizon (R96, above); that is not the circle but the fisheye, situation 3 (R126,
R128). **Adjacency is the system's, not the scheme's**: the
scheme orders addresses; whether neighbouring addresses hold neighbouring things depends on how the system was laid
down, which is why the scheme is for a linear system (R95), and why a system with two coordinates of its own, laid on
the sphere, must be given an order first.

**When the circle and the sphere are needed** (R98). "they are needed when your system has periodicty and facings." A
linear system needs only the span, 0 to π/2: one facing, laid from home to the horizon (R95). The whole circle is needed
when the system comes back round: when it is periodic, so that its end is its start, or when it has facings, sides that
are met from different directions. The quarter span then leaves a seam at the horizon where the data returns (Sep 3);
the circle, four facings laid end to end, has no seam, because the scheme closes where the data does. (Facings laid from
homes to horizons are how a situated reader lays the circle; the circle itself, read uniformly, has no home or horizon,
R131.) The sphere is the
same need one dimension up: a system with facings all round, a closed surface met from every direction. (Periodic in two
independent directions is a different closure, the torus, circle by circle; not the sphere.)

**Where the backside is in the shape** (R104; *withdrawn 1 Oct by R139: there is no front or back without a situated place to
observe from, and shapes are views from nowhere. Kept for the record.*) "the circle and sphere are where the backside is a real thing. its in the
actual shape, not just the backside." And (15:15): "the quarter turn and semi-circle are not closed, they are not shapes,
like the circle and sphere are" (R105). One facing and two are open: a span of addresses from home to a horizon, or
between two horizons, with ends, and so not a shape. Every address faces the standpoint, and anything behind is behind
only because a payload met on the way stops the sweep. The circle and the sphere close, and closing is what makes a
shape; a closed shape has a back half of its own. Each reading v/h occurs once in the front and once in the back, so the ratio cannot tell them apart; only the
facing, which the pair (v, h) carries and the ratio does not, says which half an address is in. So with four facings or
eight, where the addressing is a shape, the backside is in it, and would be there in a transparent world. With one or two it is made
only by the magnitudes (§2.6). The four demos show the difference (Appendix B): the addresses of the circle and the sphere
have to be read in two rows, front and back, from two homes; the quarter and the semicircle need one.

**Index and turn** (R99). "0 to PI/2 is just another way to address values in an array. instead of breadth, it uses
density and the cauchy and g … it useful when you want to sweep the data, not just index it." An array already has an
address: the index i, laid by **breadth**, N cells of one unit, extent N · unit. The turn addresses the same cells by
**spacing** instead: the span is fixed, N sets how many addresses share it, the Cauchy says how they fall in reading,
and g says how many are in use. The two convert exactly (ADDRESS and READING in §2.5), and nothing is written to the
array (the circle is virtual; Sep 11). What the turn adds is the sweep. By index, a look at the data is a range of
cells, and its size grows with N. By turn, a view is a span of turn, 2 arctan a, the same at every N and every g
(Prop. 2.2): one gesture covers the array at any size. And the sweep is not of the relation alone (R100: "your not
sweeping just the breadth, but your sweeping its density as well"; "breadth" there is the relation, R106). Spacing enters
twice. **Along** the sweep, the Cauchy lay
changes it: addresses crowd toward home and thin toward the horizon in reading, so a sweep in turn crosses the array at a
spacing that varies as it goes. **Across** it, g thins the addresses in place: from every address in use to none, without moving one.
Turn and g together, the relation and the count, are what a situated reader sweeps at once (the disc that drew them, §2.2,
is withdrawn). Indexing sweeps breadth at one g, every cell; it names a cell. The turn
sweeps relation and g, with breadth divided out (§2.3). Four demos, built before the four situations, show it (Appendix B): one point in the disc sets
both sweeps, its angle the relation and its radius g (the disc picture is withdrawn, §2.2), for one facing (the Cauchy sweep), two (the semicircle sweep), four (the circle sweep) and eight (the sphere
sweep, where a direction needs two angles, so the ball is turned by dragging and g is set by pinching).

**g is not the zoom.** Across the sweep, which address is which follows the formula (R94: "its what ever the math
says … its actually … just the cauchy formula including the g"). f_g(s) = g · (2/π)/(1 + s²) keeps the reading s as its
coordinate and multiplies only the height, so an address keeps its reading and a lower g thins addresses in place. Every
relation among readings, the cross ratio among them, is then the same at every g (1.33509 at g = 1, 0.75, 0.5, 0.25 for
four addresses; item 239). g is the **level of detail**: it sets the step, span/(gN). The **zoom** is the Cauchy's other
parameter, the scale γ in (2/π) · γ/(γ² + s²): it moves the median to s = γ and keeps all N addresses, pressing them
toward home or the horizon. Since 1 October γ is read, by Claude, as the placeholder for v's breadth (R138): a shape whose v breadth
is b times h's, read as v/h, is the Cauchy with γ = b (R142), and the zoom is how a wrong placeholder looks. The two are independent dials, g · (2/π) · γ/(γ² + s²); the corpus's earlier "g as the zoom or
the level of detail" was half right. (Holding each address at its place along the array instead, turn = arc/g, moves the
readings with g, which the formula does not, and its cross ratio drifts: 1.335 → 1.363 as g goes from 1 to 0.25.)

**What it explains.** Why the unit circle appears in every classical account without being derived: the classical
account works with both breadths known and equal, normalized to 1, which is situation 1 (R134). Why the Cauchy appears
with it: the classical account writes the situated reader's lay with its far end assumed (§2.1). Why 2/π keeps turning
up: it is the height of full expression. Why the fat tail and the missing mean hold at every g: they
belong to the lay, the shape, not to how much of it is expressed.

### 2.3 Divide, sweep, point

*The headline result, in two steps (Tom, 17:37): "we've mislabled breadth. division removes the breadth or scale. so it is
relation and density are our two dimensions, the third is just magnitude that each combinatino of breadth and density
points to." Then (19:06), correcting "dimensions": "x,y,z are ortogonal dimensions. relation is divided than sweeped of all
densities, magnitude is what they point to. its not dimensional."*

**The names** (R118, Sep 30: "i dont think g 'density' is the correct word, its just the sweep from pure magnitude to pure address."). What the corpus called density is the sweep: g runs from
pure address (g = 0: in situation 3, home, with nothing reached; the record had "the unit circle as a scheme", withdrawn
by R131, R140) to the magnitude fully expressed (g → 1: the full shape, every address carrying its r; in situation 3,
never reached; §2.4). The word "density" is since dropped
altogether (R124, §2.4): g is, in situation 2, the ratio of the circle to the shape and, in situation 3, the count toward
an end the reader cannot see (R140–R141); the relation, to keep "sweep" for g, is the
turn from horizontal to vertical. Quotes below keep the word as it was said. *Wander* measures situation 3's g from
arrivals (§8.5): how many of a system's addresses the reader tells apart; near 1 every address
arrives with its own r, and near 0 the whole system falls on one of the reader's addresses and is only an address.

**Not dimensional** (R125). x, y and z are three orthogonal dimensions of the world: the coordinates of the view from
nowhere, which stay what they are. Observation from somewhere is not dimensional, and is not three of anything. *Tom
(30 Sep, 22:02): "lets not call observation dimensional, that is another confusing word. its something different, it needs a new word so not to confuse the situation." Every relation is laid across the whole sweep, and every relation at every g points to a magnitude: one, the system's.
The reader sweeps relations and g; magnitudes it can only meet (R116: "the magnitues are not a sweep of possible things,
they are a specific thing which is defined by the system"). The Cauchy does not need to know what that thing is: it lays
the relations and runs the sweep, and the magnitude is only pointed to. The system is orthogonal to the sweep, so
any system can be laid onto it (R117: "cauchy does not need to know what that thing is"; "the system is orothogonal, you
can lay any system onto the sweep geometry"). Nothing here is interchangeable as x, y and z are: a rotation turns x into
y, and nothing turns a relation into a value of g or g into a magnitude, because each is reached through the one before.
A reader walks them, inside a range of addresses that the parallel reading sets all at once and the serial
reading walks (R113):

    divide  →  sweep  →  point
    v/h        the relation, laid as a turn from home, across the whole sweep g        the magnitude each address reaches

The **division** takes two magnitudes, v and h, and removes their common scale; what is left is the relation. The **sweep**
lays that relation as a turn from the reader's home, by the Cauchy, and runs it across every g, from all of N in use
to none (R93). Each address the sweep lays **points** to a magnitude, which is whatever is there. g is how fully the
magnitude is expressed, and magnitude is what the sweep reaches. So the contrast between the two views is not one of
count: the view from nowhere is three orthogonal dimensions; the view from somewhere is not dimensional, and is walked as
divide, sweep, point. That is the paper's opening claim, "geometry is
the sweep of addresses that point to a magnitude" (R91), with its first step named: the addresses come from a division.

The relation, g and the magnitude are each unbounded in their own terms, and each has a form on [0, 1] a reader can hold (R108–R110: "Magnitude is
0 to inf"; "0 - 1 density is infinite as well"):

| step | what it gives | native range | on [0, 1] by | what bounding it needs |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **divide**: the relation | v/h, from 0 at home to ∞ at the horizon | [0, ∞) | the turn, arctan(v/h), × 2/π | nothing: v/h has no unit |
| **sweep**: g, the count toward an unseen end (situation 3) | how many addresses are visible | [0, ∞): N may grow without limit (R95) | g, a share of N; with N unknown, (2/π) · arctan(n · step) (R96) | N, or a step |
| **point**: the magnitude | what a (relation, g) pair points to: the amount the division took out | [0, ∞) | arctan(d/D1), the forward lay in turn (R87) | a unit: the reader's own, or an agreed one (R89) |

**One move bounds each.** Whenever the whole is not known, a reader holds each of them the same way: the Cauchy's
turn, arctan of a quantity on [0, ∞), landing in a finite span. Only the sweep has a second route, plain division by N, and
only when N is known. What differs is what each needs in order to be turned: the relation nothing, the sweep a count or a
step, magnitude a unit. (The [0, 1] of v and h in §1 is their share of their own all, the stuff the relation is made of;
the magnitude an address points to is the amount itself, and has no upper end. Unlike the other two, it is not swept:
[0, ∞) is where it may fall, and the system fixes where it does.)

**From the reader's side to the world's.** The record had the sweep as the reader's alone (R108: "interesting that g is a
reader dimension, not the worlds"; and Sep 24: "density is created by a reader's limitation"). Under the four situations
g belongs to neither side alone (R140–R141): in situation 2 it is the ratio of the circle to the shape, and the circle,
having no g, is why the view from nowhere's symmetric case has none; in situation 3 it is the count toward an end that
exists in the world and is unseen by the reader. Magnitude is the world's alone: it is there whether or not anything
reads it. The relation sits between: v/h belongs to the pair, but it becomes an address only when a situated reader lays
it as a turn from a home.

**Breadth is divided out.** Breadth is extent (Sep 24: "breadth means extent, not quantity"), and extent is exactly
what dividing v by h removes: double both and the relation does not move. An index addresses by breadth, N cells of one
unit laid end to end; the turn addresses by relation and g, with breadth divided out (R99). Breadth comes back in
one place only, EXTENT = g · N · unit · 2/π (§2.5), and only through the unit, which comes from agreement (R89). The
paper's earlier "sweep of breadth and density" (R100–R101) is this, the turn and the sweep, misnamed.

**The division and the sweep are addressing; the magnitude is the world.** The relation can be divided and swept before
anything is there: that is the lay, and "addresses, not data". Magnitude is what the addresses point to. So the two
backsides part here: the back half of a closed shape (R104, withdrawn by R139) was held to be already in the sweep, while anything hidden behind anything
else needs magnitudes, since only magnitudes stop a sweep (§2.6).

**Cautions.** The relation may take two readings, across and up, for data with facings in two directions; writing it as
one coordinate or two is a modelling choice for a finite set ("representing it as x and y is a modeling choice", Tom
17:22) and forced to two only for a continuum (the Sep 3 concession). And the claim is about what observation from
somewhere does, not a replacement for the view from nowhere's coordinates.

### 2.4 The four situations; the unit circle and sphere defined

*Tom (19:46): "our framing allows us to create a formal definition of the unit circle an unit sphere, not just an abstract
concept." (R114.)*

The classical account takes the unit circle as given: the points at distance 1 from a centre. Here it is built from
things already defined, a pair of magnitudes, the division, the facings and the sweep, and nothing is set to 1.

**The four situations, defined (1 October; R126–R143).** What follows first is the present statement. The numbered
definitions after it (2.1–2.6) are the 29–30 September record: where they give the unit circle a home, a horizon, a
corner, a division or a g, these supersede them, and a note says so at each.

**Definition S0 (breadth).** A breadth is an extent in one direction: how far a shape reaches that way (Sep 24: "breadth
means extent, not quantity"). The world is breadth, always fully expressed, whoever observes it (R124). What a reader may
or may not know is the breadth, and the four situations are set by that alone, not by the world.

**Definition S1 (situation 1: the circle; symmetric).** The breadths are known and the same in every direction, so they can
be normalized to 1 (R134). h and v are each read relative to the other: nothing is divided (R131). The circle is read
uniformly, evenly across the whole address space, with no situated observer (R126, R127): it is a view from nowhere,
with no home, no horizon, no corner (R131), no g (R140) and no front or back (R139). Its points are the same point set as
Proposition 2.1's representatives: one per direction, h² + v² = 1, signed by quadrant. The sphere is the same with three magnitudes.

**Definition S2 (situation 2: all other shapes; asymmetric).** The breadths are known but not the same in every direction:
B(θ). Each direction's breadth is how far the shape reaches that way, and each can be made a share of its own breadth,
but no single 1 serves both h and v (R137). A shape is also a view from nowhere, with no front or back (R139). Here g
exists, as the ratio of the circle to the shape (R140):

    r(θ) = (1 − g) · 1 + g · B(θ),        0 the circle, 1 the shape, between them parts of both.

**Definition S3 (situation 3: the fisheye; neither).** h's breadth is known; v's exists but is not known (R137). v is had
only in terms of h, v = (v/h) · h (R132, R136), so the reader divides, and only here: the reading s = v/h, with home, the
corner and the horizon (§1). It cannot be normalized, because one side is unknown (R135, R137). It is the only view from
somewhere (R139), and the only situation with a front side (s < 1) and a back side (s > 1, logarithmic) (R130). The
Cauchy is what it must use (R138): with a placeholder for v's breadth it is situation 2 with that placeholder (Claude's
reading: the placeholder is the Cauchy's scale γ, and the standard γ = 1 sets the corner at s = 1). Its g is real but runs toward an end the reader
cannot see (R141). It is a **hybrid** (R150): on the front side, the working horizon (R149), it behaves like
situation 2, a span with two known ends read in plain proportion; past the corner it adds what situation 2 has not, a
back side with no end, counted by doublings. g is likewise a position between known ends on the front side and a count
toward an unseen end on the back (R148). Both readers, serial and parallel, are in it: it is the only situation with a situated reader (R152).

**Definition S4 (situation 4: impossible).** Neither breadth known: nothing to count in and no home to lay from (R137).

**Definition S5 (situation 5: v approximately known).** h's breadth is known; v's is known only approximately (R151).
Everything is as in situation 3: the corner is known, the front side is the working horizon, the back side lies past it.
What differs is one number: v's breadth enters the reading as the Cauchy's scale, a whole-rung offset on the logarithmic
count (R147), and here that offset is known within bounds. So situation 5 sits between situations 2 (offset known) and 3
(offset unknown) on exactly that number. (Added for completeness; the bounds form is Claude's reading.)

**Situation 3 in terms of the others** (R143, below): a shape of situation 2, read through situation 1's assumption, with
situation 4's unknown on the v side. And the corner (R142, below): it is the middle of a lay only when the breadths are
equal; laid as the circle is, a shape with v's breadth b times h's has its middle at v/h = b.

**Definition 2.1 (address).** *(The situated reader's, situation 3; the view from nowhere does not divide, R131.)* A pair
(h, v) of magnitudes, not both zero, read in a facing f. Its relation is
s = v/h ∈ [0, ∞]. Two pairs are the same address when they have the same facing and the same relation: the division
throws their common scale away, so doubling both changes nothing.

**Definition 2.2 (the unit circle: the scheme and the completed set).** *(Superseded by Definition S1: the circle has no
home, horizon or g (R131, R140). The facings with homes and horizons described here are how a situated reader lays the
circle.)* The unit circle names two things, at the two ends
of the sweep (R118). **As a scheme** it is the rule for addresses over four facings: every relation s ∈ [0, ∞] in each
facing, one representative each (Prop. 2.1(a)), no magnitude, nothing laid. It is pure address, and where the sweep
starts: g = 0, home. **As a completed set** it is that scheme with every address laid, g = 1; with N unknown this is the
sweep's horizon, approached and never reached (R96). The point set is the same; what differs is how much of it is laid.
The facing supplies what the magnitudes never carry, the sign (R37): the four facings are the four sign patterns of the pair,
(+h, +v), (−h, +v), (−h, −v), (+h, −v). They join end to end as in the closure visual, a horizontal of 1 sweeping to a
vertical of 1, to a horizontal, to a vertical and back: home (s = 0) at the two horizontal ends, the horizon (s = ∞) at
the two vertical ends, and each facing starting where the last one stopped. The chart ψ = atan2(±v, ±h), with the signs
of the facing, lays it evenly in turn over [0, 2π).

**Definition 2.3 (the unit disc).** *(Superseded: situation 1 has no g, R140; for shapes the mix is Definition S2's.)* The sweep drawn: the turn as angle and g as radius, g ∈ [0, 1]. The radius counts
what is laid, not a size ("unit" is not a length, below): the centre, g = 0, is home, where nothing is laid and only the
scheme holds, drawn as a point because no address is in use; the rim, g = 1, is the completed set (R93), with N unknown
the horizon.

**Definition 2.4 (the unit sphere and ball).** *(Superseded by Definition S1, as 2.2.)* The same, with a triple (h, v₁, v₂) and two relations, s₁ = v₁/h across
and s₂ = v₂/h up, over eight facings, the eight sign patterns of the triple, as a scheme and as a completed set. Home is at the two ends
of the h axis and the horizon is the circle h = 0 between them. With the sweep it is the unit ball, and the
sphere is its g = 1 shell.

**Proposition 2.1.** (a) Every address has exactly one representative pair with h² + v² = 1. (b) Signed by their facings,
those representatives are the points of the classical unit circle. (c) Likewise every address of Definition 2.4 has exactly one representative with
h² + v₁² + v₂² = 1, and these are the points of the classical unit sphere.

*Proof.* (a) For relation s ∈ [0, ∞) the pair (1, s) scaled by 1/√(1 + s²) is the only multiple on h² + v² = 1 with both
entries non-negative; for s = ∞ it is (0, 1). (b) Signing by facing places the representative in its quadrant, and
atan2 of it is the chart ψ; every point (cos ψ, sin ψ) arises from exactly one address.
(c) As (a), with (1, s₁, s₂)/√(1 + s₁² + s₂²) and the octant's signs. ∎

**Proposition 2.4 (the corner separates the sides; R157).** *Tom (1 Oct, 20:24): "g is the sweep between 1 and 2, but not to the far horizon, but the near horizon, the corner. Let a situated reader hold h, known, and v, had as a count of h's; the reading is s = v/h on [0, ∞), from home (s = 0)
toward the horizon (s → ∞). The corner is s = 1. Then the corner is the one point that separates the front side
[0, 1) from the back side (1, ∞) symmetrically, and it is found with h alone.

*Proof.* (1) Reading the pair the other way round, h in terms of v, sends s to 1/s; done twice it returns s, and it
reverses order. (2) If s < 1 then 1/s > 1, and if s > 1 then 1/s < 1: the swap carries the front side onto the back
side, point for point, and back. (3) s = 1/s with s > 0 gives s² = 1, so s = 1: the corner is the only reading the swap
fixes. (4) A sweep from home grows continuously from 0, so it reaches 1 before it can pass it; every sweep from home to
the horizon passes through the corner. (5) s = 1 is v = h, a direct comparison of v with the known h: it needs neither
v's breadth nor an outside unit, and scaling v and h together leaves it where it is. ∎

On the front side v is a proper part of the known whole (v < h); on the back side the unknown side is the larger (v > h).
"Proper and improper fractions" (Claude's naming, unruled) is this proposition's reading of the two sides.

*Corollary (g ends at the corner).* g is a share of a known whole (R140–R141), and a part cannot exceed its whole, so g
exists only where v ≤ h: on the front side. Its sweep ends at the corner, the near horizon, and never reaches the far
horizon, whatever g's exact form. That g is exactly v/h there needs one more premise, that a share adds (the share of two
adjoining stretches is the sum of their shares): with g never decreasing, Cauchy's functional equation then makes g linear,
and its value 1 at the only known end other than home fixes g = v/h. The competitor v/(v + h), which reaches the far
horizon, does not add. The additivity premise is Claude's and is not yet ruled. Open: R141 places the circle at g = 0 and
the shape at g = 1, while this corollary puts equal breadths, the circle's condition, at g = 1, the corner; the
orientation is to be settled.

### 2.5 The addressing scheme with g, formally

*Under the four situations* (1 October): the scheme below is a situated reader's, situation 3. SCHEME, with N known, is
that reader's lay written as if the far end were known (§2.1); SCHEME_OPEN, with N unknown, is the lay as a situated
reader actually has it, and its g(n) is situation 3's g (R141). Neither is the circle, which has no g (R140).

Let N be the number of addresses, **span** the turn they cover (π/2 for one facing, π for a reader's two), and **unit** the
length one address occupies where it is laid out on a line. g is not an argument anyone supplies: the scheme is the whole
sweep of g over (0, 1] (R93). The procedures below state what holds at each g of that sweep.

```
SCHEME(N, span)                                   -- the addressing scheme: the sweep of g, never one value
  for i in 0 … N − 1:
      ψᵢ ← (i + ½) · span / N                     -- each address's turn: fixed for the whole sweep
  for every g in (0, 1]:
      IN_USE(g) ← { i : ⌊(i + 1) · g⌋ > ⌊i · g⌋ } -- gN of the N, thinned evenly in place (f_g: g × the Cauchy)
      place each i in IN_USE(g) at angle ψᵢ             -- neighbours ≈ span/(gN) apart in turn
  return the addresses in use at each g           -- (earlier drafts drew these as rings of a disc, withdrawn: §2.2)

TURN(s)            ← arctan(s)                    -- the turn of a reading s = v/h: independent of g

ADDRESS(s, N)      ← ⌊ TURN(s) · N / span ⌋       -- the address whose turn holds s (in use at g iff in IN_USE(g))

READING(i, N)      ← tan(ψᵢ)                       -- the same at every g

VIEW(ψ₀, a, g, N)                                 -- the addresses seen from focus ψ₀ through aperture a
  ψ₋ ← arctan(−a) ;  ψ₊ ← arctan(a)               -- the span seen: independent of g
  return { i ∈ IN_USE(g) : ψ₀ + ψ₋ ≤ ψᵢ ≤ ψ₀ + ψ₊ }

EXTENT(N, g, unit) ← g · N · unit · 2/π           -- height at home × count: the line the addresses occupy

POSITION(i, ψ₀, g, N, unit)                       -- where address i (in use at g) falls on that line, centred at 0
  E ← EXTENT(N, g, unit)
  return ( tan(ψ₀ + ψᵢ) + 1 ) / 2 · E − E / 2
```

**Proposition 2.2.** (a) The span of a view, ψ₊ − ψ₋ = 2 arctan a, does not depend on g. (b) The number of addresses in a
view is (ψ₊ − ψ₋) · gN / span, up to one at each end: proportional to g. (c) At g = 1 every procedure reduces to the
classical layout, with extent N · unit · 2/π and step span/N.

*Proof.* (a) arctan does not take g. (b) The addresses in the view are those in use whose fixed turns fall in an interval of
length ψ₊ − ψ₋; the thinning keeps a share g of them, evenly. (c) Substitute g = 1. ∎

So the one function that reads the world, arctan, is g-free: it returns the turn, whose span is π/2 at every g (R92). The
g = 1 assumption of the classical layout sits in the two steps around it: **which addresses are in use**, all N, and
**the extent**, N · unit · 2/π. Written with g and run against the classical layout, the procedures agree
exactly at g = 1 (20,000 random cases: differences 0; item 238). As g sweeps, with N = 1,000, span π (two facings) and a
view of aperture 0.5:

| g | addresses in use | step | extent (unit 40) | addresses in the view | span of the view |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1,000 | 3.14 mrad | 25,465 | 297 | 0.9273 rad |
| 0.75 | 750 | 4.19 mrad | 19,099 | 223 | 0.9273 rad |
| 0.5 | 500 | 6.28 mrad | 12,732 | 149 | 0.9273 rad |
| 0.25 | 250 | 12.57 mrad | 6,366 | 75 | 0.9273 rad |

The span of the view never moves; everything that counts addresses scales with g. The classical layout, like the
classical Cauchy, is the g = 1 case: the situated reader's lay with its far end assumed (§2.1).

**When N is unknown** (R96). SCHEME divides the span by N. Without N the addresses are laid as items arrive, by a
**step** in reading fixed in advance:

```
SCHEME_OPEN(step, span)                          -- N unknown: laid on arrival, never re-laid
  n ← 0
  on each arrival:
      ψₙ ← arctan(n · step + step/2)              -- a fixed step in reading from home; earlier turns never move
      n ← n + 1
  g(n) ← (2/π) · arctan(n · step)                 -- the share of the span reached: the Cauchy's running total
                                                  -- (the shortcut: the sweep itself counts the steps from home, R148)
  return the sector of turn g(n) · π/2            -- fills out from home; the horizon holds what has not arrived
```

**Proposition 2.3.** Let addresses be laid one per arrival in a span of turn, with no address ever moved after it is laid
and each new address past the last. Then the gaps between neighbours tend to zero, so the lay cannot be even. Keeping
evenness without N means giving up one of the other two.

*Proof.* The turns form an increasing sequence bounded by the span, so they converge; the gaps between successive terms
are then summable and tend to zero, while an even lay has every gap equal and positive. The two escapes are the two
things assumed: re-laying everything at each arrival (SCHEME with N as the running count, whose addresses move) or
placing each arrival by halving the span (45°, 22.5°, 67.5°, 11.2°, …: even at every power of two and never moved, but the
third item lands past the second, so the list's order is lost). ∎

SCHEME_OPEN is the escape-free case, and the fisheye in its own setting: situation 3 as a reader has it. The two lays side by side:

| | N known (SCHEME) | N unknown (SCHEME_OPEN) |
|---|---|---|
| lay | even in turn, span/N | even in reading, step |
| g and the Cauchy | a factor: g · (2/π)/(1 + s²) | its running total: (2/π) · arctan(n · step) |
| sweeping g | thins addresses across the span; turns fixed | fills a sector out from home; step fixed |
| g = 1 | every address in use | the horizon, never reached |
| what moves as the list grows | every address (span/N changes) | nothing already laid |

For n · step = 1, 2, 3, 10, 100, 1000, g = 0.5, 0.705, 0.795, 0.937, 0.9936, 0.99936. The share still at the horizon is
1 − g = (2/π) · arctan(1/(n · step)), about (2/π)/(n · step) for large n (0.00637 at 100, 0.00064 at 1000): the fat tail,
with 2/π as its coefficient. At the corner, n · step = 1, g is exactly ½, whatever the step; the step only decides how
many arrivals it takes to get there (item 242).

### 2.6 Addresses and payload

Tom's statement of what this work is about (R91): "geometry is not shapes, it is the sweep of addresses that point to a
magnitude. says nothing about what the magnitudes are." And, of the magnitudes: "the magnitudes are what create the
backsides and the hidden geometry. so they are not not irrelevent."

In one line: **geometry tells a reader where and how to look; it does not tell it what it will find. What it finds is
payload, and shape is a pattern in that payload across the addresses.**

**The hand and the rings** (R78, adapted). Hold your hand out flat: that is h. Turn it until it points straight up: that
is v. The motion between is the sweep, and every way of relating h to v along it is a finger. The fingers are the
addresses: laid by the sweep, before anything is on them. Put rings on the fingers: the rings are the payload, what each
finger meets. Change the rings and the hand does not change. Slide two rings onto one finger and, looking along it, you
see only the nearer: that is the first border, and the ring behind is the backside. The hand hid nothing; the rings did.
The serial reader is the hand: it knows where each finger points, v/h, but not how big a ring is. The parallel reader
holds the rings: their sizes, at a moment, but not which finger a ring is on without the hand. They meet at the corner:
the one finger the facing leaves where it is (Prop. 1.1), and the one ring whose two parts are equal. And g is how open
the hand is (R102): fingers fully extended is g = 1, every address in use; the closed fist is g = 0, none. A curled
finger has not been pointed somewhere else; it is out of use, and the span of the hand is the same however far it is
open. Opening and closing it through every position between is the sweep of g. So the hand has two freedoms (R103): how
many fingers, N, which sets how finely the span is divided and where each finger points (R95); and how far they extend, g,
which sets how much of N is expressed. In the Cauchy they meet only in the count in use, gN; kept apart, N fixes where the
fingers point and g how far the hand is open. The metaphor goes no further than that; the rest is the definitions.

The paper is organised by that split. A reader has two kinds of thing, and they come from different places.

**The addresses** are the reader's: laid in advance from its home, by the Cauchy (situation 3, R138), before anything is out there, and the same whatever the world
holds (§3–§4). Everything proved about the lay is a fact about addresses alone and holds whatever the payload: the Cauchy
family's corner shares and medians (Prop. 4.1), the poles of crossed lays (§4.2, §4.4), the forward reading's corner at
D1 and its grain (Prop. 4.2–4.3), the facing's fixed corner (Prop. 1.1). So does the fact that nearness is in the reader's own unit, D1: the
addresses never move, only what they find does.

**The magnitudes** are payload: what the sweep meets along each address. Nothing in the addressing says what they are. But
they are not incidental, because **they make the first border**, and with it every hidden thing in this paper. The
blocked view and the hidden view (R90) exist because a sweep stops at what it meets: a transparent world would have
neither. The backside has one source, the magnitudes, met by a situated reader (R139 withdraws the second source once
given here, a back half in the shape of the addressing itself, R104). The hollow no company round about reaches (§5.3), the holes behind a moving thing (§10.3), a record's holes
(§9.2), the middle hiding takes away (§8.4), which of two things is in front (§9.4): all are made by the magnitudes. So is
contact (two nearnesses equal on one address), and so is nearness.

**Shape** is neither. It is recovered from payloads through the addresses, as far as the ladder allows (§7): up to a
projective map from relations between readers, up to a scale from a reader's own lay or its own nearness, and never with a unit
(R89). A sphere, here, is a pattern in the payload across the addresses, and how well a standpoint holds it depends on how
the surface faces the sweep (§5.1).

**The boundary.** Several results use more than "a magnitude at each address": they use that the sweep is a straight line
and the payload is the first thing it meets. Occlusion, contact, the cross ratio passing between standpoints, and the
ladder's projective rung all rest on it (assumption 4, §11). For a payload that is a value at a point rather than a surface
met along a line, the addressing results hold; the rest is untested (§13).

## 3. The reader and the three views

*In the split: the three views differ only in how the addresses are read (all at once, one at a time, or with no
standpoint); the payload they meet is the same. Throughout, "a reader" is the situated reader, situation 3 of §2.4: the
only view from somewhere (R139).*

### 3.1 What a reader has

A **reader** stands somewhere and has:

1. **addresses**, laid in advance over its front half from its home, by the Cauchy with the standard placeholder γ = 1
   (§4, R138), the same whatever the world holds. ("Front half" is the hemisphere it faces; R130's front side and back
   side are the two halves of one facing's reading, below and above the corner.)
2. along each address, a **forward sweep** that keeps the **first border** it meets: which thing, and how near (R79);
3. **nearness**, how near the first border is, 1 out to a distance D1 and D1/distance beyond, falling toward 0 at the
   vanishing point. It is in the reader's own unit, D1. (Earlier drafts called it g, the same letter as the g of R80–R81 and of the four situations; it is a different
   quantity.)
4. **contact**: two things touch where their nearnesses are equal on the same address (R82).

Four rules then do all of a reader's work: lay the addresses; read the first border along each; carry the jump between
neighbouring addresses that read different things as a primitive (the edge); make contact where two nearnesses are equal on one
address (item 139–140). Occlusion, clipping, the horizon and the vanishing point are never handled; they follow. The
address and the payload are separate: the addresses never move, only what they find does (item 141). Acting needs only
the address and nearness; a picture is for recognition (item 151).

### 3.2 Three views

A reader encounters the world in one of two ways, and the classical accounts take a third.

- **Parallel.** Every address at once. It **establishes the range of addresses this observation consists of**, and
  through them points to the **magnitudes** (nearness, the forward sweep), which stay in the system. It takes no snapshot (R113:
  "its still a parallel reading, but all thats needed is just define the range of addresses that this observation
  consists of"; and 19:26: "we don't actually take a snap shot, we just point to the magnitude"). It is parallel because
  what it points to already exists all at once (19:27). There are two layers of indirection: the serial reading points
  into the range of addresses, and the range points into the system (19:33). The range is the reader's, set before
  anything arrives (Tom, 30 Sep 05:08: "paralellel reading provides a range of address, that is set by the reader. it
  does not take a snapshot, but creates a range of addresses to scan with the serial reader"), and what arrives is placed
  in it by v over h (05:33: "from the v/h we create an address back to the parallel reader, who has the address to the
  system"). Each thing in the range is a distinct signal, and so a distinct serial read (05:18: "a reader receives
  multiple signals. each of the other objects in their parallel read would be a distinct signal").
- **Serial.** One address at a time, inside the range the parallel reading set, in an order of its own, while the world moves. It holds **ratios** (turns, and the
  sway: how fast things slide across its addresses as it moves) **over time**.
*A note on the simulations.* The tests below implement the parallel reader as a snapshot: the magnitude along every
address, copied at one moment. That copy is a **record** (§9), not the parallel reading. Where a result speaks of a
snapshot, and of its being *then*, it measures the record, and the delay belongs to the record, or to the serial reading
reaching an address; the parallel reading's own limit is what falls outside the range it sets (R113).
- **The view from nowhere.** No standpoint (R127): the circle and every other shape, read uniformly, with no home,
  horizon, corner or division (R131) and no front or back (R139). In Tom's words (09:17): "it does not have g, it all g's
  at the same time, so has no perspective, its all perspectives." (Of g, R140 now says: the circle has none; other shapes
  do, as the ratio of the circle to the shape.) **It holds what no single standpoint holds: "the hidden view, the blocked
  view, the backside"** (R90), the backside now read as what lies behind a situated reader (R139).

The simulations need a view from nowhere to run at all: a game loop holding positions and updating them (R83: "Computers
have a game loop, that is the world. … realize reality does not have game loops"). The readers never use it; the scoring
does, and says so. Tom, 30 Sep 05:14: "i think this is where the game loop really hurts us. it subverts the situation."
§8.5 describes a reader built without one: no shared now, and nothing reaching the reader but signals. The phrase "view from nowhere" is Nagel's (1986); here it names a geometric role, not a
philosophical position.

## 4. Laying the addresses

*In the split: addresses alone. Every result in this section holds whatever the payload.*

### 4.1 The Cauchy family

A reader in space has **two across readings** (a direction on its front half) and **one forward reading** (how far along
it). The question of how to lay them has one answer that treats every direction alike.

Let S^k be the unit sphere in ℝ^{k+1} with pole u₀. The **gnomonic** map sends a point u with u₀ > 0 to x = (u₁, …, u_k)/u₀
∈ ℝ^k. Uniform measure on the hemisphere maps to the k-variate Cauchy density

  f_k(x) = c_k (1 + |x|²)^{−(k+1)/2}.

For k = 1 this is §1's lay; for k = 2 it is the **even sky**, the front half laid evenly (in the corpus's bar notation,
h³/2π); for k = 3 it is the lay of the reader's whole space, with the reader's own point as home.

**Proposition 4.1 (the corner and the median).** Under f_k, the share of addresses within the corner (|x| < 1) and the
median |x| are

| k | within the corner | median \|x\| |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1/2 | 1 |
| 2 | 1 − 1/√2 ≈ 0.2929 | √3 |
| 3 | 1/2 − 1/π ≈ 0.1817 | tan ψ₃ ≈ 2.264 |

where ψ₃ solves ψ − sin ψ cos ψ = π/4.

*Proof.* Write |x| = tan ψ, ψ the angle from the pole. Uniform measure on the hemisphere of S^k gives ψ the density
proportional to sin^{k−1} ψ on [0, π/2]. The corner is ψ < π/4. For k = 1 the share is 1/2. For k = 2,
∫₀^{π/4} sin ψ dψ / ∫₀^{π/2} sin ψ dψ = 1 − cos(π/4). For k = 3, ∫₀^{π/4} sin² ψ dψ = π/8 − 1/4 and ∫₀^{π/2} sin² ψ dψ =
π/4, giving 1/2 − 1/π. The medians solve the same integrals set to half: ψ = π/4, ψ = π/3, and ψ₃ as stated. ∎

(Checked by laying 4 × 10⁵ addresses at random from each lay: 50%, 29.3%, 18.2%; items 133, 217.) These are the lay's
figures, under the standard placeholder γ = 1. Arrivals from a shape whose breadths differ do not keep them: with v's
breadth b times h's, the middle of one reading moves to b and the share within the corner to (2/π) · arctan(1/b) (R142).

### 4.2 Crossed lays have poles

The lay every early demo used was **crossed**: evenly left to right, then evenly up and down at each. Over a whole front
half that lay has two poles, straight up and straight down, where all left-to-right addresses meet. Its spread of addresses on the
sphere is not uniform, so it prefers directions.

*The Even Sky* (item 187) laid 1,600 addresses three ways and set eight equal balls on a ring round home, all at v/h 1.19.
Addresses per ball, most over fewest:

| | crossed turns | even sky | crossed Cauchys |
|---|---|---|---|
| level head | 1.63 | 1.13 | 1.19 |
| head tilted ⅛ turn | 1.63 | 1.13 | 1.19 |
| which balls get most | the two at the poles | none | the four on the axes |
| within the corner | 20% | **29%** | 22% |
| median v/h | 2.69 | **1.73** | 2.19 |
| nearness on one ball ×1000 | 885–887 | 887–889 | 886–889 |

Under crossed turns the surplus sits at the poles and moves with the head: it belongs to the lay, not to the world. The
even sky's 1.13 is the grain of the spiral that lays it. **Nearness is the same under every lay and every tilt**, since it is
read along one address; only what is counted *across* addresses depends on the lay. Order by sway, which is counted
across addresses, shows it: of 28 pairs, the even sky ordered 27.2 on average over 193 random tilts (all 28 in 105), the
crossed lay 24.8 (all 28 in 29) (item 189).

Ruled (R84): **in 3d the across readings are one even sweep over the front half**, the even sky. "Left–right" and
"up–down" name directions in it; they are not two sweeps.

The even lay is a choice, with a reason: it is the one lay that prefers no direction (uniform measure on the sphere is the
only one every rotation keeps). Other lays are possible, such as a retina's, denser at the centre, or a sensor's grid, and
a reader may use one. Each then puts its own preferences into whatever is counted across addresses (the slide, the share
of addresses on a thing), as crossed turns did. What is read along one address (nearness, the first border, contact) is the same
under every lay.

### 4.3 The forward reading

Depth had only nearness. Lay it the same way: evenly in turn, d = D1 · tan ψ, ψ ∈ [0, π/2).

**Proposition 4.2 (nearness is the forward reading from its far end; R87).** On this lay, nearness = min(1, cot ψ). Its corner falls at
d = D1, the reader's own unit.

*Proof.* Nearness = min(1, D1/d) = min(1, cot ψ). The corner ψ = π/4 is d = D1. ∎

So nearness reads the forward reading from the vanishing point's end (h/v rather than v/h) and clips it at the corner: it holds
the half beyond D1 and reads 1 for everything within. That is why the demos found near order within D1 invisible to nearness and
recoverable only from the sway (§5.2). Like g (R80: "g is just [0,1]. no horizon."), nearness has no horizon, and so no
corner; the corner is in the reading nearness is clipped from.

**The span and the count (R92).** The relations between h and v span ∫₀^∞ ds/(1 + s²) = π/2, counted evenly in turn
(π/4 each side of the corner). That span is π/2 at every g: "it is always PI/2 at every g." g, the share of addresses
in use (R93), sets how many express it, gN of N, spaced π/(2gN), gN · (2/π) · ds/(1 + s²) of them per ds. At
g = 1 the factor simplifies away and the classical equation remains: classical accounts are the g = 1 case, the situated
reader's lay with its far end assumed (§2.1). The Cauchy's height at home is then g · (2/π), and height times span is g: at g = 1 this is the identity
(2/π) · (π/2) = 1, with 2/π reading as a normalisation; with g, 2/π is the height of full expression and the 1 is one
value of g (item 235). Each value of g here is one stage of situation 3's count (R141; the disc of earlier drafts is
withdrawn, §2.2); g itself is never chosen but swept (R93).

**Proposition 4.3 (the fisheye in depth).** With addresses Δψ apart, the relative depth grain is Δd/d = 2Δψ / sin 2ψ. It is
least at the corner (Δd/d = 2Δψ at d = D1) and the same at d and D1²/d.

*Proof.* d = D1 tan ψ gives Δd = D1 sec² ψ Δψ, so Δd/d = Δψ/(sin ψ cos ψ). sin 2ψ is greatest at ψ = π/4 and unchanged
under ψ ↦ π/2 − ψ, which is d ↦ D1²/d. ∎

Measured with 1,000 depth addresses (item 216):

| lay of the forward reading | within the corner | Δd/d at d/D1 = 0.1 / 1 / 10 / 100 | 8 things at 0.01–1000 D1, pairs of 28 in order |
|---|---|---|---|
| evenly in steps (to 100 D1) | 1% | 1.0 / 0.10 / 0.010 / – | 25.7 |
| evenly in nearness | 0% | – / – / 0.010 / 0.10 | 23.2 |
| **evenly in turn** | **50%** | 0.016 / **0.0031** / 0.016 / 0.18 | **27.7** |

### 4.4 Three readings: one even lay, or a pole at the reader

With both across readings and the forward reading, the question of §4.2 recurs one reading up. **Crossed** (the even sky,
with a forward reading laid in turn along each address), the number of addresses per unit volume is

  ρ_crossed(r) ∝ 1 / (r² (1 + r²)),

which diverges at the reader's own point: every address's forward reading starts there. **As one even lay** (the 3d
Cauchy),

  ρ₃(r) ∝ (1 + r²)^{−2},

finite and flat at the reader and thinning as r^{−4} far off, as the even-nearness lay does.

*Both follow from §4.1:* the crossed lay is uniform over directions times f₁(r) along each, divided by the r² of the
spherical shell; the one even lay is f₃. ∎

| lay of the reader's space | within the corner | median r/D1 | within 0.1 D1 | addresses per volume at r/D1 = 0.01 / 0.1 / 1 / 10 (÷ at 1) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| even sky × even nearness | 0% | 2.0 | 0% | 0 / 0 / 1 / 10⁻⁴ |
| even sky × even turn (crossed) | 50% | 1.0 | **6.4%** | **2·10⁴** / 200 / 1 / 2·10⁻⁴ |
| **3d Cauchy** | **18.2%** | **2.26** | 0.04% | **4.0 / 3.9** / 1 / 3.9·10⁻⁴ |

The crossed lay spends 6.4% of all its addresses within 0.1 D1, a thousandth of the corner's volume: the poles of §4.2,
now at the reader. The one even lay reads space near the reader as evenly as a grid would, and far off as even nearness does; the
corner is where one hands over to the other. Ruled (R87): **the forward reading is laid in turn, its corner at D1; with the
across readings it is one even lay, the 3d Cauchy.**

### 4.5 A second lay, for presenting

Reading and presenting are different roles (R85). The even sky is the lay a reader **reads and measures** with. For
presenting its readings continuously, a circle of circles with its pole at home serves: every great circle through home
is a one-reading lay, evenly in turn, and the Tangent Bridge kernel K_N(x) = sin(Nx/2)/(N tan(x/2)) presents readings on
it exactly at the readings and bounded between (checked to 10⁻¹⁴ at the readings; a smooth field to 1.3 × 10⁻⁵; item 208).
That lay keeps the one-reading property that the corner is the median (50% within v = h; item 207) and bunches addresses
toward home.
It rings at every edge, as the kernel predicts at its bandwidth.

## 5. What one reader holds

*In the split: payload through one reader's addresses. What faces the sweep is held; what the payload hides is not.*

### 5.1 Every standpoint holds best what faces it

A reader reads other things' surfaces from its own point. Where a surface faces it, neighbouring addresses land on it at
small spacing; where the surface turns past the corner, facing more along the address than across it, the addresses meet
it at a graze and hold it poorly.

This showed in the rung editor's pieces, which are stored as readings from their own axis: θ evenly round, h evenly up,
and as payload the first border out, r. That is a reader standing in the piece. The error of that reader gathers where
the surface turns past the corner (|dr/dh| > 1): 5% of the bishop's and queen's skin carries 15–17% of the error; 1% of the rook's,
18%; 14% of the cube's, 55% (item 197). A reader at the piece's middle, on the even sky, holds compact pieces better and
tall ones worse (rook 13.0% of volume wrong against 1.9% from the axis), because it reads their long ends at a graze
(item 196). **Every standpoint holds best what faces it and worst what it grazes; the corner is where a surface stops
facing it.** A prediction that the axis reader's error would gather at its caps, as at a crossed lay's poles, was killed
(item 197).

### 5.2 Two kinds of depth

Nearness decides depth beyond D1 and says only "all here" within it. Of eight still pieces, five within D1, nearness decided 18 of 28
pairs, all rightly; the first border (which is read where two meet) decided 8, all rightly; the **sway**, how fast each
piece slides across the addresses as the reader moves, decided all 28, 26 rightly (item 175). So there are **two kinds of
depth, one per reader**: nearness, the parallel reader's magnitude, which saturates; and the sway, the serial reader's ratio,
which does not saturate but needs the reader to move (item 176).

### 5.3 The back, the self, the hollow

- **The back.** A front half reads half of what comes at it. A reader that turns reads everything first, one half after
  the other, and pays in warning: standing still, 50% read first; turning steadily at either rate tried, or about-facing, 100%,
  warning median 6.9–8.6 s against 9.7 s (item 173): any turning that brings the front half round sooner than a thing
  takes to arrive reads everything first. What stays unread is what arrives faster than the turn comes round.
- **The self.** Standing on its own head, a piece's reader finds its own body as its nearest first border, filling a third
  or more of its sky (about a fifth on a pointed top) (item 200).
- **The hollow.** A vessel's hollow is read from round about only through its mouth. Readers evenly round it read 1.1% of
  the hollow with 4, 32% with 64, 96% with 512, and 0.7% by none, however many; one reader standing in the hollow reads
  all of it (item 199). And reading is not symmetric between standpoints: every piece on a board reads the vessel; the
  vessel's reader, in its hollow, reads none of them (item 200).

## 6. What passes between readers

*In the split: addresses do not cross standpoints, and neither does nearness; relations among payloads do.*

Readers standing apart share no address, no nearness and no unit. What passes is **relations among readings of things both
read**.

| what is placed | what passes | measured |
|---|---|---|
| a point on a row of three landmarks | one number: the cross ratio of the four readings | 22 of 22 (a raw address: 4 of 22) |
| a point on a plane of four landmarks | two numbers: its place in their frame | 17 of 20 (a raw address: 0–1 of 20) |
| a point anywhere | four numbers per reader (its line of sight in two frames); two lines cross at the point | 26 of 26 |

(Items 159–164.) The cross ratio is the classical invariant of four collinear points under projection; in the corpus's
terms it is the relation of four readings v/h unchanged by a change of facing or of standpoint. Each step failed before
it worked, and each failure is a condition: landmarks thicker than the grain, readable from every side, a plane seen from
above rather than at a graze, readers spread up and down as well as across (items 160, 162, 164).

**Nearness does not cross standpoints.** Between readers in one place, nearness differs by one factor (the ratio of their D1s: measured
2.86 against 2.857 predicted), and a landmark both read is a shared unit for it (spread 1%). Between readers apart, the
spread is about 50% and no landmark removes it (items 158, 165). A **raw address** does not cross either: beside you, a
reported address lands on the thing 15–19 times in 20; 45 units off, 0–2 in 21 (item 157).

**A shared when.** Readers with no common clock share a *when* the same way they share a *where*: the cross ratio of a
moving landmark's turn with three still ones is the same for every reader at the same moment. Carrying readings to that
beat corrects straight motion, not curved (items 169–170).

**What a painter who sees everything can counterfeit.** A world painted on a wall, exact from one standpoint, fools the
serial reader and every relation between readers once the painter gives each reader its own picture, hidden edge-on from
the others. It cannot counterfeit **nearness**, because nearness is where the forward sweep actually stops (painted things read 58–87
thousandths where the real ones read 8–15), and it cannot counterfeit **contact** (items 179–186). The left and right
sweeps carry the picture, which can be painted; the forward sweep carries nearness, which cannot.

## 7. The ladder: what fixes what

*In the split: shape recovered from payloads. Each rung says how much comes back, and from what.*

The classical hierarchy of geometries (projective, affine, similarity, rigid) appears from somewhere as a ladder, and each
rung comes from a different kind of thing (R86).

### 7.1 Relations give the projective rung; a second reader is needed off the plane

Twenty-four posts anywhere in a block 100 across, readers round it passing **only relations**. Two readers find each
other from ten or more shared posts and place every post both read, up to a projective map of space (item 191). Error on
the block, grain 1/300, 20 worlds:

| readers | relations only: up to a projective map | as a shape | + "a parallelepiped" | + "a cube" | + "one length" |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | nothing placed | – | – | – | – |
| 2 | 0.25 | 13.2 | 0.71 | 0.80 | 0.81 |
| 8 | 0.08 | 15.7 | 0.16 | 0.17 | 0.18 |

With no grain every rung is exact (below 10⁻⁹). **No number of readers climbs a rung; only a shared fact does.** Off the
plane the bottom rung needs a second reader (a plane's readings are already a projective image of it; in space one
reader's readings are directions and place nothing), and the facts must be solid: by counting, a parallelogram fixes two
of the three numbers the affine rung needs in space, and a square two of the five the similarity rung needs.

### 7.2 A reader's own lay, or its own nearness, gives the shape up to a scale

Relations are lay-free. But a reader on an even sky also knows the **turn** between any two of its addresses, and it
reads **nearness** along each (item 193):

| passing | readers | shape up to a scale, no shared fact | as it stands, no shared length |
|---|---|---|---|
| relations only | 2 / 8 | 13.2 / 15.7 | 37 / 38 |
| + own turns | 2 / 8 | **0.26 / 0.13** | 39 / 40 |
| + own nearness | 1 / 8 | **0.51 / 0.17** | 36 / 38 |

The reader's own even sky is the fact the middle rungs need. And one reader alone reaches the shape if its forward
reading is laid in turn with its corner where the world is (Prop. 4.3): with posts 41–116 away, D1 = 70 gives 0.26, where
even nearness does best at D1 = 15 (0.51) and fails once D1 passes the nearest things (8.8 at D1 = 70) (item 218).

### 7.3 The rotation, the count, the place

- **The rotation** from a reading of something far. From a planet, turns held against the far stars left only the scale
  to find: the orbits' shapes came out within 0.13–0.21% for Mercury to Mars (from Earth, at a grain of 1′), from a 120-year diary and nothing else
  (item 201). In a maze, with no stars, the carried heading drifted 7.7° in one round; one far tower over the walls held
  it to 0.05° (item 203).
- **The count of turns** from a carry. Pledge's escape from a maze with islands rests on a carried winding count, and
  drifts with it (in 12 × 12 mazes, 85% escaped at 10° drift a turn and 42% at 20°; in 20 × 20, 71% and 22%). A far tower gives the fraction of a turn exactly, and the
  carry then only keeps whole turns: 100% at every drift tried, 2–20° a turn, as long as the drift between readings
  stays under half a turn (items 205–206). Reading gives the part of a turn; the carry gives the
  count.
- **The place** from closing a loop, or from marks left in the world. Back at its start post the walker's place came right
  (0.04), but a round post carries no turn, so its heading did not; with the tower as well, the map was off by a tenth of
  a cell (item 203). Trémaux writes its memory into the world and needs neither (item 205).

### 7.4 The unit is not geometric

**Proposition 7.1 (no reading gives a unit).** Scale the world about a reader by λ > 0 and its D1 by λ. Every reading is
unchanged.

*Proof.* Turns are unchanged by scaling about the standpoint. Nearness = min(1, D1/d) is unchanged when D1 and d scale together.
The first border along each address is the same surface. ∎

So a reader's lengths are in its own unit, and two readers' units are related by nothing either reads alone. Five readers,
each with its own D1, body and clock, each report the height of one post (item 214):

| how they report | spread of the five reports |
|---|---|
| each in its own unit | 34% |
| each in **one reader's body**, which every reader reads | **0.85%** (the grain) |
| radar, timed on each reader's own clock | 16% |
| radar, timed in the flashes of **one lamp all see** | **0.00%** |

Any thing all the readers can read, agreed to be 1, serves; readers who can read each other always have one. Radar is the
same agreement in disguise: a signal's speed and a beat, both shared. The last rung of the ladder ("+ one length") was
never information about the world; it was agreement with a unit someone chose. Ruled (R89): **"the unit … only comes
from aggreement. it is not geometric."** And: **"a solo reader would have no need for a unit, because that is how you
communicate with other readers. if you only need to communicate with yourself, a unit can just be you."** The geometry
from somewhere ends at the shape up to a scale; the unit is a matter of communication laid over it.

The reader's own D1 is that solo unit. Nearness = D1/d is a ratio of two lengths, so any agreed unit cancels in nearness itself. What
D1 does is place the reader's corner: it decides which things are in contact (d ≤ D1) and scales every nearness by one factor.
It does not touch a turn or any relation among turns, so no reading of shape depends on it. It is a property of the
standpoint, not a magnitude of the world. The step of §2.5's open lay plays the same part: the corner falls where
n · step = 1.

### 7.5 The ladder in one table

| what is fixed | where it comes from |
|---|---|
| the world up to a projective map | relations between two readers, or one reader and a return in time |
| the shape up to a scale | the reader's own lay (its turns) or its own nearness |
| the rotation | a reading of something far |
| the count of turns | a carry |
| the place | closing a loop, or marks left in the world |
| the unit (not geometric) | an agreement: a thing all read, called 1 |

Beside the reading itself, three kinds of thing appear: a **carry**, which keeps what drifts between readings; a
**relay** (§9), which passes some of what it met on; and an **agreement**, which is communication, not geometry.

## 8. Time: the serial reader

*In the split: the order in which addresses are read is itself a lay, in time, while the payload moves.*

Every result so far was read in parallel, at the moment the game loop supplies. A serial reader reads one address at a
time, in an order of its own, and holds each address as it was when last read.

### 8.1 A still world is received exactly

**Proposition 8.1.** If nothing moves, a serial reader's record equals a parallel reading, whatever its order.
*Proof.* Each address's first border does not depend on when it is read. ∎

So the hollow of §5.3, still, is received whole by one serial reader within it, a sweep at a time (item 212).

### 8.2 The order is a lay in time

A moving thing comes out where it was when its addresses were read: stretched, shrunk or torn, according to whether the
sweep ran with it, against it, or crossed it twice. How far each ball's received middle is off, in steps between
addresses, with 1 s sweeps (item 211):

| order | across | round | bobbing |
|---|---|---|---|
| spiral from home | 20 | 28 | 14 |
| round home | 9.5 | 16 | 6.6 |
| rows | 12 | 17 | 8.1 |

Every order distorts, differently, and none is free of it. The reader's own motion is written in by the same rule:
swaying through one 1 s sweep moved its still posts by up to 3 steps in the spiral (which reads home first), 11 round home
and 17 in rows.

### 8.3 Carrying to the present

The serial reader knows its order, so it knows when each address was read. From its last sweeps it finds how each thing
moved and carries each reading on to a common moment.

**Proposition 8.2 (the chord's error).** Let p(t) be a thing's place on the reader's chart, read at moments t_a < t_b
and carried along the chord to t > t_b. The error is ½ p''(ξ)(t − t_a)(t − t_b) for some ξ ∈ (t_a, t). With t_b − t_a = T and
t − t_b ≤ T it is at most sup|p''| · T². Carrying along the parabola through three readings leaves an error of order
sup|p'''| · T³.

*Proof.* The Lagrange remainder of linear (respectively quadratic) interpolation, applied outside the interval. ∎

**Corollary 8.3 (what a carry can follow).** For a change of amplitude A at angular frequency ω (p = A sin ωt), read every
T, the chord's error is at most A(ωT)² and the parabola's at most A(ωT)³. So a carry follows a change while ωT is
small, and nothing carried follows it once ωT reaches about 1: the reading must come round faster than the change turns.

*Proof.* |p''| ≤ Aω² and |p'''| ≤ Aω³ in Proposition 8.2; for the parabola the remainder is p'''(ξ)/6 · (t − t₀)(t − t₁)(t − t₂),
at most sup|p'''| · T³ for t within one reading of the last. ∎

The carry therefore corrects motion whose path on the chart is nearly straight and uniform over the readings used, and
fails for a thing that turns within them. Measured (items 213, 219), the threshold sits where the corollary puts it: three
sweeps within about half a period is 3T < π/ω, that is ωT ≲ 1:

| mover (alone), sweep | as read | chord (2 sweeps) | curve (3 sweeps) | 3 sweeps as a share of the period |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| straight, 1 s | 4.7–8.2 | **0.3–0.5** | – | – |
| swinging, 0.5 s | 5.1 | 1.8 | **0.7** | 0.21 |
| round, 0.25 s | 4.6 | 1.9 | **1.2** | 0.20 |
| round, 1 s | 16.6 | 25.7 | 34.9 | 0.81 |

**A carry follows a curve only if the readings come faster than the thing turns**: three sweeps within about half its
period. Near a whole period nothing carried helps, and carrying through more sweeps makes it worse.

### 8.4 Hiding takes the middle, not the place

Among other things, a straight mover passing behind posts and balls was carried badly (4.2–9.6 → 2.8–8.6, item 213): its
addresses were read in parts at different moments, so their middle was not its middle. Placing it instead by the centre
of a circle fitted to **its outline where it meets background** (floor, sky: there it is surely in front), and carrying
each outline point on from its own moment, gave 0.5–0.8 steps from its true centre with 1 s sweeps, against 3.0–5.0 by
middles and 0.3–0.5 when alone (item 224). An ellipse, with five numbers to fit on an outline about seven steps across,
was unsteady; a circle, with three, held. Things that are not round obey the same rule: a still post placed by the middle
of its addresses was moved up to 10 steps by balls hiding parts of it at different moments; placed halfway between its
left and right edges against background, it came right to the grain (item 230). **A thing's place is in its edges
against background.**

With outlines and a choice between chord and curve made from its own three centres (the curve if the middle centre stands
off the chord of the outer two by more than 1.5 steps), the reader keeps straight motion on the chord with 1 s sweeps (at shorter sweeps the
centres' grain sometimes tips it onto the curve) and takes the curve where it helps (swinging at ½ s, 1.8 → 1.0; bobbing at ¼ s, 0.9 → 0.4). What it cannot tell from three centres is a path
bent within reach from a thing turning faster than it reads: going round always looks bent, so the curve is chosen,
and it fails: with 1 s sweeps 18–57 steps off, with ½ s sweeps 4.3–20.3 against the chord's 3.5–15.3 (item 225). There only reading more often serves.

### 8.5 A reader that only receives

Everything above ran on a game loop, and the loop carries the view from nowhere inside it: one shared now (every system
stepped together), a snapshot every frame (the whole world read at one instant), pulling rather than receiving (the
picture goes to where the world is stored), the frame swallowing serial (every read finished inside it), and the engine
holding everything. *Wander* (items 270–271) was rebuilt without those. It is a world of the rung editor's solids, each a
radius r(θ, h) about its own axis, gathered in systems under gravity (stars, planets, moons, belts, pairs of stars, and
bodies passing between), with no edge in any direction. The loop, step by step:

1. **The system.** It keeps its own magnitudes and its own time; each system steps at its own rate (18–35 steps a
   second, none shared) and at each step sends a signal, tagged with its own time.
2. **Propagation, the world's side.** Signals travel at a finite speed (20 units a second). Where two arrive along one
   address, the one behind is blocked. What is handed on for each point is two unit pairs, across (h forward, v across)
   and up (h level, v up), each on h² + v² = 1, and a payload naming the system.
3. **Parallel, the reader's range.** Set in advance: the front half, laid evenly in turn, one address per pixel: the
   reader's fisheye under the standard placeholder γ = 1.
4. **Serial, arrival.** v over h, with the facing each pair carries, is the address, and the pair is then discarded. The
   range keeps at that address only an address back to the system: which system, and where on it (its own θ and h).
5. **The system, read through the range.** Each entry is followed to its system and the system is read there. The
   magnitude is met, not swept (R116). Choosing a thing by touch reads the range at that address and follows it back.
6. **g, from arrivals, fed back** (situation 3's g, R141). How many of the reader's addresses a system's points spread over, against the system's
   own count of addresses, is its g; g then sets how many of the system's addresses are laid (gN of the N, R94).
7. **The reader acts.** Its motion changes what arrives next; its own signal goes out, and bodies that heed it act on the
   reader that has reached them.

**Corollary 8.5 (placement by pairs alone).** *A reader that receives, for each point, the two unit pairs and nothing
else places every point exactly as a reader that knows where the point is.*
*Proof.* A unit pair with its facing is one representative of its address (Prop. 2.1(a)): a direction, not a
normalization of breadths; atan2 of the pair is that
address. The address of a point depends only on the direction from the reader to the point, and the unit pairs are that
direction, with its length divided out. ∎

The corollary is only about placing. Which of two arrivals on one address is kept needs order along the address, and in
*Wander* that was decided on the world's side, before arrival, by distance; it was the one place a magnitude decided
anything, and it was not the reader's. It is now (item 292): each body's own shape decides its own front and back, and
which body is in front is decided by the reader's own distance to each, reached through the address with names (the
distance at which the body's own shape spans what its arrivals span, 99.8–99.9% of overlapping pairs ordered right) or
by parallax without (67–74%). g needs no distance either: a large star at 37 units and a small planet at 11 both
read 0.12, and a pebble at 89 read 0.0002 (item 271), so g rises with size and falls with distance without the reader
knowing either.

*Measured (item 271).* Every body arrived after its distance ÷ 20, to within one of its system's steps; what was seen of
a body lay a mean 4.7 units (up to 11) from where it then was; a body removed at 11.1 units left the view after 0.65 s
against 0.56 expected. A point chosen by touch reads out its two pairs live: h and v as magnitudes, v/h, and the facing
in words (as Tom ruled on 25 September: direction is read from the facing, never from a negative v or h).

*Seams.* Blocking uses distance on the world's side; addresses between two laid points are filled by joining them, so
they point to places on the system that sent no pair of their own (g keeps the joins about one address wide); the
simulation must still hold every position to propagate signals; and the screen still refreshes, though it shows only
what has arrived.

*What a history of pairs gives* is §8.6.

**Nearness and g.** §13 asked whether nearness and g are one thing (they are not; renamed on 1 October). Here they come apart cleanly: nearness
is had from a history of pairs under the reader's own travel, as a unit-free ratio, and g is how much of a body's own
addresses the reader tells apart. Both fall with distance; neither is the other. Only the distance in the reader's own
lengths brings a unit in, and it is the reader's own.

**Proposition 8.6 (an address back to the magnitude; R119).** *Let a still point X be read from two standpoints P₀ and
P₁ = P₀ + b, where b is the reader's own travel between the readings, which it has as its own (its direction against its
facing, its length in its own unit). Let α₀ and α₁ be the turns from the direction of travel to the directions X arrives
along, each read from X's unit pairs. Then*

  d₀ / |b| = sin α₁ / sin(α₁ − α₀),

*where d₀ is X's distance from P₀, and X is fixed uniquely unless α₁ = α₀.*
*Proof.* P₀, P₁ and X make a triangle with angle π − α₁ at P₁ and α₁ − α₀, the parallax, at X. By the sines,
d₀ / sin(π − α₁) = |b| / sin(α₁ − α₀). Two lines that are not parallel meet in one point; they are parallel only if
α₁ = α₀, when X lies on the line of travel or is without end far. ∎

The right side is addresses alone. The left is a relation, v the world's magnitude and h the reader's own travel: the
magnitude the division took out (§2.3; met, not swept, R116) comes back, not held, but addressed, through a v/h whose h
is the reader's own step (Tom, 30 Sep, 09:11: "it also gives you an address back to the original magnitudes. can we prove that with our framework? seems to me, that is what 3d is."). Like any reading from somewhere it has a home and a horizon: as the
parallax closes, d₀/|b| runs to ∞, and turned it is the forward lay of §4.3, d = D1 · tan ψ, with D1 = |b|; the corner of
Prop. 4.2, "the reader's own unit", is the distance equal to the reader's own step. What it cannot give is the unit's
reach past the reader (Prop. 7.1, R89: ratios of two depths are unit-free, the depth itself is in the reader's own
lengths); a point on the line of travel, the counterpart of h = 0; or a point that moves between the readings, unless
the reader steps both ways (below). The triangulation is classical (§12). What the framework adds is its place: **3d is
the reader's addresses of direction plus this one relation** (R119; "the sphere of addresses" as said, the reader's
fisheye, R129). Two readings across and up give direction, and one standpoint has all of it at once; depth is not a third turn of that sphere but the relation of what an
address points to, to the reader's own travel. It needs a second standpoint, so motion: serial supplies it, as parallel
counts and serial reads motion as ratios (25 Sep). The distances in the reader's own lengths, the nearness ratios and
the order of what overlaps (items 275–278) are this proposition applied to arrivals.

**Which is in front.** Not from whose arrivals continue unbroken, which is the world's own blocking read back, but from
parallax: under the reader's own travel the nearer body's direction turns faster; a step one way and then the other
cancels a body's own motion. Of 545 overlapping pairs decided, 508 were ordered right, all of them where one was half
again as far as the other (item 278). The demo still blocks on the world's side; §13 keeps the handover open.

### 8.8 The shape in the round: what the address points to, and what arrives

"can we tap an object and show the recovered shape, with just an address?" (Tom, 30 Sep, 10:32), then, of the two panels
side by side: "this is the best result yet." (11:15). There are two routes to a body's shape, and *Wander* shows them
together (item 285).

**Following the address.** The range holds, at each address, only an address back to a system (§8.5). Following one
address gives the system, and the system holds the whole shape: every side, including those the reader faces away
from, at every scale (a shape has no back of its own, R139). One
address is enough.

**Rebuilding from arrivals.** The range also names, at each address, which point of the body's own surface arrived
there: the body's own address for that point, (θ, h). So every point arrives named, and nothing has to be matched from
one arrival to the next. For each named point the reader keeps the directions it has arrived along, and the direction
of the body's middle at the same moments.

**Proposition 8.8 (shape from named points).** *Let a rigid body that does not turn be read at moments k = 1 … K from
standpoints P_k, which need not be known. Let a named point X of it arrive along the unit direction u_k and its middle M
along w_k, and let m_k be the middle's distance at moment k. Then R = X − M satisfies*

  (I − u_k u_kᵀ)(R + m_k w_k) = 0  *for every k,*

*and R is fixed by these once two of the u_k are not parallel. If the m_k are known only up to a common factor, R is
fixed up to the same factor.*

*Proof.* X = P_k + s_k u_k and M = P_k + m_k w_k, so R = s_k u_k − m_k w_k: the standpoint cancels. Taking the part
square to u_k removes the unknown s_k and leaves the equation shown, which puts R on the line −m_k w_k + t u_k. Two such
lines that are not parallel meet in at most one point, and R lies on both. Replacing (R, m_k) by (λR, λm_k) leaves every
equation true, so a common factor in the m_k is a common factor in R and in nothing else. ∎

Three things follow. **The reader's own place drops out, and so does the body's own travel**: the shape needs neither.
**The middle's distances need not be measured**, only their ratios. Since the points must agree on where the middle was,
*Wander* solves the m_k together with the points, which leaves exactly the one factor of the proposition: the shape up
to a scale. **The scale is the one relation of R119**: the middle's distance in the reader's own lengths, from its own
travel (Prop. 8.6).

**A body that turns.** If the body turns about its own axis at rate ω, then R at moment k is R turned by ω t_k about
the axis, and each view can be turned back to one moment before solving. The range gives the axis. The body's own h runs
along it, so how h grows across the addresses in one view fixes a plane containing the axis; several views fix the axis
as the direction square to all those planes' normals. The rate is the one at which the named points agree. It follows
that the views the proposition needs can come from the body's own turning as well as from the reader's travel. Tested
(item 288), with the reader kept at a fixed offset from a spinning body so that only the turning changed the view: given
the axis, the shape came up to a scale from the turning alone (a cylinder 1.9%, an emerald 0.7%, a cube 13.6%; rates
2.38, 2.86, 3.07 against 2.37, 2.86, 3.07). The still reader's gap is the axis itself: from one standpoint every view's
plane is the same, so how h runs across the addresses fixes the axis only to that plane, not in how far it leans toward
the reader. A search over the lean found it for the cube but not reliably; that is open.

| body | rebuilt against the system's own shape, up to a scale | turning found |
|---|---|---|
| gem, still | 1.6–2.5% of its size | axis to 1.000 |
| twisted piece, still | 2.0% | |
| bishop, turning slowly | 2.3–3.0% | 0.805–0.85 against 0.80 |
| emerald, spinning | 2.0% | 2.86 against 2.86 |
| cubes, small and far | 9–13% | |
| cube, small, spinning fast | 9–18% | 3.04–3.21 against 3.03–3.07 |

What failed on the way:
- **Carrying the middle's distance by g.** An outline's g changes as the body turns, so the gem sat at 9.5% and the
  scale drifted.
- **Ignoring the turning.** The bishop sat at 50% until its turning was found.
- **Points cut by the outline.** Only points seen whole in a view are used, since a point cut by the outline centres
  wrongly; this helped little on its own.

Limits:
- A body whose axis itself tumbles is not handled.
- Only the sides looked at are rebuilt.
- While going round a moving body the reader shares its motion, so a distance from its own travel reads long there
  (14.5 against 7.1), and the scale is then withheld.

**What the two panels show.** Side by side, they are the two ways a view from somewhere holds a thing. By address, it
holds the whole thing at once, as the system has it, every side: the parallel reader's hold, by pointer, with no
reading of the thing itself. By arrival, it holds what has reached it, named point by named point, up to a scale, and
only from the sides it has faced: the serial reader's hold. What lies behind the reader, which no single standpoint has (§5.3), is on the left
panel from the first tap. On the right it is filled in only by going round, by the body turning, or not at all. The
scale on the right is the reader's own, and it is the only thing that needs the reader's own travel.

**Without names.** The rebuild needs to know which addresses are A's. With names off the range still says, at each
address, whether something arrived and which point of that thing's own surface it is; only not which thing. So A's
addresses are the ones reached from A's middle through neighbours whose surface names run on; where they jump, another
body begins (item 293). Of the addresses so found, 87–100% were A's, and the shapes came out as with names (still bodies
about 2–3%, spinning ones 10–13%, their rates found). What names alone give is the left panel: the whole shape, every side,
at once.

## 9. Relays and records

*In the split: a relay is an address whose payload is another address; a record is payload kept.*

### 9.1 One more level of indirection

Address → address → payload: an address that meets a panel either **goes on** (a mirror: a live relay, holding nothing of
its own) or **stops** (a painting of exactly what the mirror showed from the painter's standpoint: a stored relay).
From the painter's standpoint the two are identical, address for address (item 209):

A live relay passes the forward sweep through: nearness is the whole path, and near order survives. A stored relay passes only

A many-view record's standpoints are readers. Lines from two of them through where a ball shows in their stored views

### 9.4 Which of two things is in front

A reader holding nearness never asks: along an address the higher nearness is in front. A record of colours alone must. Two cues it
holds: **shape** (the border between two stored patches lies on the front thing's outline, so it fits the circle fitted to
that thing's outline against background and not the other's; one view suffices) and **place** (the front thing comes out
nearer when both are placed by relations; two views or more). Over 2,864 borders in 150 random worlds of 3–6 balls, both
cues are right on 97% of borders between balls standing apart; where one ball passes through another the border is where
they meet and neither is in front, and both cues fall to 76–78% (item 226).

### 10.3 What is left

Taking every entry's residual together: the unit is not a residual but an agreement, outside the geometry (R89). Three
remain:

1. **change faster than the reading comes round**, ωT ≳ 1 by Corollary 8.3: curves turning within a few readings (§8.3), what arrives faster than
   the turn (§5.3), and, as §10.5 shows, change of shape;
2. **what no standpoint read**: behind a moving thing at the moment it stood there; a record's holes; the 0.7% of the
   hollow no company round about reaches;
3. **the grain**: every figure here is to the grain of its addresses.

The first two have a name: they are **the backside** (R90), read since R139 as what lies behind a situated reader or
is blocked: what the view from nowhere holds, without having a back, and no standpoint does. A
reader reaches it only in part: by turning, later, or through a company, from elsewhere.

### 10.4 The conjecture

> **Serial and parallel readers, in company, recover everything of the world's geometry that some standpoint reads at some
> moment, up to a scale and to the grain. The unit is not geometric and comes only from agreement. What lies beyond is
> what lies behind a situated reader or is blocked, which the view from nowhere holds without having a back.**

Residuals 1 and 2 are, by their statement, what no standpoint reads in time, so they do not count against it; residual 3
is the readers' own grain. **What would kill it:** a quantity some standpoint reads that no combination of serial,
parallel and company recovers; a weakness nothing covers beyond the three residuals; or a cover that opens a new weakness
nothing covers. So far every cover has cost only grain, time or moves: Trémaux paid in moves, the sway in motion, the back
in warning.

**Lopsided deformation** (item 244). A thing whose across length breathes (±40%) while one end is held, so that its

across and the middle between them. Carried to now, the outline itself is fine: every end and the middle within 0.1–0.5

The two ends carry separately and correctly, and between them hold the whole of what the outline says: one end still and
one moving, or both moving at different speeds. What they do not say is which point is the thing's place. A thing that
held its left end and grew right, and one that held its right and moved left while growing, draw the same two ends. Which
point is "where the thing is" is not in the outline; it comes with what the thing is, its payload (assumption 2 of §11).
The geometry gives the ends; the thing's place, when it moves by growing, needs identity. No new residual: this is residual 1
met from a new side.

## 11. What kind of claim this is

"we won't be able to prove it definietvelyu, because this is synethetic and digital, but hopefully we can at least show
that is logical and likely to be true, at least within our own assumptions" (Tom, 09:53). Two standards, and no more:

**Logical.** The balance follows from the definitions before any test. A magnitude at a moment lacks exactly time and
motion; a ratio over time lacks exactly scale and the instant; what neither holds from one standpoint is the backside.
Proposition 7.1 puts the unit outside every reading; Proposition 8.1 gives the serial reader the still world whole;
Proposition 8.2 bounds what its clock can carry.

**Likely, within our assumptions.** Every entry was measured, and the probing tests looked for a residual the argument
did not predict and found none. The assumptions, each a place the conjecture could fail outside the simulation:

1. **One world, one update.** The loop supplies a shared *now*; the readers never used it, the scoring did. §8.5 lifts
   it for one demo: no shared now, every system on its own time, and nothing reaching the reader but signals.
2. **Identity is given.** An address's payload says which thing it read; readers never had to match a thing across
   readings, sweeps or standpoints. The lopsided test (§10.5) shows one thing this assumption carries: which point is a
   growing thing's place. In §8.5 identity travels with the signal (each system is a distinct signal), which moves the
   assumption from the reader to propagation without removing it. Tested with the name taken away (item 289): each
   arrival unnamed, A followed by where its arrivals have been heading and how large it spans. Going round, 8 planets
   and 6 belt bodies, it stayed on A all the time; travelling sideways so that everything else swept across A, with other
   unnamed arrivals inside its window in hundreds of frames a run, on A 100% (planets) and 96% (belt bodies; the rest
   said lost), and never on another body. Then with names off altogether (item 290): the reader keeps its own tracks of
   every arrival, unnamed and labelled by itself, a body wholly behind another does not arrive, and A, B and B's family
   are tracks. Every body at once, a track moved to another body 0.15–1.17 times in 10,000 track-frames; three stars
   weighed by a planet's track at 83, 36–37 and 125–127 against 84, 39 and 123, their families as tracks agreeing,
   predictions and the signals' speed as with names. So the assumption is not needed for any reading but one: the shape,
   which must know which addresses are A's, and whose left panel is the name itself (§8.8). What is lost without names
   is continuity across long absences: a body unseen for longer than its track lives comes back under a new label.
3. **Only the grain.** Readings are rounded to the addresses and otherwise exact.
4. **Straight lines and opaque things.** Mirrors ideal; nothing bends, scatters or lets light through.
5. **Simple things.** Spheres, ellipsoids, cylinders, the rung editor's pieces; motion straight, round, swinging,
   bobbing.
6. **Background is known.** Outlines are taken against floor and sky, or against anything farther for readers with nearness.
7. **The reader knows itself.** Its order, its clock, its D1, and, for records, where its standpoints stood.
8. **Small samples.** From a single run or a handful of worlds up to a few hundred per condition.

Assumptions 2 and 6 matter most: each hands the reader something that is, outside the simulation, itself a reading.

**What each result needs.** Switching one thing off at a time (item 291):

| switched off | what goes | what stays |
|---|---|---|
| the reader's own travel | distance, and with it size in the reader's own lengths, weight, the circle, prediction, the signals' speed | loop times, loop sizes as turns (Kepler's ratios), the family by shared motion (outsiders along one line get in without the weight test), the shape up to a scale from a body's own turning, given its axis |
| the ticks each arrival carries | arrivals against ticks, the signals' speed; prediction in arrival time misses by 1.2 lengths instead of 0.13–0.35 | everything else |
| the surface names (θ, h) | the shape in the round, a body's axis | size (from the outline's widest turn), everything else |
| the names (which system) | the whole shape the address points to; exact blocking (99.8% to 67–74%, since the body's own size is reached through the address); smooth going round | everything else, from the reader's own tracks, with continuity across long absences lost; the shape in the round from surface names running on (item 293) |
| the range's addresses | everything | nothing |

The reader's own travel is the one input most results hang on, as Prop. 8.6 and R119 have it: it is the one relation
that makes 3d. The names hang on least.

**Known results are consistency, not validation.** "so it shows that our framework assumpitions can derive known
results, which validates our assumptions?" (Tom, 30 Sep, 10:27). Partly. §8.7 shows the assumptions *suffice*: a reader
with only pairs arriving, a range it sets itself, its own travel and no shared now recovers size, weight, prediction,
membership and the signals' speed, and each step could have failed there (the signals' speed would have been out of
reach had it needed a shared clock; it did not). Every miss on the way was mended by a method within the rules, never by
loosening one. But *Wander*'s world was built with Newton's gravity and a fixed signal speed, and the reader recovered what
was put in; a classical observer recovers the same. Agreement with known results does not tell the framework from the
classical account. What would go further: *necessity* (dropping an assumption makes a result unreachable, not only
keeping them makes it reachable); a result the view from somewhere gives that the view from nowhere does not contain,
shown with shapes and then, if it holds, in a planned test; and the demo's remaining world-side steps moved to the reader
(§13).

## 12. What is classical

The mathematics is classical, and much of it has a classical name. The invariance of the cross ratio under projection,
and placing a point in a frame of four coplanar points, are projective geometry. The hierarchy projective–affine–
similarity–rigid is Klein's (1872). Reconstruction up to a projective map from two uncalibrated views, and up to a scale
from calibrated ones, is the geometry of multiple views (Longuet-Higgins 1981; Hartley and Zisserman 2004). The gnomonic
image of uniform measure on a hemisphere is the multivariate Cauchy, and the Cauchy's invariance under Möbius maps is
known (Knight 1976; McCullagh 1996). Even lays of a sphere without poles are standard (the Fibonacci lattice, González
2010; HEALPix, Górski et al. 2005). Kepler found Earth's orbit from Mars's returns by the triangles the reader of item 201 uses (1609), and the
transits of Venus (1761, 1769) gave the scale. Kepler's third law (1619) was found from ratios of sizes and periods, with no
unit; Newton weighed Jupiter and Saturn against the Sun by their moons (1687); Rømer had light's speed from Io's eclipses
coming early and late as Jupiter's distance changed (1676); separating G from M took Cavendish's weighing (1798). Tilted
loops seen only in direction are the visual binary's problem, and bodies grouped by shared motion are moving groups. §8.7
retraces these. The wall-follower, Pledge (Abelson and diSessa 1980) and Trémaux (Lucas
1882) are classical maze algorithms; a far landmark for heading and a closed loop for place are what mapping while moving
calls a compass and loop closure (Durrant-Whyte and Bailey 2006). A serial record is a rolling shutter. The metre has been
an agreed speed of light and an agreed second since 1983.

Separating the geometry of access from what is accessed has classical forms too: a fibre bundle's base space against its
fibre; the plenoptic function, intensity as a function of direction from a point (Adelson and Bergen 1991); Gibson's
ambient optic array, the structured array of directions at a point of observation, prior to any object (Gibson 1979). The
corpus's own name for it is *Reader Geometry as Addressing*.

What is ours is, first, **the four situations** (§2.4): what a reader knows of the breadths, not the world, sets where it
stands. The circle (both known and equal) and all other shapes (both known, varying) are views from nowhere, with no home,
horizon, corner, division or front and back; only the fisheye (h's breadth known, v's existing but unknown) is a view from
somewhere, and only it divides; the fourth, neither known, is impossible. Within that: **observation from somewhere as
not dimensional**, consisting of relations, addresses, g and magnitudes, each reached through the one before and walked as
divide, sweep, point (§2.3), where the view from nowhere has x, y and z at right angles; **g placed in each situation**
(none in the circle, the ratio of the circle to the shape in other shapes, a count toward an unseen end in the fisheye);
**the classical Cauchy as situation 3 with its far end assumed** (§2.1), with its scale γ read (by Claude) as the placeholder for
v's breadth; **situation 3 explained by the other three** (a shape read through the circle's assumption with the impossible's
unknown on one side); and **the corner as a measuring point** (R142, measured on ellipses, item 328): the middle of a lay only when the breadths are
equal, and otherwise the distance from it to the middle of the arrivals is the breadth ratio. Then the route and the
accounting: an address space laid from somewhere (the Cauchy family, with a corner), nearness in the reader's own unit,
and a measured account of which shape-facts come back from which source. Every step here is a situated reader, its
addresses and its payloads; the view from nowhere is used to make readings and to score, and is otherwise defined by what
the readers lack. Told that way, the division is visible: what a reading gives, what a carry gives, what a relay passes,
what only an agreement supplies, and what lies behind a reader or is blocked. Whether this route is better or
worse than the classical ones is an engineering question and not our concern; what the geometry holds and requires is.

The scope is a geometry of access: what a reader, as defined in §3, can hold of a world through its addresses. It is not
offered as a theory of perception or of mind, and "a coherent system of observation" means only the balance of §10.

## 13. Limits and open questions

- Every result is from simulated worlds, and is held to the standard of §11. A claim about real observers needs a planned
  test.
- **Nearness, renamed** (1 October). Earlier drafts, and *The View from Somewhere in 3d*, called nearness along an
  address g (g = D1/d). Once R140–R141 fixed what g is (the ratio of the circle to the shape in situation 2, the count
  toward an unseen end in situation 3), nearness is plainly a different quantity: a magnitude turned against the
  reader's own unit, D1/d = cot ψ on the forward lay (R87). It is renamed throughout; §8.6 already found it apart from
  g. The 3d paper still uses the old letter.
- **Blocking without distance.** In §8.5 the only decision made with a magnitude is which of two arrivals on one
  address is kept, and it is made on the world's side. Measured (item 278): from pairs alone, under its own travel, a
  reader orders overlapping bodies by parallax; stepping one way and then the other cancels a body's own motion. Of 545
  decided pairs, 508 were right: all 283 where one was half again as far as the other, 106 of 111 between 1.2 and 1.5
  times, 119 of 151 below that; every error involved a body with motion of its own. It needs travel, and near-equal
  depths remain uncertain, so the demo still blocks on the world's side; whether the reader should take it over is open.
- **Histories of pairs.** §8.6 gives motion along the address, unit-free nearness, distance in the reader's own
  lengths, approach, a body's own path, periods and Kepler's third law, each measured; §8.7 adds size, weight,
  prediction, the signals' speed, membership and the shape in the round (up to a scale, §8.8). Open: how a still
  reader finds a turning body's axis from one standpoint (given the axis, the turning alone gives the shape, item 288).
  Still unbuilt: records made from
  corner and horizon crossings alone.
- **World-side steps left in *Wander*.** Blocking is now by the reader's own distance to each body (through the
  address with names, 99.8–99.9% of overlapping pairs right; by parallax without, 67–74%), and going round uses only
  what arrives (item 292). What remains world-side is that the range is drawn from the arrived states' positions, and
  the scoring.
- **Necessity.** §8.7 shows the assumptions suffice for the classical results; which of them each result needs is
  unproved. Prop. 8.6 is a start: without the reader's own travel no history of pairs gives a distance.
- **Payloads that are not surfaces.** For a value at a point (a temperature, a stored number) rather than a surface met
  along a line, the addressing results hold (§2); first border, contact and the ladder are untested.
- **Identity, stripped.** Readers that must match things by outline and place alone, with no payload naming them.
  Every body followed with no names, all readings but the shape reached from the reader's own tracks (§11, items
  289–290). Open: continuity across long absences, which without names becomes a new label.
- **Background, cluttered.** Readers that must decide what an outline is taken against.
- **Where a growing thing is.** A lopsided change carries correctly at both ends, but which point is the thing's place
  is not in the outline (§10, item 244). It needs identity, the same open question as *identity, stripped* above.
- **A chain of agreements.** If A agrees a unit with B and B with C, but A never reads C, does the unit pass? A question
  about communication, not geometry (R89).
- **Borders between two things** were decided in records by shape and place (§9.4); a record that must decide it while
  things pass through each other has no answer to give.
- **A reading of the four situations offered by Claude, not yet ruled.** That the placeholder for v's breadth is the
  Cauchy's scale γ, with γ = b reading a situation-2 shape as an even turn (R138, R142). (That the N-unknown count of R96
  is situation 3's g, and that the classical Cauchy is situation 3 with its far end assumed, were taken up on Tom's
  go-ahead of 1 October, 06:49.)
- **How fast situation 3 closes on situation 2.** The breadth ratio b is read from the middle of the arrivals to about
  1/√n (R142, item 328), for an even-share lay; for other lays (even in length, even in direction) the corner says
  nothing about b. Which lay a real reader's arrivals follow is open.
- **Situation 2 drawn.** No demo yet shows a shape with breadths that differ by direction, the one place g works as a mix
  (R140); the breadth sweep is situation 1.
- The older corpus questions this work did not touch remain in *Reader Geometry as Addressing* §14.
