The payload doesn't matter

One reader stands at home. Its directions are given, uniformly by turn over a quarter turn, before anything arrives; where each ray points is its address, fixed by the standpoint alone. Change the world behind it to anything, any size, or nothing at all, and the addresses do not move. Only what each ray finds there, the payload, changes.

The reader at home (bottom left), h to the right, v up. Its rays at equal angles; the corner, v = h, dashed at 45°. The world on the reader's lay of distances (the writer's picture; no distance arrives): in proportion out to one H (the gold ring), then each tenfold the same width (rings at 10, 10³, 10⁶, 10⁹, 10¹²). A dot where a ray meets something, in its colour; a ray that meets nothing runs to the horizon.

For contrast, equal-metres addresses: the same number spaced evenly up a line one H away, out to the world's size, a writer's layout. It needs that size. Its rays move with every S, and with an unknown or empty world it has none.

Top row: the addresses, one tick per ray (v/h on a log scale), the same in every world. Below: the payload, each ray's colour, and how far away it met something (higher is farther, in tenfolds). This is all that changes from world to world.

The address is the turn's; whatever arrives can be swapped at every address and no address moves (Observation from Somewhere, Theorem 6(a), §7.2). So nothing the addresses say is about the payload, and the world's size never enters them: equal angles are free, equal metres cost N. The same in 3D. All demos.
