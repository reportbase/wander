One turn, one read

One direction from home, given before anything arrives, meets one point, and that one read breaks down two ways. Counted as a direction from where you stand, it is the direction with its heat, the inscribed sweep; counted from the centre of the circle on the chord, it is the point where the paint lands, the central sweep. Both end at the same point: the direction meets its paint there. The heat is given; the paint is met. Over the whole quarter turn the two sweeps, as a writer measures them, take the line as π/2 and 2/π (numbers with π in them, the writer's); their product is the 1 that only the writer holds. In complex form the point is the direction squared, ½e^(2iθ) from the centre: where the direction's reading runs off to ∞ at the horizon, the radius has made its half turn to home, e^(iπ) = −1.

The chord from home A to B, and the circle drawn on it. Gold: the direction, A's ray at θ. Teal: where the paint lands, the radius from the centre at 2θ. Violet: the arc from B already turned through, over its chord.

Over the quarter turn, as a writer measures it: the central sweep's arc over chord (teal, rising to π/2), the inscribed sweep's chord over arc (gold, falling to 2/π), and their product (white), 1 throughout.

The same read on the Cayley disc, w = (s − i)/(s + i) = −e^(2iθ) for the reading s = tan θ: the standpoint i goes to the centre, zero to −1, the corner to −i, the horizon to +1. The Thales circle's landmarks, turned a half turn.

The Cauchy's constant, from the same turn. Left: the landing point w = (1 + is)/(1 − is) = e^(2iθ), built from the reading s by arithmetic alone; the logarithm, followed along the arc from +1, gathers 2iθ. Right: the kernel 1/(1 + s²), the arc's turn per unit of reading, its area from home to s equal to θ. At the horizon w reaches e^(iπ) = −1, the logarithm has gathered iπ, and the area is π/2: the half-Cauchy's 2/π is one over it. The constant comes from the three together: the endpoint, what the logarithm gathered, the change from angle to reading. That each finite read stays rational and π enters only with the total is our reading, not a theorem.

One pole, the constant and the phasor. Left: a standpoint z = m + iγ above the line, equal turns from it landing on the line, and the Cauchy kernel they make, (1/π)·γ/((s − m)² + γ²), its one pole at z. Right: weight that kernel by a turn e^(its) and total it: the result is e^(izt) = e^(imt − γt) (gold), a spiral in from 1, the foot turning it and the height damping it; the ring is the same total taken directly, the phasor averaged over 20,000 equal turns from z. At t = 0 both are 1, the normalized kernel's total; unnormalized, at z = i, it is the π of the panel above. For t < 0 the mirror pole gives e^(imt − γ|t|), the other half (the slider shows t ≥ 0). One residue, at the pole, gives both. Classical (the Poisson kernel and its Fourier transform); that the pole is where the standpoint is drawn is our reading.

What needs π, and what does not. The product of the two sweeps is 1 with no π in it: the two are reciprocals, the reader's s × (1/s) = 1. The landing point reaches −1 with no e or π: the corner is i, and two corners are i × i = −1. The heat 1/(1 + s²) is rational, and the corner halves it; π enters only when the area is closed to a total. And the circle's own equation, h² + v² = 1, is 1/(1 + s²) + s²/(1 + s²) = 1 at every direction. π/2, 2/π and e^(iπ) are the completed circle's, the paper's: a writer at work has fractions near them, and lands exactly only at the corner.

One relation, held two ways. The fourth panel's w = (1 + is)/(1 − is) gives the reader's form of Euler's identity: w(s) × w(1/s) = −1 at every slope, a direction and its reciprocal, and at the corner i × i = −1. The writer's e^(iπ) = −1 is the one member of that family that pairs home with the horizon, which no read holds, and so the one that carries π. The algebra joining the two forms is ordinary and they are equivalent; which form a read holds is a reading. The rule holds for every pair at once and nothing in it happened: a geometric law needs no memory, and only the turns actually taken need keeping (Reads and Writes §1, "The same relation, held two ways", "A law needs no memory").

In physics. e^(izt) is a spectral line: a phase turning and fading has the Lorentzian as its spectrum, the line's width and the lifetime one height read two ways; for sodium's D2 line, measured each way independently, Γτ = 1.0010 ± 0.0025. (Reads and Writes in Physics, §4.3.)
