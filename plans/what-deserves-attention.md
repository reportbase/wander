# What deserves more attention

*A review, 6 October 2026, by Claude, asked by Tom "what are the interesting ideas that deserve more attention from
this project". Read from SPN's central hypothesis, abstract and §11–§14, and the open list in* v-and-h.md *§9; not from
the whole corpus. A reading, not a ruling: nothing here changes a paper or a lab. Ranked by how much each would settle,
most first; each ends with a next step.*

---

## 1. The test for convention, and "convention or absent"

> A thing is a convention only if changing it leaves every reader's reading of every arrival unchanged. (§11.4)

The sharpest idea in the project, and the one that travels furthest beyond *Wander*. It is what lets the wider
conjecture fail, and it has already done work: it sorted the frame and the now as *measured* under *Wander*'s medium
(AET's readings move by 0.333 with the reader's speed) and as *conventions* under no preferred rest (NOR, SIM, CMV, HUB).
No other result separates the two settings as cleanly.

The parked follow-up (§14, *Convention or absent*) is better still. PRS suggests a third case: a value no record moves
with, which passes the test, yet which in a world that draws on demand cannot be assigned consistently at all (Bell).
If that holds, what is not recoverable is either **convention** (can be chosen freely) or **absent** (cannot be
assigned), and telling them apart needs a second test.

**Next step.** Write the test up on its own, set against the existing literature. SIM's result lands in the debate over
the conventionality of simultaneity (Reichenbach 1928; Grünbaum), where Malament (1977) argued that simultaneity is not
conventional once causal structure is fixed. Engaging with Malament would either sharpen the test or show a gap in it.

## 2. Showing a result is needed, not only enough

§12 says it plainly: "Agreement with known results does not tell the framework from the classical account", and "a
conjecture that never meets a world-side kill has not yet been pushed where it could fail". More than a dozen labs are
exact by construction (§12 lists AET, NOR, SIM, ESC's first run, CMV, HUB, CMP's still case, MOM, BAL's reading run,
ELV's and EQV's runs 3–4, RNG, DOP's relativistic match). Every kill so far was in the reading, the prediction or the
set-up; none came from the world.

The nearest thing to necessity is the table of inputs switched off one at a time (§12, item 291): **the reader's own
travel** is the input most results hang on. §14 names it "a candidate for a paper of its own; not begun".

**Next step.** Begin that paper. And when choosing new labs, prefer ones where the world could kill the prediction over
ones that hold by construction.

## 3. The 2 of the central hypothesis: the deciding run is designed, not run

The central hypothesis rests on the recursion (R180), and the paper names the rival a test should aim at: candidate (c),
an h fixed for the reader wherever it stands. It also names the run that could decide it (central hypothesis, *Standing
after the shape and signal runs*; §14, *From the shapes*): readers that take their own h from their parent's record,
reading signals built on ratios of 2, 3 and the golden ratio. If the parent-to-child ratio of h settles at 2 whatever the
signal, the 2 is the reader's (R183); if it follows the signal, the 2 is what is read.

It is listed as open in both papers. Nesting alone cannot single out 2 (an address scheme on tripling nests as well), so
this is the run that looks where the paper says to look: at where the corner falls.

**Next step.** Write its prediction and kill first, then run it. Cheap, and either answer settles something.

## 4. Number lines in people: an independent fact that already exists

The hypothesis needs "an independent fact, not built to fit, that only a nesting reader explains", and finding one is
called the first job. The paper names an unchecked candidate: people place numbers on a line in proportion for familiar
numbers and compressed beyond them, with the switch point moving as the familiar range grows.

This is well studied, with data published: Siegler and Opfer (2003) on children's number lines, where placement moves
from logarithmic to linear with age and the change comes earlier for smaller ranges; Dehaene, Izard, Spelke and Pica
(2008) on Mundurucu adults, who place numbers logarithmically. If the switch point tracks the edge of each person's
familiar range as a corner at one h would, that is evidence the project did not build.

**Next step.** Take the published curves and, with a prediction written first, ask where the switch from proportion to
compression falls against the familiar range.

## 5. The inversion at the corner needs a null model

The paper's main contribution: near and far as one geometry, inverted where v = h (§3.2). The disc correspondence,
s²/(1 + s²), half its maximum at the corner, is striking.

The weak point is already recorded (§14, *Changes of form at the edges*): FRM stopped undecided because no chance
baseline separated the corner from the arithmetic, and CRY's fold at s = ½ was noticed after the fact and counts for
nothing. Until there is a baseline, the strong form of the claim is hard to tell from "formulas change where their own
numbers cross 1".

**Next step.** Fix the null model before choosing cases: for example, draw formulas at random from a stated family and
count how often their change of form falls near s = 1.

## 6. Who can tell identical things apart (TRK), and the Gibbs paradox

TRK found that a reader keeps identical balls apart only while one frame's step is shorter than the balls' nearest
approach, and that this decides whether mixing raises the spread for that reader (§9.9, item 7). That is a measured
form of an old argument: Jaynes ("The Gibbs Paradox", 1992) held that the entropy of mixing depends on what the
experimenter can tell apart. TRK makes "can tell apart" a reader's own capacity.

**Next step.** A short note setting TRK beside Jaynes, ideally with a run that sweeps the frame rate across the
threshold.

## 7. The floating-point picture of the reader's scale (R176)

Plain proportion up to the corner, octaves past it: like a float's subnormals then normals, except that the hand-over
is at the corner, where the facing turns, not at the bottom of the range (§14). It is the clearest way into the geometry
for a newcomer, and it ties to item 4: the number-line data is a human instance of the same layout.

**Next step.** A page like `labs.html` that shows it: the reader's step against its reading, with a float's beside it.

## Lower priority for now

- **The quantum labs** (PRS, ONE, TWO) have the world's draw built in, and by §14's own account show "little more" than
  that the framework does not break. Already paused (Tom, 3 October), rightly.
- **ELV's next order**: the explanation for its kill is in hand (the prediction's cut at C⁻³), and it does not bear on
  the conjecture.
