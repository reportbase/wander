# OFS: the reader holds only H

27 Oct 2026. Tom: "this is a small but very important change to the logical chain. The reader has no concept of V, let
alone c. It only holds H. V is only known if you hold both hemispheres. So the reader creates circles without V, only
H." After a pasted passage arguing that "V = H is still a choice" is a writer's objection. An outline for Tom to approve
before OFS is touched, as with `plans/ofs-spine.md` (R530).

## The change in one sentence

The reader's circle is made from H alone: one bar of length H, turning; V, and with it c (V = cH), exist only for a
writer, who holds both hemispheres, and "V = H" is the writer's name for the circle the reader makes, not something the
reader draws, sets or chooses.

## What changes in the chain, and what does not

No proposition's mathematics changes. What changes is who holds which number, and so the order of the chain.

| Link | Now | After |
|---|---|---|
| (P0) | A relation is two unit relations, each to an *assigned* breadth, h to H, v to V | A relation is two unit relations, h and v in [0, 1]; for the reader both are shares of its own bar, whose one length is H. No breadth is assigned to the other party |
| (P1) | The reader chooses one breadth, H | Unchanged |
| 3(a) | The reader must assign V = cH; no reading depends on c | The reader has one length, H; there is no second length to assign and so no c. A writer, holding the other's breadth, can write the reader's drawing as V = cH; no reading depends on c |
| 3(b)–(d) | With V = H, … | The same, said of the bar of length H |
| 3(e), (f) | The attitudes are turns of one bar only if V = H (V = V₂ = H) | A writer's statement: drawn on a page with all of v at c times all of h, the sweep is a turn only at c = 1. So the reader's circle, written by a writer, is V = H, and an instrument that stretches one axis (c imposed, not chosen) is not a turn |
| 3(g) | The sweep draws V = H in its own measure | A uniform sweep that returns has one length for every attitude, its own; that length is H. Unchanged in substance; now the reader's own derivation of its circle, needing no V |
| 5(d) | "Let the reader draw all of v at c times all of h" | "Let all of v be drawn at c times all of h", by a writer's page or an instrument; the Cauchy at the corner only at c = 1 |
| Working back from the Cauchy | Step 3: "only if both breadths are drawn at one length, V = H" | Only if all of h and all of v are one length: for the reader automatic (one bar), for a page or an instrument the condition c = 1 |
| 5(f), (g) | The lighthouse and Mercator require V = H | Unchanged: both are written drawings (a shore, a chart), so c is theirs, and c = 1 is what makes them the lamp's turn and true bearings |

## The order of the chain (27 Oct, a pasted passage, agreed)

Hold H; turn it uniformly; the orbit is a circle because the one length is fixed; the uniform turn's readings
s = tan θ are the Cauchy, median at the corner, quartiles a reciprocal pair, the tail reaching the horizon. The Cauchy
is then not a law laid on a circle that already held a V: it is the measure of the addresses the one-length turn
makes. A writer can later add a second axis and see that it coincides with H.

Two precisions for the text:
- Where tan θ is a length: on the tangent to the circle at all of h, one H from home, the bar's line meets it at height
  H·tan θ, so s is a ratio of two lengths both in H (the flat screen one H away, the lighthouse's shore at β = H). On the
  Thales circle it is the inscribed ray at θ, the radius at 2θ (§4). In neither does a second length enter.
- "Uniform" stays what §2 says: the turn measured by itself (R327); the Cauchy is the uniform turn's, and other weights
  keep the corner and the missing mean but not its shape (Theorem 6(e)).

So §5 and §7.1 read in this order: (P1) one H; the turn (3(g), uniform and returning, its one length H); the circle
(3(c)); the readings and their law (5(a)–(c)); then, for drawings with two axes, c and 3(e), 5(d), (f), (g).

## Sections to rewrite

1. **Abstract.** "For there to be a relation the reader must give the other party a breadth, and its only measure is its
   own: it draws the mountain at the breadth of its thumb, V = H" becomes: the reader holds one length, H, and its
   sweep is one bar of that length turning, a circle; V and c are a writer's, who writes the circle as V = H. The
   lighthouse and Mercator stay as written drawings that need c = 1.
2. **§1, the hand and the thumb.** "You set the mountain equal to your thumb, V = H" becomes: you hold only your thumb;
   the mountain arrives as directions on the thumb's circle; "mountain equals thumb" is what a writer, looking at your
   drawing, would say it amounts to, since the drawing has no other length. The image is kept (R205), reattributed.
   Results bullet "A reader that holds one breadth must assign the other party a breadth … V = H" rewritten likewise.
3. **§5.** (P0) and Proposition 3 as in the table. "What V = H means" becomes the writer's name for the reader's
   circle. "Two choices, and a number that is not one" becomes "One choice, and two numbers that are not the reader's":
   H free (the zoom); c a writer's or an instrument's, never the reader's; V*/H not a choice. "Neither choice can be
   declined": H cannot; the sentence "without a breadth for the other party there is no relation" goes. "Can a reader
   decline V = H?" becomes "Where c ≠ 1 comes from" (the question is a writer's; for a reader that turns there is no c
   to decline): the four signatures stay (writing, an uncalibrated instrument, the estimate taken for the drawing, no
   turn), each now an instrument or a writer imposing c.
4. **§3.6, §3.8, Appendix C.** V's notation entry: a writer's breadth for the other party, never held by the reader;
   V* the true breadth, a writer's amount; κ = V*/H.
5. **The wedge (3(f), "More parties", §2's truth as the sum).** The reader's sphere has radius H; V = V₂ = H is the
   writer's name for it; the truth off the centre by V*/H and V₂*/H stays the writer's statement.
6. **§9: designating and sizing, Proposition 13.** Designating is turning to the other party, free; there is no
   drawing it at V = H to do. Sizing it is writing. Proposition 13's estimate (the median of what is known; H with
   nothing known) stays, scoped as the writer's question asked on the reader's behalf: an estimate of a size is an
   amount.
7. **§10 step 5, "Mountain equals thumb".** As §1.
8. **§12.2 question 6.** Now simpler: whether readers *draw* V = H dissolves, since a reader has no V to draw. What stays
   empirical is whether a reader's sweep is a turn, and whether its instrument imposes a c. "Should, not does" (R262)
   moves to those.
9. **Every other "drawing V = H" (about 60 places)**, each read in place: where it says the reader draws, sets or
   chooses V = H, it becomes the reader's circle (of radius H) or the writer's description of it; where it is about a
   page, a chart, an instrument or standardizing (Appendix A "V = H under other names"), it stays, since there c is real.
10. **CS companion** carried out to match (R421, 15 occurrences). **SPN** a ruling. **CLAUDE.md**'s "keep the case"
    and "settled view" bullets: V is the writer's; the reader holds H alone.

## What stays

- Every proposition's mathematics, every number, Theorem 6, the main result.
- c as a real parameter wherever something written or built has two axes: a page, a chart, a shore, a camera's pixels,
  standardized variables. Appendix A's "V = H under other names" is exactly these, so it stands.
- "Should, not does" for what remains open.
