# Five ways to observe

*A note, 6 October 2026, by Claude, at Tom's request ("write a note describing the 5 ways to observe"). It gathers
in one place what SPN §2.1 now holds across several paragraphs ("The situations restated, on the sweep alone" and
what follows it), in the order of Tom's list. It rules nothing: where it says more than SPN, it says so. The 3d
repository draws the five as a slideshow, one frame each: https://reportbase.github.io/3d/situations.html.*

> "0 - no equation or information is known. 1 - syemtrical equation is known (circle). 2- asymetrical equation is
> known (shapes). 3. parallel sitution you can count one hemispher (outside). 4. serial situation. you can project one
> point a time (inside). both 3 and 4 can be known approximately" (Tom, 6 October)

## The five, in one table

| | what is known | how it is read | standpoint | example (Tom's) | SPN's older numbering |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **0** | nothing: no equation, no information | — | — | — | situation 4, impossible |
| **1** | a symmetric equation: the circle | all at once | nowhere | an equation | situation 1 |
| **2** | an asymmetric equation: every other shape | all at once | nowhere | an array | situation 2 |
| **3** | one hemisphere, counted together | parallel | outside | an apple held in the hand | situation 3, its parallel reader |
| **4** | one point at a time, projected | serial | inside | a molecule of an apple | situation 3, its serial reader |

3 and 4 can each be known approximately, with one number known only within bounds; that was SPN's situation 5. It is
a variant of 3 and 4, not a sixth way, and the 3d page leaves it out.

The first cut is whether v is known. With v and h both known, it is 1 or 2, depending on whether they are equal. With
v not known, it is 3 or 4, depending on where the reader stands and how it reads. With neither known, it is 0.

## 0. Nothing

No equation and no information: nothing to count in and no home to lay from. There is no reader here in SPN's sense,
which needs a known side to take its ratio from. It is not a claim about what else might be learned with nothing
known. On the page it is an even grey: not darkness, which would be something seen, but nothing at all.

## 1. A symmetric equation: the circle

Both breadths known and equal, so both can be called 1. The whole is held at once, as an equation is: h² + v² = 1, or
for a solid x² + y² + z² = 1. There is no standpoint. Nothing faces the reader and no part is nearer than another, because there is no
reader in the picture, only the mathematics.

- **The corner is calculated**, not found: v = h, at 45°, read off the equation.
- **The line is held.** A sweep from A to B is held as pure distance, 0 to 1, whole.
- **No horizon.** Nothing lies past an edge, because there is no edge of view.
- **Recursion stops.** A known shape can be read in nested levels, but there is nothing left to read past the first,
  because the circle has no detail.

## 2. An asymmetric equation: every other shape

Both breadths known, but they differ with direction, B(θ). Still held whole and at once, still from nowhere. No short
equation holds a real shape, so the natural form is an array: the shape's breadth in every direction, every entry known
together. The apple's radius at twelve heights and sixteen directions round is such an array, and it is the 3d page's
frame 2.

- **The corner is still calculated**, from the known breadths.
- **No horizon**, as in 1.
- **Recursion follows the shape.** Nested readings hold, level by level, what the levels above left out: an octave
  entered only where some detail remains, and stopping where none does (SPN §2.1, revised 6 October; NST, STR).

The difference from 1 is only that no single unit serves every direction. Each direction's breadth is a share of
itself.

## 3. One hemisphere, counted together: parallel, outside

Now there is a reader, standing outside a thing and facing it. Tom's example: an apple held in the hand. The reader
counts the whole facing side at once, and the other side is turned away, not unknown in kind. What it lacks is v as a
breadth of its own: it has v only as a count of h's.

- **A sweep is one hemisphere.** Measured from the point facing the reader, the angle runs from 0 there to π/2 at the
  edge (the limb). Every relation v/h fits in that one quarter turn, so "every relation" means every relation that can
  be seen from one side.
- **π/2 is every relation, counted at once.** Each relation s = v/h takes a share of the turn, ds/(1 + s²), and they
  sum to π/2. On the octave axis, x = ln(v/h), the shares make a bell centred on the corner, ½·sech x. This bell
  is not the normal curve: it keeps half as much in each octave further out, without end.
- **Approaching the line from above.** Arc over projection, θ/sin θ, rises from 1 at the facing point to π/2 at the
  limb. The parallel reader's measure is always above 1, the line.

## 4. One point at a time, projected: serial, inside

The reader is inside the thing, one part among others. Tom's example: a molecule of an apple. It takes the others one
at a time, nearest first, each projected as a point in its own sky. It never has them all.

- **The serial reader is the fisheye.** Its view is a disk whose radius is the turn, straight ahead at the centre and
  the horizon at the rim, π/2. The corner, v = h, is the ring halfway out. Inside that ring the view is proportional.
  Outside it the octave rings crowd toward the rim, each half as wide as the last. The fisheye is the running total of
  the parallel reader's bell (the Gudermannian): the same function, one held at once, the other point by point.
- **2/π is a sweep, projected.** The arc projected onto the line, sin θ/θ, falls from 1 to 2/π at the limb: the mean of
  sin θ over the quarter turn (Buffon's needle).
- **Approaching the line from below.** The serial reader's measure is always below 1.

## What 3 and 4 share, and 1 and 2 do not

- **A horizon.** Parallel approaches the line from above (θ/sin θ → π/2), serial from below (sin θ/θ → 2/π). The two
  are reciprocal at every turn, so 1 is their exact middle on the logarithmic scale. Neither reaches it while there is
  any turn. That is the horizon: **only 1 and 2 hold the line; 3 and 4 approach it.**
- **The corner is approached, never found.** In 1 and 2, v = h is read off the equation. A situated reader measures by
  turning, so its v is never the line's v, and the true corner stays out of reach.
- **Forced recursion.** Halve the sweep and each half lies four times closer to the line, and again, without end. This
  is Archimedes' squeeze on π: a polygon inside and one outside, sides doubled at each step. The octaves are those
  halvings, and each octave is a quarter turn. Drawn round a centre they make a logarithmic spiral. Unwrapped they are
  one continuous sweep. In 1 and 2 recursion follows a shape's detail and stops. In 3 and 4 the horizon forces it, and it
  never stops.
