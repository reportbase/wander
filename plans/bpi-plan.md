# BPI: Buckingham's π theorem and the reader's addresses

27 Oct 2026. Tom: "are you familiar with Buckingham pi theory", then "yes" to a plan stating the correspondence exactly.

## The claim

Buckingham (1914; Vaschy 1892; Rayleigh; Bridgman 1922): a law among n quantities whose units are built from k
independent base units (the dimension matrix D, k × n, of rank r) is a law among n − r dimensionless groups, the
products ∏ x_j^{a_j} with D·a = 0. The reason is the group of unit changes, x_j ↦ x_j·∏ λ_i^{D_ij}, acting on the
amounts; the π's are its invariants.

Read in OFS's terms:

1. **One dimension (k = r = 1).** Every quantity a length, D = (1, …, 1): the group is x ↦ λx, and its invariants are
   exactly the functions of the direction [x₁ : … : x_n], a point of the positive orthant's projective space, OFS's
   wedge. The π theorem in one dimension is the step from amounts to addresses, and Proposition 8 ("scale cannot be
   read") is that theorem applied to a reader's readings.
2. **The rank is the count of amounts writing needs.** The π's are readings, free. Recovering every dimensional quantity
   from them needs r amounts carried in (independent in D), no fewer: with r − 1 a family of worlds remains, all with the
   same π's. In OFS's geometry r = 1: one amount (§9; RWT's nullity).
3. **The π theorem says nothing about the law on the π's.** The unit-change group acts trivially on the invariants, so
   it constrains no measure on them: every law on the directions is equally scale-invariant. The uniform turn's law (the
   Cauchy, the median at the corner) needs the turns and the exchange (§2, Jaynes), not Buckingham.
4. **More dimensions.** With k ≥ 2 the invariants of the positive orthant are the π's, n − r of them in log coordinates
   a linear quotient; the quotient is a product of the single-dimension wedges (one direction space per base unit) exactly
   when every quantity's dimension is a power of one base unit (each column of D has one nonzero entry). Otherwise the π's
   mix base units and there is no such product.

Known beforehand: the π theorem and its proof by the null space of D (Buckingham 1914; Bridgman 1922; Barenblatt 1996,
*Scaling, Self-Similarity, and Intermediate Asymptotics*, ch. 1); that ratios are the invariants of a common rescaling;
OFS Proposition 8; RWT's count of steps by the Jacobian's nullity (`plans/rwt-plan.md`). What is ours: reading the k = 1
case as the reader's addresses and r as the count of amounts writing needs. draw's labs were not checked (the draw
repository is not in this session); none is about dimensional analysis as far as CLAUDE.md lists them.

## Predictions (written before any script)

- **B1 (one dimension: the π's are the directions).** For n lengths, n = 2 … 6, the exponent vectors a with Σa_j = 0 form
  a lattice of rank n − 1 generated by the ratio vectors e_j − e_1; a monomial is scale-invariant iff it is a function of
  [x₁ : … : x_n] (checked symbolically: f(λx) = f(x) for the generators, and the map x ↦ (x₂/x₁, …, x_n/x₁) is constant
  on every ray and separates distinct rays). Kill: any rank or check otherwise.
- **B2 (the rank is the amounts to carry in).** In every example, the map from the log amounts (n of them) to (the log
  π's, the log of m carried-in quantities) has full rank n exactly when the m carried-in quantities have independent
  columns of D and m = r; with m = r − 1 its rank is n − 1, a one-parameter family of worlds left. Kill: any example
  otherwise.
- **B3 (no law from Buckingham).** For n = 2 lengths, two laws on the direction [x₁ : x₂] (the uniform turn, density
  2/(π(1 + s²)) on s = x₂/x₁, and a uniform law on θ² over [0, (π/2)²]) are both exactly invariant under x ↦ λx (their
  pushforwards through every λ are identical), and they differ (medians s = 1 and s = tan(π/(2√2)) ≈ 2.64). Kill: either
  not invariant, or equal.
- **B4 (more dimensions, when the quotient is a product of wedges).** In each example the number of π's equals
  n − rank D; the π lattice splits into groups each built from quantities of one base unit only exactly when each column
  of D has one nonzero entry (true for the constructed case, false for the pendulum, drag, range and conduction). Kill:
  either part otherwise.

- **B2 killed, by my set-up**, in its second clause. Carrying r independent quantities writes everything, and dependent

  clause assumed every choice of r − 1 independent; one dimensionless or dependent choice leaves more than one family.
  B2′ below.
- **B3 not killed.** The direction x₂/x₁ is unchanged by x ↦ λx, so every law on it is scale-invariant; the uniform turn
  (median 1) and the law uniform in θ² (median tan(π/(2√2)), checked exactly) differ. My prediction's decimal "≈ 2.64"
  was my arithmetic slip: tan(π/(2√2)) = 2.0180.
- **B4 not killed.** The number of π's is n − rank D in every example, and the π lattice splits into single-unit groups
  exactly in the constructed case, where each column of D has one nonzero entry (the pendulum, drag, range and conduction
  mix units).

## What BPI says

Buckingham's groups are the invariants of the unit changes. With one base unit those invariants are exactly the
directions [x₁ : … : x_n] of the positive orthant, the reader's wedge (B1): in one dimension the π theorem is the step
from amounts to addresses, and OFS Proposition 8 is that theorem read from a standpoint. The rank of the dimension
matrix is the count of amounts writing needs: r independent ones write every quantity, and r − 1 independent ones leave
one family of worlds (B2, B2′); OFS's geometry has r = 1. The theorem places no law on the groups: unit changes act
trivially on them, so every law on the directions is as scale-invariant as the uniform turn (B3); the Cauchy is the turns'
and the exchange's, not dimensional analysis's. With several base units the groups split into one wedge per unit only
when each quantity carries one base unit (B4). Classical throughout; the reading ours. Carried into OFS Appendix A,
"Buckingham's π theorem" (§9), beside Proposition 8's citation of Buckingham.
