## The spine in one sentence

A situated reader builds with ratios alone, and they are enough for everything it does from where it stands; a
blueprint, the writer's statement in amounts, adds capabilities the ratios cannot give, and they matter for intricate
systems whose parts must meet, but it is not required.

## Why the present thesis already carries it

The present thesis (the address and the payload kept apart; the addresses given, complete before anything arrives) is
the reason ratios suffice: every reading is a ratio (Proposition 8(a), Theorem 6(a)), so the reader's whole geometry is
built without an amount. The spine adds the second half the paper now leaves implicit: what exactly the amount buys.
Nothing proved changes. What changes is the order of emphasis and some wording.

## What a blueprint adds (the new section)

A new section, placed where §9 now opens ("What the geometry does not give: amounts"), titled for the capabilities
rather than the lack: **"What a blueprint adds."** Each capability is stated with the result that shows the ratios
cannot give it, so the section adds no new claim, only a gathering and a direction:

1. **The size.** V*/H, one number; no reading carries it (Prop 8; §3.8).
2. **Meeting out of view.** Two systems that never see each other can be made to fit only through an amount agreed
   beforehand (a unit; "What a unit is"); in view, a ratio read between them suffices (Prop 7). Interchangeable parts are
   this case.
3. **Sums of amounts.** Totals, means and budgets add amounts; the readings have medians and no mean (Theorem 6(b),
   (c)). A blueprint can be added up; the readings can be ordered.
4. **The join.** Both hemispheres, every facing, the back and the inside held at once; the reader holds one facing at a
   time and never the signs (§3.7's box, §7.3's arrays joined by the writer).
5. **Before the thing.** A blueprint can state what does not yet exist and be built to; a reader reads what arrives
   ("What reaches a standpoint", Prop 9). Putting a thing at a place is writing ("To move the furniture is to write").
6. **Completeness, stated.** A reader recovers exactly and still cannot certify that it has everything ("Recovered,
   not written"); a blueprint states the whole by construction.

Then one paragraph, the spine's second half: none of the six is needed for a reader to build, place, compare, order or
check; each becomes needed when parts made apart must meet, when amounts must be added, or when the whole must be stated
before it exists. That is a statement about what the mathematics makes possible, not about how any builder works.

## Classical support (Appendix A, one entry each)

- **Euclid, Book V (Eudoxus).** A geometry of magnitudes in ratio, with no unit length: the ratio regime carried a whole
  mathematics. (Check the text before citing.)
- **Descartes, *La Géométrie* (1637).** The unit segment chosen so that a product of lengths is a length: the amount
  introduced to do arithmetic on lengths, capability 3. Stated neutrally.
- **ISO 216.** A system stated by one constraint (a half keeps the shape), every breadth a ratio, one written amount (A0's
  1 m²) added to meet other systems; 66 sizes from three amounts and one rule (MOD, exact). Capability 2 in its simplest
  form.
