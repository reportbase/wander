# Essay 1: Reading and writing — an outline

28 Oct 2026. The first of the long essays (`plans/essays-plan.md`, SPN R562; Tom: "merge it and start essay 1
outline"). This is an outline for Tom to reorder and cut before any prose is written.

Its geometry is OFS's: §3.4, §3.8, §9 ("Ratios suffice" through "Recovered, not written") and Propositions 7, 8 and
13. Its history is `plans/rwf-literature.md`; quotations marked [checked] were read in the source.

## The one thing the essay says

From where you stand, everything you read is a ratio, and it comes free. To state an amount — how many metres — you
must carry one amount in from outside, and you can count how many: one for yourself, and one more for each other reader
who must agree with you. Reading and writing are two acts; one test tells them apart.

## What it does not claim

- That the invariance is new. Measurement theory has it (Stevens 1946; Krantz, Luce, Suppes and Tversky; Narens), and
  Poincaré's thought experiment is a century old. The essay credits them, early and plainly.
- What it adds is the standpoint, which none of them ties to it, and the count of amounts carried in, per reader.
- No physics is claimed new. Every case is classical; what is offered is the one test across them.

## Shape

About 8,000–10,000 words in nine sections, each opening on a scene and ending on what it showed. The case of h, v, H
and V is kept throughout, and there are no symbols before §4.

### 1. What a reader can do with no unit at all
- **Scene:** a reader with no ruler maps a room. Two rooms, one stride between two standpoints: every corner is placed,
  every thing sized against every other, all in strides (`room-ratios.html`).
- **Point:** none of this needed a unit. Everything read was a ratio, and the map is complete up to one number. Name the
  two acts: reading gets all of this for free; writing (saying how many metres) costs that one number, carried in.
- Tom, 28 Oct: Poincaré's universe that doubles overnight is "rather mundane". The invariance is not the point; what a
  reader can build without a unit, and what writing costs, is. Poincaré gets one line of credit in §4.
- **Demo:** `room-ratios.html`, steps 1–3.

### 2. What a reader reads
- **Scene:** a thumb held up against a mountain range (OFS §10).
- **Point:** from one standpoint, everything arrives as a direction (v against h): an angle, a ratio, where one thing
  sits against another. None of it carries a unit, and none of it changes when the world is scaled (OFS Proposition 8:
  scale cannot be read).
- "Mountain equals thumb": with nothing carried in, the reader's best estimate of the size is its own length
  (Proposition 13). This is not a mistake; it is the best guess from where it stands.
- **Demo:** `writer-readers.html` step 1, ghost boxes on the same rays; `pinhole.html`.

### 3. What a writer writes
- **Scene:** a blueprint. Every length is in one unit, front and back drawn, from no standpoint (OFS §3.4, "the writer";
  `blueprint.html`).
- **Point:** writing states amounts, and an amount needs something carried in:
  - a counted stride;
  - a known size;
  - a base walked;
  - a clock and the signal's speed (ranging is writing);
  - a standard agreed beforehand.
- This is where Martens is quoted [checked]: "A unit is thus merely a relation—to a standard reference body … in
  disguise."
- **Point:** the writer's number line has no corner (OFS "the writer's ruler"); the reader's lay does. That is
  difference, not criticism (R478).
- **Demo:** `blueprint.html` and `blueprint-3d.html`, three vantages; `writer-box.html`.

### 4. The test: scale the world
- **Point:** multiply every length by k, the observer's included.
  - A quantity that does not change is a reading.
  - A quantity that moves by k is a writing.
  - Nothing else can happen to a length-like quantity.
- Worked through at k = 1/10, 1 and 100 on the mountain range (OFS "Two tests").
- **History:**
  - Poincaré 1914, pp. 94–95, the universe a thousand times larger [checked], one line;
  - Bridgman 1922, p. 20, "the requirement of the absolute significance of relative magnitude" [checked];
  - Stevens's ratio scale and permissible statistics;
  - meaningfulness as invariance (Narens [checked from his papers]).
- Say plainly: this test is theirs. What is new is asking who can apply it, and from where.

### 5. How many amounts: a count
- **Point:** the cost of writing can be counted.
  - One reader writes nothing new from one standpoint.
  - One known step writes everything in the reader's own H.
  - One exchanged number lets a second reader agree.
  - One agreed notch writes in a public unit.
- RWT's nullity, computed on the page: 4, 1, 0, 0, 1, 0, 1, 0 (`write.html`).
- **History:**
  - Buckingham's n − r;
  - the gauge freedom of bundle adjustment: seven numbers, one of them the scale (Triggs et al. 2000);
  - Duff, Okun and Veneziano's argument over how many units physics needs.
- Each counts the amounts for a theory or a reconstruction. The essay counts them per reader.
- **Demo:** `write.html`.

### 6. Many readings, one amount: the survey
- **Scene:** Snellius 1617; Roy's base on Hounslow Heath, 1784. Thousands of angles are read, one base is walked, and
  every side is written. A second base checks the first and adds no scale (OFS "A survey").
- Then the ground: one's own eye height as the base walked into every reading (Sedgwick's horizon ratio; OFS "The
  ground"), and Warren's stair climbing at R/L = 0.88 for short and tall alike [0.88 checked; "π number" to check].
- **Point:** in perception the carried-in amount is the body.
- **Demo:** `ground.html`, `room-ratios.html` (one stride counted), `room-walk.html`.

### 7. Units within units: the tower
- **Scene:** a building, two rooms, two things in each (OFS "A worked example of the tower").
  - Six readings fix seven lengths up to one amount: a₁/b₁ = 27/16 by two quotients.
  - One amount at the top writes all of them.
  - A misread link moves its room by one factor (9/10).
- The ISO thread and paper sizes (ISO 216: √2 and one written amount) show whole hierarchies of units held in ratios,
  with one amount at the top.
- **Point:** sophisticated systems can be built in ratios alone. Writing still costs one amount, however deep.
- **Demo:** `slide-rule.html`, chains and loops.

### 8. Closing a loop, and what a reader cannot know
- **Point:**
  - A tree of readings cannot find its own error; a second route can (a closure of 10/9).
  - Completeness is never a reading. A reader can recover a thing exactly and still only in part.
  - Recovery does not make the reader a writer: it got there by cleverness (standpoints, routes, the amount carried in)
    (OFS "Recovered, not written").
- **Second test:** change what arrives and keep the directions. What stays the same is the address; what changes is the
  payload. Use one example, LIGO's 8 s shift, to show the test is runnable (OFS "Two tests").
- **Demo:** `slide-rule.html` misread, `payload-free.html`.

### 9. Why the distinction is worth having
- Where taking a reading for a writing goes wrong:
  - the drawing taken for a measurement ("mountain equals thumb" read as metres);
  - Lutz–Kelker as the one shown case (`parallax.md`).
- What it gives:
  - a question to ask of any quantity (did it survive the night?);
  - a count of what must be brought in;
  - a reason a lone reader can do so much with no unit at all.
- **Close:** back in the room. The map in strides was finished long before anyone asked for metres; the metre is the
  one thing it had to be handed.
