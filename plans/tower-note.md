A working note, to iterate on. A reading, not a ruling. Its test is TWR (`plans/twr-plan.md`). Citations below are from

## 1. The picture

A situated reader holds two towers at once.

- **A tower in scale.** OFS's tower of holds: a building, its rooms, the things in them. Each link is a ratio read one
  level up (a thing in room units, a room in building units), not an amount. Any number of levels are held in ratios
  alone; writing the whole costs one amount, at the top.
- **A tower in time.** OFS's "Sweeping from zero": every stop is a new zero, readings compose by tangent addition, and a
  walk is a chain of legs, each read against the one before it (leg i over leg i − 1, from a thing seen at three stops).

Moving runs both. What it does to them is not the same:

- **The ground floor churns.** The addresses (directions from here, [h : v : v₂]) belong to this standpoint. Every step
  changes them all, and changes which things are held: some merge into home, some pass the held edge, some go behind a
  nearer thing, new ones come in. No act of the reader's; the geometry adds and removes them.
- **The upper floors stand.** The links (a size over a size, a distance over a distance) come out the same from any
  standpoint that sees what they relate. Once read they stay read, whether or not the reader ever stands there again.
- **Steps add floors.** Two standpoints turn address ratios into a place (parallax), so each step trades old addresses
  for a new link: a distance in legs. The first leg is the reader's H.
- **Loops check floors.** A misread link moves everything above it. A second route back to an old ratio shows the error
  (OFS's "Closing a loop"), and draw's cycle sheet (`cyc`) shows how: ratios on a graph with no common unit split into the
  part one ground explains and the obstruction round each cycle, from the readings alone.

## 2. Can a reader build with ratios only, and never write?

The claim to test: **yes, for everything it does alone.** A lone reader's whole map, its routes, its comparisons of size,
its way home and its time to contact are ratios in its own H (its first leg, its stride, its eye height). None of it
changes when the world is scaled, so none of it needs a unit. Writing is needed only where an amount comes from outside
the reader's own view:

- a quantity in another unit that no thing in view relates to (a speed in m/s, a clock's seconds, a stock of energy);
- another reader with whom it shares nothing in view (then the ratio of their H's is an agreement, a unit).

And one case that looks like writing and is not: **two readers that see one thing in common** align their maps by that
thing alone. The ratio of their H's is read off the shared link, with no amount passed (TGT already found the exact
form: closure between two readers' counted edges gives the ratio of their holds and nothing more). So "a unit between
readers is an agreement" holds only where they share no view.

If this holds, writing is not something a reader needs for its own geometry; it is how a reader meets the amounts of
others: other readers it cannot see, and instruments that state amounts.

## 2½. Each system is its own unit

27 Oct. Tom: "lets keep to the tower of ratios. for example, it seems to me that each system is self defined by its own
ratios. the system may have used physics as it constraints, why it used one breadth for another, or why it standardized
on a specific set of breaths. systems seem to be somewhat constrained, and consistent in their units, but they are their
units, based on its constraints."

The reading, stated so it can be checked:

- **A system's unit is one of its own breadths** (its module), and every other breadth in it is a ratio to that one.
  Nothing outside the system is needed to state the system.
- **The constraint chooses the ratios.** Why this breadth and not another, and why a standard set of breadths, is
  answered by what the system must do (physics, use, manufacture), not by any outside unit. The constraint is stated in
  the system's own terms, so it yields ratios.
- **Consistent within, different between.** One system's breadths agree with each other because they come from one
  module and one set of constraints; two systems' modules are unrelated until a link is read between them (a ratio, where
  both are in view) or an amount is written (where they are not).
- **The tower is systems nested.** A level of the tower is a system; its module is a breadth of the level above (or a
  constraint's answer there); the link between levels is the ratio of the two modules.
- **One amount at the top.** Tying a system to the outside (to metres) is one written number for the module; the system
  does not change when it is written.

Cases to work through (exact where the constraint is mathematical; checked against the standards before use):

1. **Paper, ISO 216.** The constraint: a sheet halved keeps its shape. So its sides' ratio r satisfies r = 2/r, r = √2,
   and the series is one doubling of area a step. The module is the sheet itself; the written amount is A0's area, 1 m².
   Halving swaps the long side and the short (the exchange), and √2 is the ratio fixed by it.
2. **Preferred numbers, Renard (ISO 3).** The constraint: equal steps in ratio across a range. R5 is 10^(1/5) a step
   (1, 1.6, 2.5, 4, 6.3), R10 10^(1/10): sizes laid evenly on the levels, the standard breadths a geometric series
   whatever the unit they are later written in.
3. **The classical orders, Vitruvius.** The module is the column's own diameter (or half of it); every other part of the
   order (height, entablature, spacing) is stated in modules. A temple is stated without a foot; the foot enters only to
   build it. (Check Book III–IV for each order's module.)
4. **Screw threads (ISO 68-1, ISO 261).** The profile in units of the pitch, the pitch against the diameter: already OFS's
   "Units within units". The constraint is strength and making.
5. **Music.** Intervals are ratios (2:1, 3:2, 4:3, or 2^(k/12)); a scale is stated with no frequency; concert pitch (A at
   440 Hz, ISO 16) is the one written amount, and older pitches show the system unchanged by it.
6. **Brick bonds.** A brick's length is about two widths and a joint, so a bond closes at corners and returns: the
   brick's breadths are each other's units, chosen by the bond.
7. **Gears.** The module (pitch diameter over the number of teeth) makes gears that mesh share one breadth; the tooth is
   stated in modules.
8. **Type.** The em, the size of the type itself; spacing and leading stated in ems.

Questions this raises:

- Is every standard set of breadths a constraint read as ratios plus one written amount? Find a counter-case.
- When two systems meet (a page in a book in a shelf; a screw in a gear in a machine), is the link between them always a
  ratio of modules that the joining constraint fixes?
- Which constraints give the levels (Renard), which the exchange (paper), which a small whole-number ratio (music, bricks)?

## 2¾. Building without a blueprint

27 Oct. A passage pasted by Tom (its author not named): "A blueprint is a dual-hemispherical object: every length held at
once, at true scale, with no standpoint, no order of arrival, and no horizon. Nothing in the construction of an ant nest,
a termite mound, a beaver dam, a bird's nest, or a spider's web begins from such an object. The builders start from local
ratios … and the finished structure is the set of ratios those local constraints have stabilized." It goes on: readers are
"not incomplete writers waiting for the missing amount. They are the ordinary case"; writing is needed "only when the
structure must meet a frame that already exists outside it"; and "the draft's language sometimes treats the writer's
geometry as the fuller or more complete one … The natural distribution of building runs the other way."

Checked against the corpus:

- **It agrees with §2.** A lone reader builds from ratios in its own unit, and writing enters only where an outside
  amount must be met (a surveyed plot, a clearance in metres, a mating part to a standard). The passage adds the reverse
  emphasis: that mode is not a fallback but the common one.
- **One slip.** Situation 1 is not the writer's alone: the circle V = H is the reader's drawing and the writer's equation,
  one circle held two ways (SPN R299). Situation 2 is the writer's.
- **The builders still have a unit, their own.** A body length, a stride, a leg span is the system's module (§2½), an H,
  not an outside amount. "Ratio-only" means ratios to the builder's own breadth, not ratios to nothing.
- **What OFS says now.** It names the writer's knowledge as the complete object (abstract, §1) and has a section "What the
  reader lacks is one number" (§3.8). The arithmetic there is right; the word "lacks" is measured from the writer's side.
  The passage's point would be met by one clause saying the number matters only where the reader must meet an amount
  from outside, and that most of what a reader does never meets one. Not made: OFS changes are Tom's to approve, and the
  claim about building is empirical (below), outside OFS's scope (geometry, not readers' adaptations, R455).
- **The biology is a claim to check, not a result.** The classical anchor is stigmergy (Grassé 1959, check): building
  steered by the state of the work, with no plan held. Cases to look up before anything rests on them: termite and ant
  building by local cues and templates (Bonabeau, Theraulaz and others, check), orb-web spacing set by the spider's own
  legs (check), beavers plugging where they hear running water (check), wasp combs and bird nests. Each would be a
  builder whose module is its own body and whose constraints are local; none is evidence for OFS's geometry.
- **The question it sharpens.** Which built things needed a blueprint, and why? Candidate answer from MOD: a structure
  needs writing exactly where two systems with independent modules must meet without either being in view of the other
  (thread to nut across factories, paper to printer across countries). Joined in view, a ratio read between them does
  (one shared link, §2). That is testable on standards: every standard amount should sit at such a meeting.

## 2⅞. Write once, then read again

27 Oct. A passage pasted by Tom (ChatGPT): the number line "is necessary for some operations and over-used for others";
needed "when the near-field extents are not shared and the systems must still exchange absolute amounts"; not needed "once
a system has its baseline and is working inside its own constraints", when "the subsequent construction, adjustment, and
checking can return to ratios"; human practice "often does not return to it", re-expressing local comparisons as metres,
"a failure to hand the system back to its ratios after the baseline has been set".

Checked against the corpus:

- **Kept: write once, then read.** One amount writes a whole tower ("A worked example of the tower"), and after it every
  relation inside is still a ratio; the amount fixes the scale and changes no ratio. So the writing step can be taken
  once, where an outside amount must be met, and the work after it can go on in ratios against the baseline it gave.
  That is already OFS's arithmetic; the passage names it as a practice.
- **Not kept: "over-used", "failure", "paid for continuously".** These are claims about how engineering is done and what it
  costs, with no measurement behind them (R364), and they speak against the number line (R478). OFS stays out of both.
- **One error.** "Loss of the direct proportional comparisons inside the corner": the number line is proportional on
  both sides of 1 (Proposition 14), so it loses no proportion inside the corner or anywhere; what it lacks is the corner
  itself, a reader's.
- **A question it raises, testable.** Do standards state their internal relations as ratios to a module (ISO 216's √2,
  ISO 68-1's profile in P) or as amounts (ISO 261's pitches by size)? MOD found both. Counting which a set of standards
  does, and whether the amounts sit exactly at meetings out of view, would test the passage's claim about practice
  without asserting it.

## 2⅞½. The number-line paper already had much of this

1. **Writing between readers is one factor.** "It puts back the scale division took out": x = r_A·H_A = r_B·H_B, so the
   trip through the ruler carries exactly the ratio of the two H's (§1, "What the ruler is for"), and "the ruler is a
   bridge, not a place". This makes §2's agreement concrete: an agreed unit carries what TGT's closure recovers when two
   readers share a view.
2. **Units began as one reader's body made public.** "The foot, the cubit and at last the metre were each one reader's
   body or object, made into a public notch." A module (§2½), a builder's own body (§2¾), promoted to the one written
   amount: how a module becomes the outside amount.
3. **The seventh capability, worked.** "Five metres is far field for an ant and near field for a mountain. The ruler
   carries the magnitude; near or far … each receiver decides again", and "the ordinary number line is the near-field
   ruler, extended across the corner so that every reader can share it" (§1). No agreement on a near field's extent is
   needed because each receiver places the amount against its own corner.
4. **Bounded against open lines** (§5, §6): a line with both ends given is a held whole, read by its midpoint; an open
   line is read against one's own unit. A possible signature of reading a blueprint against reading from where one
   stands; NLE run 1's kill on bounded lines fits it, and the open-ended test is unrun.
5. **Level rulers and scientific notation** (§1): "a plain ruler inside a level count", one number holding a module
   inside a level and a count of levels, a two-floor tower; it bears on §2⅞'s question of how standards state things.
6. **The ruler's grain caps what can be exchanged** ("Depth cannot cross finer than the notches", §1, to be restated as
   grain): a limit on what writing can carry, worth setting beside the seven capabilities.
7. **"n breadths leave n − 1 relations, and exactly one thing is lost: the scale"** (§8): the general form of "one amount
   at the top", the same count as Buckingham's rank (BPI).

Its terms predate 9 Oct and later rulings, so nothing comes over without restating: recursion in the levels (rejected,

## 3. Classical ideas to build on

Each with what it gives, what the tower adds, and its standing. "Check" marks a citation from memory.

1. **Eudoxus and Euclid, Book V: magnitudes in ratio, no unit.** Euclid's geometry of lengths, areas and volumes is
   built on ratios and proportions between magnitudes of one kind, with no unit length and no number attached to a
   segment; Book V (Eudoxus's theory) makes ratio rigorous without one. Classical geometry was, for two thousand years,
   built with ratios only. What the tower adds: the standpoint (Euclid's ratios belong to no one standing anywhere).
2. **Descartes, *La Géométrie* (1637): the unit segment.** To make the product of two lengths a length (not an area),
   Descartes chose one segment as unity; from then on lengths are numbers. The unit enters to do arithmetic on lengths
   as numbers, not to do geometry. A reading to examine: the unit is where geometry is turned into writing. (Kept
   neutral: OFS says nothing against the number line.)
3. **Thales and the pyramid's height, by its shadow.** The height read as the ratio of the shadows, against a staff:
   the oldest worked case of a tower link (the pyramid in staff units) with no unit at all. (Check: the story is
   Plutarch's and Diogenes Laërtius's; details vary.)
4. **Klein's Erlangen programme (1872): similarity geometry.** The geometry of the similarity group is exactly what is
   unchanged by scaling the world: OFS's test ("scale the world") is membership in it. Kendall's shape space (1984,
   check) is configurations modulo similarity, so a reader's map from ratios alone is a point of Kendall's space; draw's
   `owt` sorted which quantities survive similarity, affine and projective accounts.
5. **Bearing rigidity.** A framework of points is fixed up to translation and scale by the bearings between them when it
   is bearing-rigid (Eren et al. 2003; Zhao and Zelazo 2016, check). The tower's claim (a map from directions alone, one
   scale left) is this theorem; what is ours is the reading of that one scale as the one amount writing costs.
6. **Structure from motion and monocular SLAM: scale ambiguity and scale drift.** A single moving camera recovers the
   scene up to one scale; over a long walk the scale drifts as a random walk in its logarithm, and a loop closure corrects
   it as a similarity, not a rigid motion (Strasdat, Montiel and Davison 2010, check; Hartley and Zisserman 2003, the
   stratification projective, affine, metric). The tower in time is exactly this chain, and its drift is the chain's.
7. **Cycle consistency and synchronization; Hodge decomposition of ratios.** Pairwise ratios on a graph are consistent
   with one ground exactly when every cycle closes; least squares on log ratios projects onto the gradient part and the
   cycle part is the inconsistency (Jiang, Lim, Yao and Ye 2011, check; Singer 2011, check). Draw's `cyc` is this.
8. **Cognitive maps: topological before metric.** Tolman (1948) and Kuipers's spatial semantic hierarchy (2000, check):
   places and routes first, metric later and optional. A reading to examine: the topological and similarity levels are
   ratios; Kuipers's metric level is writing.
9. **Animals that navigate in their own units.** Bees' odometer reads optic flow, and bees flown down a narrow textured
   tunnel overstate distance (Srinivasan et al. 2000, check): a distance in the surroundings' units, a reading. Desert
   ants count strides, and ants on stilts overshoot (Wittlinger, Wehner and Wolf 2006, check): a distance in the ant's own
   H. Bees home by matching a snapshot of directions (Cartwright and Collett 1983, check): no distance at all. Each is a
   reader building with ratios only. (Perception is out of OFS's scope; this stays here.)

## 4. What seems not fully understood

- Which ratios a moving reader may **drop**. Candidate: any it can get back by closing a loop; a ratio is lost only if no
  route returns to it. (TWR T4.)
- How the **drift** of the tower in time is shared out by a loop: the Brownian bridge, error at the loop's middle half of
  the open chain's. (TWR T5.)
- Whether **two readers sharing a view** ever need writing between them. (TWR T6(f).)
- The two towers as **one structure**: the links in scale are the gradient part of a ratio graph whose nodes are things,
  the links in time the gradient part of one whose nodes are standpoints; a loop is a cycle of either. To be written out.
- Where exactly **writing** first becomes unavoidable, case by case: an outside amount, another reader out of view, or
  nothing else? (TWR T6(e).)
