An infinite star field, in 3D

The reader sweeps a whole octant, its addresses the same however many stars there are. The world holds N stars, eight times as many in each doubling of distance. With few, the far field is sparse; with more, it fills; with N infinite, every line of sight ends on a star. H, the reader's own breadth, is a zoom: it slides every star along its ray and moves no address. Point at a star for its index.

Home where the three axes meet: h, v (up) and v₂. Distances on the reader's lay: in proportion out to H (the gold sphere, the corner), then every doubling the same depth, so the infinite field fits. Drag to turn, scroll or pinch to zoom toward the cursor: zoomed far enough, every star is there to point at. A level too full to draw star by star is thinned to a random choice of its stars, filling in as you zoom.

Point at a star, in the scene or on the map below, for its index.

The reader's sky on Lambert's equal-area map: azimuth across (h left, v₂ right), height v up, one cell per address, the corners dashed. Each painted by the first star it meets, dark if none. The same cells at every N and every H.

Olbers in three dimensions: each doubling of distance holds eight times the stars, each a quarter of the solid angle, so each level covers twice the sky the last did. The addresses never needed N, and H never moved one: equal angles are free, equal metres cost N. The same in 2D, the payload doesn't matter in 3D, all demos.
