An infinite star field, in 3D

The writer sees the stars where they are: give the world more and its far field fills. The reader sees each star only as a direction, one of its own addresses over the octant, and holds the share of what it reads at each. At N, 2N or infinitely many stars, and at any H, that view is the same. N is invisible to the reader; H only slides the writer's stars along their rays.

The writer's view

Where the stars are, every distance an amount. Home where the axes meet: h, v (up) and v₂; in proportion out to H (the gold sphere, the corner), then every doubling the same depth, so the infinite field fits. Double N and the far field holds twice the stars; change H and every star slides along its own ray. Drag to turn, scroll or pinch to zoom toward the cursor; a level too full to draw is thinned to a random choice of its stars, filling in as you zoom.

The reader's view

at N

at 2N

Each star read as a direction only, counted at the reader's own addresses: Lambert's equal-area map of the octant, azimuth across (h left, v₂ right), height v up, one cell per address, the corners dashed. Each cell shaded by its share of what is read: an even share is one flat colour, at N and at 2N alike. No cell knows a distance, so there is no far field for the reader to fill. With fewer stars than cells the reader sees single stars; once N is well past them, N and 2N are the same map.

The writer needs N: to place a star it needs its distance, and the far field's count grows with N. The reader needs none of it: its addresses are fixed by its sweep, and its view is the share of what it reads at each, the same at N, 2N and ∞, and at every H. The same in 2D, the payload doesn't matter in 3D, all demos.
