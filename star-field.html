An infinite star field

A reader turns its head through a quarter turn, its addresses at equal angles, the same however many stars there are. The world holds N stars, evenly scattered, four times as many in each doubling of distance. With few, the far field is sparse and most addresses meet nothing; with more, it fills; with N infinite, every line of sight ends on a star and the far field is solid. Every star has an index: point at one to read it.

The field on the reader's lay: home at bottom left, h to the right, v up; distances in proportion out to H (the gold arc), then every doubling the same width (arcs at 10, 100, …), so the infinite field fits. The corner, v = h, dashed. Drag to pan, wheel or pinch to zoom in anywhere: zoomed far enough, every star is there to point at. Shaded where they are too many to draw one by one.

Point at a star, on the map or the strip below, for its index.

The sky from home: one column per address, at equal angles from h (left) to v (right), the corner dashed. Top: each address painted by the first star it meets, dark if it meets none. Below: how far away, by level of distance. The addresses are the same at every N; only what they meet changes.

Olbers in two dimensions: each doubling of distance holds four times the stars, each half as wide in the view, so it covers twice as much of the sky, and a sky reaching far enough is covered. The reader's addresses never needed N: equal angles are free, equal metres cost N (the payload doesn't matter, the dark sky). All demos.
