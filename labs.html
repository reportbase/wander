the lab

Experiments that ask one question again and again: can someone inside the world, holding only what has reached them, work out what physics says the world is doing? Each lab answers it for one known result. You can run any of them here, in your browser, and read what each one is for.

What Wander is

Wander is a world of stars, planets and travellers moving under gravity, and a reader flying among them. The reader holds nothing of the world except what has reached it. Every system ticks on its own clock and, at each tick, sends out a signal carrying where its bodies are and the time of that tick (its tag). Signals travel at 20 lengths a second, so the reader always sees each body where it was when its latest signal left, never where it is now.

From those arrivals, its own path and its own clock, the reader works out what it can: distances, sizes, masses, the signals' own speed. Physics textbooks state their laws from nowhere, with the whole world laid out at once. Wander's reader is always somewhere.

What a lab is

Each lab takes one result that physics established from nowhere (Kepler's laws, the Doppler shift, Rømer's measurement of the speed of light, the dark night sky, and so on) and asks whether the reader, from somewhere, recovers it. The lab builds a small world out of Wander's own pieces, runs it without drawing anything, and lays the reader's reading beside the known law in a table. They are correspondences, not claims: a lab shows that a reading lines up with a law in Wander's world, not that our universe works this way.

The labs keep to a few rules, which is what makes their verdicts worth anything:

  1. A lab's prediction and its kill condition (how far off would count as failure) are written into its note before it is run.
  2. A run that is killed stays recorded as killed, with its numbers. A fix is a new run with its own prediction written first; an old prediction is never edited to fit.
  3. When a run is killed, the note says whose fault it was: the world's, the reader's reading, or the lab's own set-up.

Reading a result

Each run ends with a verdict:

not killed
The reader's reading landed where the prediction said it would, inside the kill line.
killed
The reading missed by more than the kill line allows. This is a recorded result, not a crash: an open question written up in the lab's note.
error
The lab threw an error or gave no verdict. That means something in the code is broken.

In the result tables, a teal number is within half the kill line, gold is within it, and red is beyond it. Each note says exactly what its colours mean.

What it all adds up to

Behind the labs is a conjecture: all known physics is recoverable from the view from somewhere. Everything measurable can be had from what a reader holds; what cannot is convention, a choice such as the unit of length, or which mass counts as the standard. To stop "convention" becoming an excuse, there is a test: a thing is a convention only if changing it leaves every reader's reading of every arrival unchanged. Each lab's line "What it adds" says where it leaves the conjecture: recovered, recovered up to a convention, open, or against.

Words the notes use

the reader
The one inside the world, flying, who holds only arrivals, its own path and its own clock.
arrival
A signal reaching the reader. The reader takes, for each body, the latest signal that has had time to cover the distance.
tag
The time stamped on a signal when it left.
own lengths
Distances in the reader's own unit, which it has to earn from its travel and the signals' speed.
softened gravity
Wander's pull is eased at very short range so bodies never fling apart; from far off it reads as a mass spread out a little.
medium / none
Two settings of "the rest switch". medium: signals travel through a world at rest, with one shared clock (Wander's own). none: no preferred rest, as in special relativity.
run 1, run 2…
Each attempt at a lab, recorded in its note in order, with its own prediction and outcome.

The labs

Each lab runs in a hidden copy of Wander (the same as opening index.html?lab=CODE). Most take a second or less; TMP and TRK take about a minute each, and the page stops responding while a lab runs. Running every lab takes three or four minutes.
