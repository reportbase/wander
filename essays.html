What can be known from where one stands

Fifteen essays, read in order. Each opens on something familiar, shows the geometry in full, says which older work saw which piece, and ends with what it changes. The proofs are in Observation from Somewhere, which stays the source of truth. Each title opens its essay; the links under it are the pages that show it. Some essays also have a scrolling explainer.

  • Reading and writing. A reading is a ratio from a standpoint, free, and unchanged when the world is scaled. A writing states amounts, and costs an amount carried in.
  • The Cauchy. The law of a reader's directions, the circle traced by one length turned: its median the corner, its far end the horizon.
  • Two tests. Scale the world: is it a reading or a writing? Change what arrives: is it an address or a payload?

essay 13 · opens on Hamilton on the canal

Why the plane is special

In the plane one law plays four parts: the Möbius image of the uniform turn, harmonic measure, the powers of the standpoint and the two sweeps' rates. In space they part (Liouville), turns stop commuting, and a statement must say which law it means.

essay 14 · opens on a roof's pitch

Rates, not angles

Writers have angles; readers have sweeps. A rate is of something per something: payload per address. A hand swept from horizontal to vertical goes at π/2 or 2/π, never the writer's 1; the corner is where v fills as fast as h empties; the Cauchy is a speed with no payload in it. Tangent and arctangent cross to a share of the whole turn, and are the writer's tools.

essay 15 · opens on Bernoulli's bank

What only a writer holds: π, e and the completed infinity

The transcendental numbers are what a finished infinity gives. The reader makes every rational step toward them and holds none: π completes the turn (the turns close, with a period), e completes the scale (the rescalings never close, and have a base). They meet at e^(iπ) = −1, where Lindemann proved π transcendental. A machine, holding only fractions, is a reader here.
