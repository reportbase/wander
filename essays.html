What can be known from where one stands

Sixteen essays, read in order. Each opens on something familiar, shows the geometry in full, says which older work saw which piece, and ends with what it changes. They are drawn from Reads and Writes, the source of truth; the earlier Observation from Somewhere, archived, holds the proofs for reference. The known physics they touch is sorted in Reads and Writes in Physics, and fourteen of them close with a paragraph on it. Each title opens its essay; the links under it are the pages that show it. Every essay also has a scrolling explainer.

  • Reading and writing. A reading is a ratio from a standpoint, free, and unchanged when the world is scaled. A writing states amounts, and costs an amount carried in.
  • The Cauchy. The heat of a read's directions, hottest along the look and cooling toward the horizon, the circle traced by one length turned: its median the corner, its far end the horizon. Where it meets computing, a direction is an address, with the heat under it.
  • Two tests. Scale the world: is it a reading or a writing? Change what arrives: is it a direction or a payload, heat or paint?

essay 15 · opens on Bernoulli's bank

What only a writer holds: π, e and the completed infinity

The transcendental numbers are what a finished infinity gives. Ratios and counts make every rational step toward them and hold none: π completes the turn (the turns close, with a period), e completes the scale (the rescalings never close, and have a base). They meet at e^(iπ) = −1, where Lindemann proved π transcendental. A machine, holding only fractions, holds none either.

essay 16 · opens on mem[a]

K, not N: reading and writing for computer science

Computing's read and write already keep the address apart from what is found there, in memory laid out beforehand, with an N. A read holds K, a fineness read continuously and addresses only when counted, and never N; the Cauchy is the cull between them, a heat over directions at any N, at a cost constant in N. Why reads never race: Bernstein's conditions, given here rather than built. The corner as a register switch, order statistics as the ones that survive, the floor's remainder as address, floating point's levels, H as a zoom, what a blueprint adds, and what a machine can hold.

essay 17 · opens on a photograph's dark corners

The Cauchy map: heat given, paint met

The darkening at a photograph's corners is mostly geometry: three of its four cosines are how directions spread evenly over the half sky in front land on a flat screen. That is the Cauchy, read as a map of where a standpoint is looking: continuous, given before anything arrives, hottest wherever it looks. Where it comes from, what it is not (the turn per unit of the blueprint's grid, not a grid seen from home), the same map as a random walker's landing on a wall (harmonic measure, in every dimension; Laplace's, not the heat equation's), stepping back as the Poisson blur, the map moved by a boost, the second map in distance laid by the thumb, a middle and no mean, and addresses as a word for counted places. Where the map does not apply: seed-dispersal kernels, fat-tailed but no turn's heat, the test that could fail and did.

essay 18 · opens on pinching and twisting a map on a phone

Read and write on one plane

A two-finger gesture on a phone is one complex number, and its logarithm splits it: the pinch on the real part, the twist on the imaginary. Divide a point by the thumb and take the logarithm, ln(z/H) = ln(d/H) + iθ, and reading and writing are the two directions of one plane: turns slide up and down, scaling the world or the thumb slides across, and the scale test is which part moved. The blueprint's grid on it (circles evenly spaced, every line one curve moved, a spiral straight), the two corners as two mirrors, the thumb's lay as a turn in distance (Mercator's function), the blueprint's sphere behind the three charts with the thumb as a boost, heat as a count per chart, reads counted toward Euler's limit, what the plane is not, and where it stops: in space the split survives and turns stop being slides.

essay 19 · opens on a butterfly landing on a leaf, a walker reaching a house

Arriving: the walk, the landing and Euler's −1

One road, thumb held out. One look is a read. Two looks and one counted stride give the strides left and the size in strides, exactly, at any stride. Thumb over house falls evenly, a ladder with no corner on it; the corner is seen, the size is counted. The other pace covers a fixed share of what is left, counts nothing, and only approaches. On the blueprint's circle the road runs from 1 through i to −1: Euler's identity is the arrival, reached by a count, approached by a ratio, and its infinity is the far start of the walk.

essay 20 · opens on scaffolding round a wall

Scaffolding: what a denominator is for

Every reading is one thing in terms of another, and the denominator is half of it. Any size will do, and it is never zero: the horizon is the denominator gone. Join readings into a tower and the middle comes down, 3/4 × 2/5 = 3/10 with the door in neither side; a loop closes to 1; the last storey stays, so a tower gives the whole one number short. A tower ends on the thumb, a kept step or an agreed unit: a ratio in thumbs, a ratio in steps, or a size in a unit. The circle's equation in terms of the pair's own size is the circle, in terms of the thumb the Cauchy. A unit is scaffolding left up by agreement.
