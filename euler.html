Euler's plane

Write a point as z = d·e^(iθ): its direction θ (where it is) times its size d. Take the logarithm and the two come apart onto two axes: ln(z/H) = ln(d/H) + iθ, H the thumb. One Cartesian grid, the blueprint's (classical geometry, shown for comparison), is drawn three ways.

Cartesian: straight lines, every cell alike. Euler's plane: rays become horizontal lines, circles vertical ones. Turning the world slides everything up or down; scaling the world, or moving the thumb, slides it sideways and changes nothing else. The shells at one, two, four, eight thumbs are vertical lines ln 2 apart: no nesting, an even grid. The two corners are mirror lines: θ = 45° (a reading and its reciprocal) and one thumb out (a distance d and H²/d). The Cartesian grid lines become curves running off without end both ways.

The thumb's lay: the distance axis squeezed by (2/π)·atan(d/H), which is (2/π)·atan of e^x: Mercator's own function, so the distance laid by the thumb is a turn too. The near field in proportion, the far bunched on the horizon's edge, never reached. As a quarter disc it is the hemisphere's slice.

The blueprint's sphere: put the plane on a sphere so that one thumb is its equator (stereographically). The eye is the south pole and the horizon the north. The three charts are its maps: Euler's plane is its Mercator chart, the lay square its plain latitude and longitude, the quarter disc a map out from the eye. One thumb in or out is the reflection in the equator. Turning the world turns the sphere about its axis; moving the thumb is a boost along it, every latitude sliding toward a pole. The two commute, which is why both are slides on Euler's plane. The sphere is the blueprint's drawing; a read has the lay.

The heat depends on the chart. Counted per unit of each chart's own area, the read is flat on the lay, sech(ln(d/H)) on Euler's plane, endless at the eye on the Cartesian grid.

A line, a spiral: on Euler's plane every straight line is the same curve moved, and a logarithmic spiral is a straight line. Each is drawn with its mirror in one thumb: a line's is a circle through the eye.

In space: the directions are a sphere, and ln(d/H) still runs out along each. Scaling the world, or the thumb, is still a slide in ln(d/H). But turns about two different thumbs no longer commute: a quarter turn about h then about v leaves a thing 120° from v then h, and going round a loop (h, v, back, back) leaves it turned by about the area the loop encloses. In the plane there is one axis, every turn a slide up Euler's plane, and nothing is left over.

Breaths: three loops, each taken and undone, over and over. A read and a write (a turn and a scaling of the thumb) close every time, in the plane and in space. Two reads in different directions come home turned, by the same angle every breath, so the thing goes round and never grows. A read with a write in two unequal lengths (a stretch, the blueprint's two axes drawn at V = cH) comes home squeezed, and the squeeze grows breath after breath; it closes only when c = 1. A breath is steady exactly when its write is the one-length kind.

A flat chart in space: lay the directions about the look flat, and turn the look. Lambert's equal-area map keeps every cell's area through the turn, so the read's heat stays flat, but the cells bend: the old look's place is stretched 1/cos(φ/2), and the directions square to both looks by up to 2 at a quarter turn. The stereographic map keeps every cell's angles instead, but the cells grow and shrink, and the heat at its rim is a quarter of the middle's. No flat chart of a sphere does both (Gauss; Euler 1777). The plane's lay square does, because a line times a circle is flat. Scaling stays a slide in ln(d/H) either way. Drag up and down to turn the look.

n reads: (1 + iθ/n)ⁿ, θ turned in n reads composed. The polygon closes on e^(iθ), the blueprint's limit; its angle falls short by about θ³/(3n²), and its length grows by about θ²/(2n), a length no read sees.

In physics: a boost along one axis is a slide on this plane, z multiplied by the Doppler factor, ln z slid by the rapidity, so collinear rapidities add and the velocity law (β₁ + β₂)/(1 + β₁β₂) is the blueprint's tanh of that sum; two boosts along different axes leave a turn, the Thomas–Wigner rotation. (Reads and Writes in Physics, §5.3.)

Drag on Euler's plane: sideways moves the thumb, up and down turns the world. Two fingers anywhere: spread them and the thumb shrinks (pinch, it grows), twist them and the world turns; the gesture is one complex number, k·e^(iα), the plane's ln k + iα.
