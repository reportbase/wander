The Cauchy map

Stand a distance d from a wall and turn evenly. Equal turns of the look cross the wall crowded at its foot and thinning toward the horizon: that crowding is the heat of the directions, the Cauchy, there before any light arrives. Release random walkers from the same point and they land on the wall by the same law. The blueprint's tiles, evenly spaced, are flat. The heat is given; the paint is met.

The look and the walker

From above. The standpoint (gold) and the wall (the vertical line). Teal rays: 24 equal turns of the look, crossing the wall crowded at its foot. Violet ticks: the blueprint's tiles, evenly spaced along the wall. Gold paths: a few walkers released at the standpoint, each stopping where it first meets the wall.

Along the wall, per unit of wall (in space, per unit of area at radius r from the foot). Teal: the turn's heat, (1/π)·d/(d² + y²), or (1/2π)·d/(d² + r²)^(3/2), the cos³ of a flat screen. Gold dots: where the walkers landed. Violet: the tiles, flat. Half the turn and half the walkers land within d of the foot in the plane.

Stepping back

What a pattern on the wall keeps when seen from the height d, against its wavenumber k (one stripe every 2π/k). Teal: the Cauchy, e^(−d|k|). Gold: a Gaussian blur of the same half-width, e^(−σ²k²/2): never zero either, but falling by k². Red: the floor's box blur of the same width, sin(kw)/(kw), with zeros where a pattern is lost outright.

Heights add. The view from d₁ seen again from d₂ is the view from d₁ + d₂: the Cauchy from d (teal) convolved with itself (gold dots, computed here by summing) lies on the Cauchy from 2d (violet). The Poisson semigroup: Laplace's steady state, not the heat equation, whose kernel is the Gaussian.

The second map, in distance

Along any direction, against distance on a log axis: the weight of the distances read by ratio, (1/π)·sech(ln(d/H)), hottest at one thumb, 0.8 of the peak at one in 2 and at 2 thumbs, 0.47 at one in 4 and 4, 0.25 at one in 8 and 8. Move the thumb and the map slides along distance; its shape never changes. Multiplied by the first map, the heat is greatest at one point: on the look, one thumb out, the thing looked at.

Where no turn can be found: seeds

The test that could fail, and did (TCF). A seed leaving a plant straight, every downward direction alike, would land as the hemisphere's Cauchy: Clark's 2Dt with shape p = ½, its tail falling as r⁻³ (gold). Thin lines: the 2Dt fitted to each of the 35 wind-dispersed kernels in Bullock et al.'s compilation (2017), each drawn at its own scale (distance over √u, density over its value at the foot), dashed where the fit ended on a bound. Below, each fit's p on a log axis against the band [0.3, 0.8] predicted beforehand. The tails are fatter, about r⁻², and the shapes spread widely, as a mixture over wind speeds makes them: amounts, not a turn. A fat tail alone does not find a turn.


    


    
What needs π. The heat 1/(1 + s²) is rational in the slope, and the corner, as far along the wall as the standpoint is from it, halves it; π enters only
        when the heat is closed to a total. The Gaussian beside it is the opposite kind of law: in polar form a turn times a
        length, its π all in the turn and its e all in the length, kept by sums (the mean and the variance, exact already
        for a count of tosses) where the Cauchy is kept by order (the median, the equal halves).

    
Between the two laws. Student's t runs from the Cauchy (one other amount in the divisor) to the
    Gaussian (endlessly many). It is unmoved by scaling at every step, so it is a law of directions throughout; no step
    short of the limit has an e, and the turn's constant is there only for an odd count of amounts in the divisor.
